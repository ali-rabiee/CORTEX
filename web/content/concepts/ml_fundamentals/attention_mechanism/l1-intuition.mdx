## A soft dictionary lookup

$$
\text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
$$

Read it right to left. $QK^\top$ scores every query against every key. The
softmax turns each row into a distribution over positions. Multiplying by $V$
returns a weighted average of value vectors.

<Intuition>
An ordinary dictionary lookup is exact: you supply a key, you get its value.
Attention is the soft version — you supply a query, every key reports how well it
matches, and you receive a blend of their values weighted by the match.

The learned part is not the blending. It is deciding what the keys and values
*are*, which is what the projections do.
</Intuition>

<KeyIdea>
Every output is a **convex combination** of value vectors, so attention cannot
create content that is not already in some value. It only decides what to mix.
All the expressive power lives in $W_Q$, $W_K$, $W_V$ — which is why "attention
focuses on the important parts" is a description of the effect rather than of the
operation, and why an interviewer will push past it.
</KeyIdea>

## The cost, and where the usual framing goes wrong

"Attention is quadratic in sequence length" is true and routinely misapplied. It
is quadratic in *both* arithmetic and memory, and those two hit at very different
scales.

<Figure src="/figures/attention-memory-vs-flops.svg"
        alt="Left: attention FLOPs as a fraction of the feed-forward block, rising from 0.016 at 512 tokens to 1.0 at 32k. Right: the materialised attention matrix per layer, 0.13 GiB at 512 tokens and 32 GiB at 8192."
        caption="At width 4096, attention's arithmetic only matches the feed-forward block at about 32k tokens. Its memory footprint is already 32 GiB per layer at 8k. The binding constraint at ordinary lengths is memory traffic." />

<Steps>
<Step title="The arithmetic">
Attention costs about $2n^2 d$; the feed-forward block costs about $16 n d^2$.
They cross at $n \approx 8d$ — around 32,000 tokens at $d = 4096$. Below that,
attention is 1.6% of the FFN's FLOPs at 512 tokens and 25% at 8k.
</Step>

<Step title="The memory">
The $n\times n$ matrix, per head, per layer, held for the backward pass. At
$n = 8192$ with batch 8 and 32 heads in fp16 that is 32 GiB — for one layer.
This is what runs you out of memory, and it does so long before the FLOPs matter.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
So the productive question is not "how do I reduce the quadratic FLOPs?" but "why
am I writing this matrix to memory at all?" FlashAttention answers the second:
tile the computation, maintain a running softmax normaliser so you never need the
full row at once, and recompute tiles in the backward pass instead of storing
them. It computes **exact** attention — no approximation, no changed arithmetic —
and is much faster, because GPUs are far more starved of memory bandwidth than of
arithmetic.

That is also why the linear-attention approximations that dominated 2020-21
research have largely not displaced exact attention in practice. They attacked
the resource that was not binding.
</Warning>

## The other cost, at inference

Generating one token at a time, you do not want to recompute the keys and values
for every previous token. So you cache them — and that cache grows linearly with
context and is often the reason you run out of memory when serving.

<KeyIdea>
For a 32-layer model with 32 heads of width 128, the cache at 32k context is
about 16 GiB per sequence. Sharing key and value heads across query heads —
grouped-query attention with 8 groups — cuts it to 4 GiB, and a single shared
head cuts it to 0.5 GiB. That is why essentially every recent model uses grouped
rather than full multi-head attention at inference, and it is a memory decision
rather than a modelling one.
</KeyIdea>

## What multi-head is actually for

Splitting $d$ into $h$ heads leaves the parameter count essentially unchanged, so
it is not about capacity.

A single softmax produces exactly **one** distribution over positions per query.
So in a single-head layer, a query that needs to attend to two different things
for two different reasons has no choice but to blend them, and the blend is a
compromise serving neither. With $h$ heads there are $h$ independent
distributions in $h$ learned subspaces, so several relations coexist.

<Warning>
The cost is dimension per head. Each head sees $d/h$ dimensions, and a head too
narrow cannot represent a useful similarity at all — which is why $d_k$ is
typically kept at 64 or more rather than $h$ being pushed arbitrarily high.
</Warning>
