## The operation, and the scaling

$$
\text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V,
\qquad Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_k},\ K\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d_k},\ V\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d_v}
$$

<Derivation title="Why $1/\sqrt{d_k}$">

Let $q, k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}$ have independent entries with mean 0 and
variance 1. Then

$$
q\cdot k = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i,
\qquad
\mathbb{E}[q_ik_i] = 0,
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(q_ik_i) = \mathbb{E}[q_i^2]\mathbb{E}[k_i^2] = 1
$$

so $\mathrm{Var}(q\cdot k) = d_k$ and typical logits have magnitude
$\sqrt{d_k}$.

**What that does.** At $d_k = 64$, logits are around 8 and the differences
between them are of the same order. A softmax over logits separated by 8 is
essentially one-hot, and the Jacobian of a saturated softmax is nearly zero — so
no gradient reaches the queries or keys, and it gets worse as $d_k$ grows.

Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ restores unit-variance logits at any dimension.

**The assumption doing the work** is that queries and keys have roughly
unit-variance entries, which is what the initialisation and the layer norm before
the projections maintain. It is a condition the architecture arranges, not a law.

</Derivation>

## Attention as nonparametric regression

<Derivation title="It is Nadaraya–Watson with a learned metric">

Classical kernel regression predicts at a query point $x$ by averaging observed
values weighted by a kernel:

$$
\hat f(x) = \frac{\sum_j K(x, x_j)\, y_j}{\sum_j K(x, x_j)}
$$

Attention's row $i$ is

$$
o_i = \sum_j \alpha_{ij} v_j,
\qquad
\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp\big(q_i^\top k_j/\sqrt{d_k}\big)}{\sum_{j'}\exp\big(q_i^\top k_{j'}/\sqrt{d_k}\big)}
$$

which is the same expression with $K(q_i, k_j) = \exp(q_i^\top k_j/\sqrt{d_k})$.

**What is new is that the metric is learned.** $W_Q$ and $W_K$ define the space
in which similarity is measured, so the model chooses what "nearby" means rather
than inheriting a fixed kernel.

**Two consequences worth carrying.** Attention generalises by *interpolation*
among its values, so it has no mechanism to produce an output outside their
convex hull. And the softmax temperature — here fixed at $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ —
controls the bandwidth: sharper attention is a narrower kernel, attending to
fewer positions more strongly.

</Derivation>

## Cost, in both resources

<Derivation title="FLOPs, memory, and where they cross">

**Arithmetic.** $QK^\top$ is $n \times m \times d_k$ multiply-accumulates and
$AV$ is $n \times m \times d_v$, so for self-attention with $m = n$ and
$d_k = d_v = d/h$ summed over $h$ heads:

$$
C_{\text{attn}} \approx 2\,n^2 d \ \text{MACs}
$$

The feed-forward block with the standard $4\times$ expansion is two matrices of
size $d \times 4d$, forward and backward:

$$
C_{\text{ffn}} \approx 16\, n\, d^2
$$

Setting them equal:

$$
2n^2 d = 16 n d^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\,n = 8d\,}
$$

At $d = 4096$ that is $n \approx 32{,}768$. Below it, attention is a small
fraction of the arithmetic: 1.6% at $n = 512$, 6% at 2k, 25% at 8k.

**Memory.** The attention matrix is $b \cdot h \cdot n^2$ entries and must be
retained for the backward pass. At $b = 8$, $h = 32$, $n = 8192$, fp16:

$$
8 \times 32 \times 8192^2 \times 2\ \text{bytes} = 32\ \text{GiB per layer}
$$

**The conclusion.** Memory binds first, by a wide margin. So the productive
optimisation is not reducing $n^2$ arithmetic — it is not writing the matrix
down.

</Derivation>

## The online softmax

<Derivation title="Computing a softmax in one streaming pass">

The stable softmax needs the global maximum:

$$
\operatorname{softmax}(x)_i = \frac{e^{x_i - m}}{\sum_j e^{x_j - m}},
\qquad m = \max_j x_j
$$

A tiled computation does not have $m$ yet. Carry a running maximum $m$ and a
running denominator $\ell$, and correct them as tiles arrive. Processing a new
tile with local maximum $\tilde m$ and local sum $\tilde\ell$:

$$
m^{\text{new}} = \max(m, \tilde m),
\qquad
\ell^{\text{new}} = e^{\,m - m^{\text{new}}}\,\ell \;+\; e^{\,\tilde m - m^{\text{new}}}\,\tilde\ell
$$

and the accumulated output is rescaled by the same factor
$e^{\,m - m^{\text{new}}}$.

**This is exact.** No approximation is introduced — the rescaling exactly
compensates for having normalised by a stale maximum. That is why FlashAttention
can claim exact attention while never materialising the $n\times n$ matrix, and
why it recomputes tiles in the backward pass rather than storing them: recomputing
costs arithmetic, which is the resource that was not binding.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
The general lesson is worth more than the kernel. When an operation is
memory-bound, trading arithmetic for memory traffic is a win even if it increases
total FLOPs — and the way to find such trades is to ask which intermediate
results are being written down only because the naive implementation happened to
compute them in that order.
</KeyIdea>

## The inference-time cost: the KV cache

Autoregressive generation reuses the keys and values of all previous tokens, so
they are cached. The cache size is

$$
2 \cdot L \cdot h_{kv} \cdot d_{\text{head}} \cdot n \cdot b \cdot \text{bytes}
$$

linear in context, and often the binding memory constraint when serving.

<Steps>
<Step title="Multi-head: $h_{kv} = h$">
32 layers, 32 heads, $d_{\text{head}} = 128$, 32k context, fp16: about 16 GiB
per sequence.
</Step>

<Step title="Grouped-query: $h_{kv} = g < h$">
Share each key-value pair across several query heads. With $g = 8$, the same
configuration is 4 GiB — a 4x reduction for a small quality cost.
</Step>

<Step title="Multi-query: $h_{kv} = 1$">
One shared key-value head. 0.5 GiB, and a larger quality cost, which is why
grouped-query is the usual compromise.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Note that this changes the *inference* memory profile and barely changes training
FLOPs. It is a serving decision that leaks into the architecture, which is why
models are now designed with $h_{kv} < h$ from the start rather than converted
afterwards — and it is a good example of a deployment constraint determining a
modelling choice.
</Warning>

## Multi-head, formally

$$
\text{MHA}(X) = \big[\text{head}_1;\ldots;\text{head}_h\big]W^O,
\qquad
\text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(XW_i^Q, XW_i^K, XW_i^V)
$$

with $d_k = d/h$, so the parameter count is essentially unchanged. The gain is
not capacity: a single softmax yields exactly one distribution over positions per
query, so one head must blend all the relations it needs into a compromise, while
$h$ heads let several coexist in separate learned subspaces.

$$
\text{one head} \ \Rightarrow\ \text{one relation per layer}
\qquad
h \ \text{heads} \ \Rightarrow\ h \ \text{relations}
$$

This is structurally the same argument that appears wherever a single softmax has
to represent multiple modes, and the same trade applies — heads too narrow to
represent a useful similarity are worse than fewer, wider ones.
