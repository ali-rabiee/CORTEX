## The operation, and the two costs it created

<PaperCard refKey="vaswani2017transformer" />

Removing recurrence bought constant path length between any two positions and
full parallelism across the sequence. It also created two costs that the next six
years of research went after separately: quadratic arithmetic, and quadratic
memory.

<KeyIdea>
Almost the entire "efficient attention" literature is a bet on which of those two
was the real constraint. The bet that lost went after arithmetic; the bet that
won went after memory. That is worth knowing as a case study in choosing an
optimisation target, quite apart from what it says about attention.
</KeyIdea>

## The sub-quadratic wave

<PaperCard refKey="choromanski2021performer" />

Between 2020 and 2021 a large family of methods reduced attention's asymptotic
cost: low-rank projections of the key-value sequence, sparse patterns, and
kernel-feature approximations. Performers are the most principled — approximate
the softmax kernel with random features so the operation factorises and becomes
linear in sequence length.

The mathematics is sound and the methods work. They largely did not displace
exact attention, and the reason is arithmetic in the other sense.

<Warning>
At width 4096, attention matches the feed-forward block's FLOPs only around
32,000 tokens. At 2k tokens attention is about 6% of the arithmetic — so halving
it changes total training time by 3%. Meanwhile the materialised attention matrix
is tens of gigabytes and is what actually limits batch size.

So these methods delivered an asymptotic improvement in a term that was not
dominant, at the cost of an approximation with a quality question attached. It is
a clean example of the general failure: **optimise the resource that binds, and
measure which one that is before choosing.**
</Warning>

## The bet that won

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="dao2022flashattention" />
<PaperCard refKey="dao2023flashattention2" />
</PaperGrid>

FlashAttention's framing is in its subtitle: IO-awareness. Attention on a GPU
spends most of its time moving the $n \times n$ matrix between high-bandwidth
memory and on-chip SRAM, not computing. So tile the computation, use an online
softmax so no tile needs the global normaliser, and recompute tiles in the
backward pass rather than storing them.

<Steps>
<Step title="It is exact">
No approximation, no quality question, and therefore no argument to have about
whether it is safe to use. That is a substantial adoption advantage over any
approximate method, independent of speed.
</Step>

<Step title="It uses more FLOPs, not fewer">
Recomputation in the backward pass is extra arithmetic. It is faster anyway,
because arithmetic was the abundant resource. This inversion is the part worth
carrying: when an operation is memory-bound, spending FLOPs to avoid a write is a
good trade.
</Step>

<Step title="FlashAttention-2 confirms the diagnosis">
The follow-up gains come from better parallelisation and work partitioning —
scheduling, not mathematics. That the remaining headroom was in *how the work was
distributed* is evidence the original diagnosis was right.
</Step>
</Steps>

## The orthogonal problem: serving

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="shazeer2019mqa" />
<PaperCard refKey="ainslie2023gqa" />
</PaperGrid>

FlashAttention addresses a transient intermediate inside one forward pass. At
inference there is a different memory problem: the KV cache is *persistent* state
that grows with every generated token and every concurrent sequence.

Multi-query attention shares a single key-value head across all query heads,
shrinking the cache by the head count — 16 GiB down to 0.5 GiB for a 32-layer,
32-head model at 32k context — at a quality cost. Grouped-query attention
interpolates: group query heads over a smaller number of shared key-value heads,
recovering most of the quality at most of the saving, and it can be produced from
an existing multi-head checkpoint rather than requiring a retrain.

<KeyIdea>
These are orthogonal to FlashAttention and used together. The pattern worth
noticing is that a *serving* constraint has propagated back into the
architecture: models are now designed with fewer key-value heads than query heads
from the start, because the deployment memory profile is a design input rather
than a downstream concern.
</KeyIdea>

## What this episode is actually about

<Steps>
<Step title="Asymptotics do not identify the bottleneck">
$O(n^2)$ against $O(n)$ says which wins eventually. It says nothing about which
dominates at $n = 2048$, and that is the question that determines where to spend
effort.
</Step>

<Step title="Exactness is worth a lot">
An exact method needs no quality argument, so it can be adopted by default. An
approximate one has to be defended per application, which is a real adoption cost
that rarely appears in a speedup table.
</Step>

<Step title="Measure the resource, then optimise it">
The productive question about any expensive operation is which resource it is
starved of — arithmetic, memory bandwidth, memory capacity, or latency — because
the answer determines which improvements are even relevant.
</Step>
</Steps>
