## The problem is curvature, not noise

The intuitive story about gradient descent is that it is slow because gradients
are noisy. The more fundamental problem is deterministic and survives with exact
gradients: **conditioning**.

<Figure src="/figures/sgd-conditioning-momentum.svg"
        alt="Left: an elongated contour plot with gradient descent zigzagging across the narrow axis while creeping along the shallow one, and a smooth momentum path underneath. Right: a table of iterations to fixed error, showing GD scaling with kappa and momentum with root kappa."
        caption="The largest stable step is set by the steepest direction, so progress along the shallow direction is throttled by curvature you were not trying to descend. Momentum lets the oscillating components cancel." />

<Intuition>
Walking down a long narrow valley. The floor slopes gently toward the exit and
the walls are steep. Step size is limited by the walls — anything larger and you
are bouncing off them — so you shuffle toward the exit at a pace set by
geometry that has nothing to do with where you are going.

Momentum is carrying speed: the bounces off opposite walls cancel, and the
component along the valley floor accumulates.
</Intuition>

<KeyIdea>
The condition number $\kappa$ — the ratio of largest to smallest curvature — sets
the iteration count. Plain gradient descent needs $O(\kappa)$ iterations;
momentum needs $O(\sqrt\kappa)$. At $\kappa = 10^4$ that is a hundredfold
difference, which is why momentum is not an optimisation but a requirement.
</KeyIdea>

## What momentum is, mechanically

$$
v_t = \beta v_{t-1} + g_t,
\qquad
\theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \eta\,v_t
$$

$v$ is a running sum of past gradients with geometric decay — so it averages
roughly $1/(1-\beta)$ of them.

<Warning>
That averaging window is also an **effective step multiplier**. For a consistent
gradient, $v$ converges to $g/(1-\beta)$, so the step is $1/(1-\beta)$ times
larger than plain SGD's: 10x at $\beta = 0.9$, 100x at $\beta = 0.99$.

Raising $\beta$ without lowering $\eta$ therefore multiplies your step size, and
the usual result is divergence attributed to the wrong cause. If you increase
momentum, decrease the learning rate by roughly the same factor.
</Warning>

## Batch size and learning rate move together

A batch of size $B$ averages $B$ gradient samples, so the noise in the estimate
falls like $1/\sqrt{B}$ and a larger step becomes safe.

<Steps>
<Step title="The linear scaling rule">
Multiply the batch size by $k$, multiply the learning rate by $k$. The reasoning
is about progress per epoch: $k$ times fewer steps, each $k$ times larger. It
holds over a surprisingly wide range.
</Step>

<Step title="Why it needs a warmup">
The rule is about the steady state, and the first steps are where a large rate
does its damage — the weights are random and the gradients are unrepresentative.
Ramping the learning rate over the first few epochs is what makes the rule work
in practice rather than in principle.
</Step>

<Step title="Where it stops">
Past the **gradient noise scale**, the gradient is already close to
deterministic, so a larger batch no longer permits a proportionally larger step.
Doubling the batch then halves your step count without any compensating gain, and
wall-clock time stops improving. That scale is measurable rather than a matter of
taste.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Schedules

Almost nobody trains at a constant learning rate, for a reason that follows from
the noise argument: a step size appropriate for making progress early is too
large to settle later, when the remaining improvement is smaller than the noise.

<Steps>
<Step title="Warmup">
Ramp up over the first few hundred to few thousand steps. Necessary for large
batches, for post-norm transformers, and whenever the early gradients are
unrepresentative — which is most of the time.
</Step>

<Step title="Cosine decay">
Smoothly to near zero over the run. The dominant choice, and its main practical
property is that it has no cliff — nothing has to be tuned about where the drops
happen.
</Step>

<Step title="Step decay">
Divide by 10 at fixed milestones. Older, still competitive, and it makes the
schedule's effect visible in the loss curve — each drop produces a step, which is
diagnostically useful when you want to know whether the rate was the limiting
factor.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
A schedule is doing two separable jobs: keeping the early steps small enough to
survive an unrepresentative gradient, and keeping the late steps small enough to
settle rather than bounce. Knowing which job you need explains why the same
schedule shape appears everywhere and why the transitions sit where they do.
</KeyIdea>

## Where the family fits

<Warning>
Adam and its relatives differ from everything above by dividing out the *scale*
of each coordinate's gradient, which makes the learning rate a distance in
parameter space rather than a multiplier on an unknown gradient magnitude. That
is a genuinely different property, and it is why Adam's hyperparameters transfer
across architectures where SGD's must be retuned.

The honest counterweight is that well-tuned SGD with momentum still generalises
better on some vision problems. The effect is real, it is problem-dependent, and
"well-tuned" is doing a lot of work — so the default is adaptive, and the
comparison is worth running if you are chasing the last point.
</Warning>
