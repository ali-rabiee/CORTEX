## Scaling up, and what it took

<PaperCard refKey="goyal2017largebatch" />

The linear scaling rule — multiply the batch by $k$, multiply the learning rate
by $k$ — plus a gradual warmup, taken to batch size 8192 on ImageNet without
accuracy loss.

<KeyIdea>
What makes this a useful paper rather than a rule of thumb is the accompanying
detail. Excluding weight decay from biases and normalisation parameters, handling
batch-norm statistics correctly under data parallelism, and scaling the warmup
with the target rate all turn out to matter as much as the rule itself. The rule
is the headline; the reason it works in practice is the list.
</KeyIdea>

## The sharp-minima story, and what happened to it

<PaperCard refKey="keskar2017largebatch" />

Large-batch training generalises worse, and the proposed explanation is that it
converges to *sharper* minima — regions where the loss rises quickly under small
parameter perturbations — which are hypothesised to generalise worse than flat
ones.

<Warning>
The observation is robust and the causal explanation is contested, which is worth
separating carefully because the framing became very widely repeated.

The sharpest objection is reparameterisation: for a network with rescaling
symmetries — multiply one layer's weights by $c$ and the next layer's by $1/c$ —
you can change the measured sharpness of a minimum without changing the function
it computes at all. A quantity that can be altered arbitrarily without changing
the model cannot by itself determine generalisation.

And much of the gap closes with correct learning-rate scaling and warmup, which
suggests a substantial part of the original effect was a tuning artefact.
</Warning>

## The measurable quantity

<PaperCard refKey="mccandlish2018noisescale" />

The contribution here is a *measurement* rather than a recommendation. The
gradient noise scale

$$
B_{\text{noise}} = \frac{\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)}{\|g\|^2}
$$

compares the total gradient variance against the squared gradient magnitude, and
predicts the batch size beyond which additional parallelism stops reducing
wall-clock time.

<Steps>
<Step title="Why this beats a recommended batch size">
A recommendation is a fact about someone else's problem. The noise scale is
estimable from your own gradient statistics during a run, before you commit
hardware.
</Step>

<Step title="It changes during training">
Typically growing as the model improves and the gradient shrinks — so a batch
size well chosen at the start can be below the useful scale later. That is the
justification for batch-size schedules, and it is invisible from a fixed
recommendation.
</Step>

<Step title="It varies enormously across problems">
Which is why the folklore about "the right batch size" was contradictory before
there was a quantity to measure.
</Step>
</Steps>

## The adaptive-versus-SGD question

<PaperCard refKey="wilson2017marginal" />

Adaptive methods can converge to solutions that generalise worse than well-tuned
SGD, demonstrated across several problems and including a constructed case where
adaptive methods provably find a worse solution.

<Warning>
The honest statement has four qualifications, and dropping any of them makes the
claim wrong in one direction or the other.

The effect is **real** — it is not an artefact, and it was shown constructively as
well as empirically. It is **problem-dependent**, appearing most clearly in
vision. It is **sensitive to tuning effort**, and "well-tuned SGD" conceals a
learning-rate search that Adam does not require, since Adam's rate transfers and
SGD's does not. And it **does not clearly transfer** to transformers on language,
where adaptive methods are essentially universal at scale without an evident
penalty.

So: default to adaptive for robustness, and run the comparison if you are chasing
the last point on a benchmark where the gap has been observed.
</Warning>

## Schedules

<PaperCard refKey="loshchilov2017sgdr" />

The origin of the cosine schedule, introduced with periodic *restarts* — jumping
the learning rate back up to escape a basin and explore another. The restarts
were the point; the cosine shape is what survived, and it is now the default in
settings that use no restarts at all.

<KeyIdea>
That is a common pattern worth recognising: a component of a proposed method
outliving the method's central idea. The reason cosine spread is mundane — it has
nothing to tune about where the transitions occur, unlike step decay, and it
decays smoothly to a floor. Its one real cost is needing the total step count in
advance, which makes extending a run awkward.
</KeyIdea>

## What to take from this literature

<Steps>
<Step title="Established">
Linear scaling with warmup works over a wide range. The gradient noise scale
predicts where parallelism stops paying. Weight decay should exclude biases and
normalisation parameters.
</Step>

<Step title="Contested">
Whether large batches inherently generalise worse, and whether sharpness is the
mechanism. The observation is more robust than the explanation.
</Step>

<Step title="Problem-dependent">
Whether adaptive methods cost you generalisation. Real in vision, unclear
elsewhere, and confounded by unequal tuning effort.
</Step>
</Steps>
