## Gradient descent on a quadratic

Take $f(\theta) = \tfrac12\theta^\top H\theta$ with $H$ symmetric positive
definite, eigenvalues $\mu = \lambda_{\min} \le \cdots \le \lambda_{\max} = L$,
condition number $\kappa = L/\mu$.

<Derivation title="Why the iteration count scales with $\kappa$">

Gradient descent gives $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta H\theta_t = (I - \eta H)\theta_t$.
In the eigenbasis this decouples completely:

$$
\theta^{(i)}_{t} = (1 - \eta\lambda_i)^t\,\theta^{(i)}_0
$$

**Stability.** Every direction must contract: $|1-\eta\lambda_i| < 1$ for all
$i$, and the binding constraint is the largest curvature:

$$
\eta < \frac{2}{L}
$$

**Rate.** With $\eta$ capped there, the *slowest* direction contracts by

$$
|1 - \eta\mu| \;\approx\; 1 - \frac{2\mu}{L} = 1 - \frac{2}{\kappa}
$$

so reducing its error by a constant factor takes $O(\kappa)$ iterations.

**Read what happened.** The largest curvature sets the ceiling on the step and
the smallest sets the progress, so the *ratio* is what costs you. And none of
this involved noise — conditioning is a more basic difficulty than stochasticity,
and it is why second-order and adaptive methods exist at all.

</Derivation>

## Momentum, and the $\sqrt\kappa$ acceleration

$$
v_t = \beta v_{t-1} + g_t,
\qquad
\theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \eta v_t
$$

<Derivation title="Two facts about momentum">

**Fact 1: the effective step multiplier.** For a constant gradient $g$, unroll:

$$
v_t = \sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\beta^k g \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{g}{1-\beta}
$$

so the parameter moves $\eta g/(1-\beta)$ per step — ten times plain SGD at
$\beta = 0.9$, a hundred times at $\beta = 0.99$.

**Consequence.** Raising $\beta$ is a step-size change wearing a smoothing
change's clothes. Increase it and you must decrease $\eta$ proportionally, or you
will diverge and blame the wrong parameter.

**Fact 2: the acceleration.** In the eigenbasis, each direction obeys a
second-order linear recursion:

$$
\theta^{(i)}_{t+1} = (1 + \beta - \eta\lambda_i)\,\theta^{(i)}_t - \beta\,\theta^{(i)}_{t-1}
$$

whose convergence rate is the modulus of the larger root of
$z^2 - (1+\beta-\eta\lambda_i)z + \beta = 0$. When the discriminant is negative
the roots are complex with modulus exactly $\sqrt\beta$ — *independent of
$\lambda_i$*.

Choosing $\eta$ and $\beta$ so that this holds across the whole spectrum gives

$$
\sqrt\beta = \frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\text{iterations} = O(\sqrt\kappa)
$$

**The table, computed:**

| $\kappa$ | GD $\sim\kappa$ | momentum $\sim\sqrt\kappa$ | speedup |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $10$ | 10 | 3.2 | 3.2x |
| $10^2$ | 100 | 10 | 10x |
| $10^3$ | 1,000 | 32 | 32x |
| $10^4$ | 10,000 | 100 | 100x |

**The assumption doing the work** is that the problem is quadratic with a known
spectrum. Real losses are neither, so the optimal $\beta$ is not computable and
0.9 is a default that works — but the *shape* of the result, that momentum buys
a square root, is what justifies using it at all.

</Derivation>

## Noise, and the linear scaling rule

With minibatch gradients, $\hat g = g + \xi$ where $\xi$ has covariance
$\Sigma/B$ for batch size $B$. Treating SGD as a discretisation of a stochastic
differential equation, the update

$$
\Delta\theta = -\eta\,\hat g = -\eta g - \eta\,\xi
$$

has a deterministic drift $\eta g$ and a noise term with covariance
$\eta^2\Sigma/B$.

<Derivation title="Linear scaling, and where it stops">

**The rule.** Holding the *ratio* of noise to drift fixed across a change in
batch size requires $\eta^2/B$ to be constant... but holding the noise per
*epoch* fixed — the quantity that actually governs the trajectory the
optimisation follows — gives $\eta \propto B$. That is the linear scaling rule:
$k$ times fewer steps, each $k$ times larger, same total progress and same
effective noise per epoch.

**Why it needs warmup.** The argument is about the steady state, and the first
steps are the ones taken from a random initialisation with an unrepresentative
gradient. A large rate there does damage the analysis does not model, so ramping
over the first epochs is what makes the rule work in practice.

**Where it stops: the gradient noise scale.**

$$
B_{\text{noise}} \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)}{\|g\|^2}
$$

This compares the total gradient variance against the squared gradient magnitude.

- $B \ll B_{\text{noise}}$: the batch gradient is noise-dominated, the step is
  noise-limited, and halving the noise permits doubling the step. Linear scaling
  holds.
- $B \gg B_{\text{noise}}$: the batch gradient is already close to the full
  gradient. The step is now limited by *curvature*, which batching does not
  change, so a larger batch reduces the step count without permitting larger
  steps and wall-clock stops improving.

**Why this is useful.** $B_{\text{noise}}$ is estimable from gradient statistics
during training, so "how large a batch is worth using?" becomes a measurement
rather than a sweep — and it explains why the answer differs by orders of
magnitude between problems.

</Derivation>

## Nesterov's variant

$$
v_t = \beta v_{t-1} + \nabla f\big(\theta_{t-1} - \eta\beta v_{t-1}\big),
\qquad
\theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \eta v_t
$$

The gradient is evaluated at the point momentum is *about to take you*, not where
you are.

<KeyIdea>
The intuition is a correction term: if momentum is going to overshoot, evaluating
ahead sees the gradient already reversing and damps it before the overshoot
happens, rather than after. In convex optimisation this yields a genuinely better
constant; in deep learning the difference from plain momentum is usually small,
which is why both are in use and neither is a strong default.
</KeyIdea>

## Schedules, in terms of the same quantities

<Steps>
<Step title="Warmup addresses the unrepresentative early gradient">
At initialisation the gradient reflects random weights, and the noise scale is
different from its steady-state value. A small early rate avoids taking a large
step on bad information.
</Step>

<Step title="Decay addresses the noise floor">
Late in training the remaining improvement per step is small relative to the
gradient noise, so a fixed step size makes the iterate diffuse in a ball around
the optimum with radius proportional to $\eta$. Decaying $\eta$ shrinks that
ball, which is the entire reason the loss drops at each step-decay milestone.
</Step>

<Step title="Cosine is a smooth version of the same thing">
Its practical advantage is having nothing to tune about where the transitions
occur, and its practical disadvantage is that the total step count must be known
in advance — which is why runs that get extended need care.
</Step>
</Steps>
