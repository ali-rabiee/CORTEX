## Failure modes

Applying dropout in the wrong location or with wrong scaling causes silent performance degradation. Common mistakes: (1) forgetting to switch to eval mode at inference (model.eval()), causing continued dropout and degraded performance; (2) using dropout after BatchNorm, which introduces a variance shift between train and eval; (3) using high dropout ($p > 0.5$) in small networks, causing underfitting. Symptoms: inexplicable train-eval gap, non-deterministic inference outputs. Fix: always verify eval mode, place dropout before normalization layers, and prefer lower $p$ (0.1-0.3) for modern architectures.

## Interview-ready answer

Dropout randomly zeros activations with probability $p$ during training, providing regularization through implicit ensemble averaging. The inverted dropout formulation (scaling by $1/(1-p)$ during training) means no modification is needed at inference. Key practical points: dropout is less common in modern architectures (ResNets, Transformers use other regularization), but MC-Dropout is valuable for uncertainty estimation in safety-critical robotics applications. In transformers, attention dropout ($p = 0.1$) on the attention weights and residual dropout on sub-layer outputs are standard. The interaction between dropout and BatchNorm is subtle — dropout changes activation variance, which interacts with BatchNorm statistics. Generally prefer placing dropout after normalization or using architectures that avoid the interaction.
