## The original, and what did not change

<PaperCard refKey="vaswani2017transformer" />

Multi-head attention, a position-wise feed-forward block, residual connections
around both, and layer normalisation. Eight years later that outline is
unmodified, which is unusual enough to be worth noting explicitly — most
architectures of that vintage have been replaced.

<PaperCard refKey="ba2016layernorm" />

Layer normalisation predates it and was the right import: normalising across
features within one example rather than across the batch makes it batch-size
independent and compatible with variable-length sequences, neither of which batch
normalisation offers. That property is why it was available to be used.

<KeyIdea>
Only three things about the block have actually changed since 2017. Reading them
as *fixes to identified failures* rather than as improvements is what makes the
list memorable — and it is also how you should evaluate the next proposed
modification.
</KeyIdea>

## Fix one: the norm placement

<PaperCard refKey="xiong2020prenorm" />

Everyone knew transformers needed a learning-rate warmup and nobody could say
why. This paper shows that in post-norm, gradients at initialisation scale with
depth — because the normalisation sits on the residual path and every layer's
gradient passes through all of them — and that moving the normalisation inside
each branch removes the dependence entirely.

<Warning>
The general shape is worth recognising. A known-necessary hyperparameter with no
explanation is a place where you cannot reason, only tune. Identifying the
mechanism turned warmup from a mandatory schedule into an unnecessary one, and
predicted where the problem would recur.

It also created the most common inherited bug in the area: copying a pre-2020
implementation gets you post-norm, the model refuses to train at depth, and the
natural response is to add a warmup and spend a day on it — solving a problem
that one boolean removes.
</Warning>

## Fix two: positions that extrapolate

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="su2021rope" />
<PaperCard refKey="press2022alibi" />
</PaperGrid>

Learned absolute position embeddings work within the trained length and have no
basis for behaving sensibly beyond it — index 5000 was never seen, so its
embedding is whatever initialisation left there.

RoPE rotates queries and keys by an angle proportional to absolute position.
Because rotations compose, $R_m^\top R_n = R_{n-m}$, so the attention inner
product depends only on the offset — relative position emerges from an absolute
operation, with no parameters and nothing added to the residual stream.

ALiBi does it at the other end: no position embeddings at all, just a
distance-proportional penalty added to the attention logits with a per-head
slope.

<Steps>
<Step title="RoPE modifies the vectors">
It changes the geometry of the space where similarity is measured, so positional
and content information interact. More expressive — the model can represent
non-monotonic positional relationships if the data supports them.
</Step>

<Step title="ALiBi modifies the scores">
An explicit locality prior, independent of content: attention decays with
distance, monotonically, by construction. Stronger prior, usually correct, and it
extrapolates by design rather than by good behaviour.
</Step>

<Step title="Both are still limited">
RoPE's lowest-frequency components have periods longer than the training context,
so a full cycle was never observed and behaviour beyond it is untested. That is
why long-context adaptation rescales $\theta$ rather than trusting extrapolation.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Fix three: the feed-forward block

<PaperCard refKey="shazeer2020glu" />

Replace $W_2\,\sigma(W_1x)$ with a gated pair, where one branch multiplicatively
modulates the other. Three matrices instead of two, so the hidden width is
reduced to keep the parameter count matched.

<KeyIdea>
The gating provides a data-dependent multiplicative interaction that a plain MLP
cannot express at the same width — an input can suppress or amplify a channel
rather than only contributing additively. Note the careful part of the
comparison: matched parameters. A gated variant at unchanged hidden width has 1.5x
the parameters, and an unmatched comparison measures capacity rather than gating.
</KeyIdea>

## What that leaves

<Warning>
Three changes in eight years. Multi-head attention, the residual structure, the
position-wise feed-forward block, the $4\times$ expansion, and the overall block
outline all survive unmodified.

That stability is informative in two directions. It suggests the original design
was unusually well chosen. And it sets a bar for anything proposed next: the
modifications that stuck each fixed a *diagnosed* failure — warmup fragility, no
extrapolation past the trained length, no multiplicative interaction at matched
width — rather than being ideas that seemed promising. When you meet the next
proposed change to the block, the useful question is which identified failure it
fixes, and whether the comparison holds parameters and compute fixed.
</Warning>

<Steps>
<Step title="What is genuinely contested now">
Whether attention is necessary at all, or whether state-space and linear-recurrent
models match it at long context; whether the $4\times$ expansion ratio is right;
and whether sparse mixture-of-experts feed-forward blocks should be the default.
None is settled.
</Step>

<Step title="What is not contested">
The residual structure, pre-norm placement, relative positions, and the basic
two-sublayer block. These have survived enough scrutiny that a proposal to change
them carries the burden of proof.
</Step>
</Steps>
