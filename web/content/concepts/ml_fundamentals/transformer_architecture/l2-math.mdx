## The block

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{pre-norm:}\quad
&x \leftarrow x + \text{Attn}\big(\text{norm}(x)\big) \\
&x \leftarrow x + \text{FFN}\big(\text{norm}(x)\big)
\end{aligned}
\qquad
\begin{aligned}
\text{post-norm:}\quad
&x \leftarrow \text{norm}\big(x + \text{Attn}(x)\big) \\
&x \leftarrow \text{norm}\big(x + \text{FFN}(x)\big)
\end{aligned}
$$

The difference looks cosmetic and determines whether the model trains at depth.

<Derivation title="Why pre-norm trains without a warmup">

**Pre-norm.** Write the update as $x_{k+1} = x_k + F_k(x_k)$ where $F_k$ includes
the normalisation. Then

$$
\frac{\partial x_{k+1}}{\partial x_k} = I + J_k,
\qquad J_k = \frac{\partial F_k}{\partial x_k}
$$

and across $L$ layers

$$
\frac{\partial x_L}{\partial x_k} = \prod_{j=k}^{L-1}\big(I + J_j\big)
= I + \sum_j J_j + \sum_{j<j'} J_j J_{j'} + \cdots
$$

**The leading $I$ is the argument.** Whatever the Jacobians do, there is a path
from the loss to every layer that passes through no multiplicative factor.
Compare a plain stack, where the product is $\prod_j J_j$ with no identity term
and the magnitude is exponential in depth in whichever direction $\|J\|$ points.

**Post-norm.** Now the normalisation sits *on* the residual path:
$x_{k+1} = \text{norm}(x_k + F_k(x_k))$, so

$$
\frac{\partial x_{k+1}}{\partial x_k} = J_{\text{norm}}\big(I + J_k\big)
$$

and the identity term is multiplied by the normalisation's Jacobian at every
layer. The gradient reaching layer $k$ now carries a factor depending on $L - k$,
so its scale at initialisation depends on depth — large near the output and
attenuated near the input.

**What warmup is for.** The imbalance is worst at initialisation, before the
weights have adapted. A small learning rate for the first few thousand steps lets
the network reach a configuration where the scales are more even, after which a
normal rate is safe. So warmup is a patch for a structural problem that pre-norm
does not have.

**The cost of pre-norm.** Since the stream is never normalised, each block adds a
roughly independent contribution and the stream's variance grows like $L$ — so
its standard deviation grows like $\sqrt{L}$: about 5x at 24 layers. A final
normalisation before the output head is therefore required, not optional.

</Derivation>

## Positional information

Attention over a set is permutation-equivariant, so position must be injected.

<Derivation title="RoPE: absolute rotation, relative effect">

Split the head dimension into 2-D pairs and rotate the pair at index $i$ by an
angle proportional to the position $m$:

$$
R_m^{(i)} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos m\theta_i & -\sin m\theta_i\\ \sin m\theta_i & \cos m\theta_i\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\theta_i = 10000^{-2i/d}
$$

Apply $R_m$ to the query at position $m$ and $R_n$ to the key at position $n$.
The attention logit is

$$
\big\langle R_m q,\; R_n k\big\rangle = q^\top R_m^\top R_n k = q^\top R_{n-m}\,k
$$

using $R_m^\top R_n = R_{n-m}$, which holds because rotations are orthogonal and
compose additively in angle.

**Read what happened.** Each vector was rotated by its *absolute* position, and
the inner product — the only thing attention consumes — depends solely on the
*offset* $n - m$. Relative position emerges from an absolute operation.

**Why this beats a learned absolute embedding.** No parameters, nothing added to
the residual stream, and position enters only where it is used. And a learned
absolute embedding has simply never seen index 5000, so it has no basis for
behaving sensibly there — whereas the rotation is defined for any $m$.

**The limit.** Extrapolation is better, not free. The lowest-frequency components
have periods longer than the training context, so the model has never seen a
full cycle of them and their behaviour beyond the trained length is untested. That
is why long-context adaptation typically involves rescaling $\theta$ rather than
relying on extrapolation.

</Derivation>

The alternative, ALiBi, skips embeddings entirely and adds a distance-proportional
penalty to the attention logits, $-\lambda_h |m - n|$ with a per-head slope — a
learned locality prior that also extrapolates, by construction rather than by
luck.

## The accounting

<Derivation title="$N \approx 12Ld^2$ and $C \approx 6ND$">

**Parameters per layer.** Attention has $W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O$, each $d\times d$:
$4d^2$. The feed-forward block with $4\times$ expansion has $d \times 4d$ and
$4d \times d$: $8d^2$. Total $12d^2$, so

$$
N \approx 12\,L\,d^2
$$

At $L = 32$, $d = 4096$: $6.4\times10^9$, plus $Vd \approx 0.13\times10^9$ per
embedding matrix at $V = 32{,}000$.

**Compute per token.** A matmul against a weight matrix with $n$ entries costs
$2n$ FLOPs — one multiply and one add per entry — so the forward pass is $2N$.
The backward pass needs two gradients per layer: with respect to the input, to
keep propagating, and with respect to the weights, to update them. Each costs
about a forward matmul, so backward is $\approx 4N$.

$$
C \approx (2 + 4)\,N\,D = 6\,N\,D
$$

**What it ignores.** The $n^2$ attention term, which is why the estimate is good
below the $n \approx 8d$ crossover and increasingly optimistic above it. Also
activation functions and normalisations, which are negligible in FLOPs and
sometimes not in wall-clock, since they are memory-bound.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
These two formulas do most of the practical work. $12Ld^2$ tells you a model's
size from its shape; $6ND$ turns a training plan into a GPU-hour estimate before
you write any code. Being able to produce them in an interview reads as having
planned a training run rather than launched one someone else configured.
</KeyIdea>

## The feed-forward block, and why it changed

The classic block is $W_2\,\sigma(W_1 x)$ with a $4\times$ expansion. Most recent
models use a gated variant instead:

$$
\text{SwiGLU}(x) = \big(\text{Swish}(W_1 x) \odot W_3 x\big)W_2
$$

Three matrices rather than two, so the hidden width is reduced (typically to
$\tfrac{8}{3}d$) to keep the parameter count matched. The gating means one branch
*modulates* the other multiplicatively rather than both being summed — a
data-dependent scaling that a plain MLP cannot express at the same width.

<Warning>
When comparing FFN variants, check the parameter count is matched. A gated
variant with three matrices at the same hidden width has 1.5x the parameters, so
an unmatched comparison measures capacity rather than the gating. This is the
same control problem that appears everywhere in this material: hold the resource
fixed and vary only the thing you are studying.
</Warning>
