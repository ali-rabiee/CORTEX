  keyPoints:
    - "Say what the update is: $\\mu/\\sqrt{\\mu^2+\\sigma^2}$, a per-coordinate signal-to-noise ratio bounded in $[-1,1]$ — not a rescaled gradient."
    - "Derive the two consequences: scale invariance per coordinate, and a learning rate that means a distance in parameter space and therefore transfers."

interview:
  answer: >-
    The characterisation I'd lead with is that Adam's update isn't a rescaled
    gradient — it's a signal-to-noise ratio. The first moment estimates the
    gradient's mean and the second estimates its second moment, not its
    variance, so the ratio m-hat over root v-hat converges to mu over root of mu
    squared plus sigma squared. That's bounded in minus one to one, and you can
    verify it numerically — it matches to three decimal places. Two things
    follow and they explain almost everything about the optimiser. First,
    per-coordinate scale invariance: multiply any coordinate's gradient by a
    constant and both moments scale, one linearly and one quadratically, so the
    ratio is unchanged. A consistent gradient of a thousandth and a consistent
    gradient of a thousand both produce an update of exactly one. That means
    layers whose gradients differ by orders of magnitude — normal in a deep
    network — all get comparable steps with no per-layer tuning. Second, the
    learning rate means something portable. In SGD the step is eta times the
    gradient, so a good eta depends on how big the gradient happens to be, which
    changes with the loss scale, the depth, and the layer. In Adam the update is
    at most one, so eta is a distance to move in parameter space. That's why
    three-times-ten-to-the-minus-four is a sensible starting point across wildly
    different transformers, and why the same claim would be meaningless for SGD.
    On the bias correction: the moments start at zero, so unrolling gives
    expectation of m equal to one minus beta-one to the t times the true mean —
    dividing by that is an exact correction. What's worth adding is that it
    matters far more for v, because beta-two is 0.999. At step ten, one minus
    beta-one to the ten is 0.65 and one minus beta-two to the ten is about 0.01,
    so v is under-estimated a hundredfold, its square root tenfold, and an
    uncorrected update is ten times too large. People usually patch that with a
    warmup instead of noticing it's a bug. And AdamW: adding L2 to the loss puts
    lambda-theta in the gradient, which then gets divided by root v-hat like
    everything else — so coordinates with large gradients receive less decay,
    meaning the parameters doing the most work are regularised the least. That
    inverts the intent. Decoupling applies the decay to the parameters directly,
    outside the adaptive scaling. Under plain SGD the two are equivalent up to a
    factor of the learning rate, which is why nobody noticed for years.
  followUps:
    - q: "When would you use SGD with momentum instead?"
      a: >-
        When I'm chasing the last point of accuracy on a vision benchmark and
        can afford the tuning, because there's a real and repeatedly observed
        generalisation gap on some of those problems — well-tuned SGD with
        momentum ends up in flatter or otherwise better-generalising solutions.
        I'd be careful about how I state it, though: the effect is
        problem-dependent, it mostly hasn't shown up for transformers on
        language, and "well-tuned" is doing a lot of work, since SGD's learning
        rate doesn't transfer the way Adam's does. So my default is AdamW and
        my reason is robustness rather than a claim that it optimises better.
        If someone tells me SGD generalises better, I'd want to know on which
        problem and whether both were tuned equally hard.
    - q: "Your model diverges in the first hundred steps. Walk me through it."
      a: >-
        First I'd look at update-to-parameter norm ratios per layer, because
        that's scale-free, comparable across layers, and has a known healthy
        range around ten to the minus three. Uniformly large says the learning
        rate is too high; large in one layer localises it immediately. Then
        specifically for early divergence I'd check the bias correction is
        actually applied, since without it the first few hundred steps are
        roughly ten times too large and that's exactly the window that fails.
        Then epsilon against my precision — one-times-ten-to-the-minus-eight is
        below the smallest normal fp16 number, so the guard silently vanishes
        when a small v makes it necessary, which is why mixed-precision recipes
        keep the optimiser state in fp32. And I'd check clipping is applied
        after unscaling, because clipping a scaled gradient clips at the wrong
        threshold and either never fires or always fires, neither of which
        announces itself.
    - q: "Why does Adam need so much memory, and what would you do about it?"
      a: >-
        Two extra tensors the size of the parameters — the two moments — so in
        fp32 it's sixteen bytes per parameter counting the weight, the gradient,
        and both moments. A seven-billion-parameter model is a hundred and
        twelve gigabytes before a single activation, which doesn't fit on any
        single accelerator. So sharding the optimiser state across devices is a
        precondition rather than an optimisation. Beyond that there are two
        directions. Factor the second moment into row and column statistics,
        which is Adafactor, taking state from order n-times-m to order n-plus-m
        per matrix — the approximation being that the second moment is roughly
        rank one. Or drop it entirely and take the sign, which is what Lion
        does, keeping only momentum. That Lion is competitive is itself
        informative: Adam's update already tends to plus or minus one for
        consistent gradients, so the second moment's main job may be producing
        that boundedness rather than encoding curvature.
    - q: "How do you know whether a bad run is the optimiser or the data?"
      a: >-
        Log Adam's own signal-to-noise quantity — the mean absolute value of
        m-hat over root v-hat. It's exactly what the optimiser is computing and
        it separates the two cases cleanly. If it's near one, the gradients are
        consistent and Adam is taking full steps, so a problem is elsewhere. If
        it's near zero, the gradients are mostly noise and Adam is correctly
        refusing to move — and no learning rate fixes that. That points at
        batch size, augmentation being too aggressive, label noise, or
        something wrong with the data pipeline. I find that a much better first
        question than "what learning rate should I try", because it tells you
        whether the optimiser is even the right thing to be adjusting.
  traps:
    - "Describing Adam as 'momentum plus per-parameter learning rates' and stopping. That is true and omits the signal-to-noise property, which is what explains why it works."

    - "Treating the bias correction as a minor detail. Without it, $v$ is under-estimated a hundredfold at step 10 and the early updates are an order of magnitude too large."
    - "Claiming Adam is simply better than SGD. It is far more forgiving and transfers better; the generalisation gap on some vision problems is real and worth acknowledging."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Diverges in the first hundred steps → bias correction, or $\epsilon$ in low precision">
Without the correction, $v$ is under-estimated a hundredfold at step 10 and
updates are ten times too large. And $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$ is below fp16's
smallest normal, so the guard vanishes exactly when needed.
</Step>

<Step title="Regularisation seems to do nothing → `Adam` rather than `AdamW`">
L2 in the loss is attenuated by $\sqrt{\hat v}$, most where gradients are
largest. Switching requires re-tuning the coefficient, which confirms the
diagnosis.
</Step>

<Step title="One layer's updates are enormous → check per-layer update ratios">
Around $10^{-3}$ is healthy. This localises what a global gradient norm cannot.
</Step>

<Step title="Mean Adam SNR near zero → not an optimiser problem">
The gradients are inconsistent. Batch size, augmentation, label noise, data
pipeline. No learning rate fixes it, and Adam is behaving correctly.
</Step>

<Step title="Out of memory before training starts → optimiser state">
16 bytes per parameter in fp32. Shard it, or factor the second moment.
</Step>
</Steps>

<InterviewAnswer />

<Warning>
The sentence that distinguishes this answer is that the update is a
signal-to-noise ratio rather than a rescaled gradient. Everyone can say "adaptive
per-parameter learning rates", and that phrasing does not distinguish dividing by
$|g|$ from dividing by $\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[g^2]}$ — which is the entire mechanism.
From the sharper version, scale invariance, the portability of the learning rate,
and the automatic damping of noisy coordinates all follow in one step each, and
the AdamW argument becomes obvious rather than something to memorise.
</Warning>
