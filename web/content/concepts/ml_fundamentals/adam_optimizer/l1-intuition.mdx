## The update is not a rescaled gradient

Adam is usually introduced as "momentum plus per-parameter learning rates", which
is true and hides the property that matters. Look at what the update actually
computes:

$$
\Delta\theta = -\eta\,\frac{\hat m_t}{\sqrt{\hat v_t} + \epsilon}
$$

where $\hat m$ estimates the gradient's mean and $\hat v$ estimates its *second
moment*. For a coordinate whose gradient has mean $\mu$ and noise $\sigma$:

$$
\frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v}} \;\approx\; \frac{\mu}{\sqrt{\mu^2 + \sigma^2}}
$$

<KeyIdea>
That is a **signal-to-noise ratio**, bounded in $[-1, 1]$ — not a gradient. Adam
does not ask "how steep is it here?" but "how confident am I about the
direction?", and it moves a fixed distance scaled by that confidence.
</KeyIdea>

## Two consequences that explain everything else

<Steps>
<Step title="Per-coordinate scale invariance">
Multiply any coordinate's gradient by a constant and its update is unchanged. So
layers whose gradients differ by orders of magnitude — normal in a deep network —
all receive comparable-sized steps without anyone tuning per-layer rates. This is
why Adam works on badly-conditioned problems where SGD needs careful scaling.
</Step>

<Step title="The learning rate means something portable">
In SGD the step is $\eta g$, so a good $\eta$ depends on how large $g$ happens to
be — which changes with the loss scale, depth, initialisation, and layer. In Adam
the update magnitude is at most 1, so $\eta$ is a *distance in parameter space*.
That is why $3\times10^{-4}$ is a reasonable starting point across wildly
different transformers, and why the same claim would be meaningless for SGD.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Intuition>
A hiker who takes a fixed stride, and shortens it in proportion to how uncertain
they are about which way is downhill. On a clear slope they stride confidently
whether the slope is gentle or steep. In fog they shuffle.

SGD is a hiker whose stride length is proportional to the steepness — fine on
well-behaved terrain, and it either crawls or leaps depending on units nobody
chose deliberately.
</Intuition>

## The bias correction, and why it is not optional

The moment estimates start at zero, so early on they are biased toward zero — at
step 1, $m_1 = (1-\beta_1)g_1 = 0.1\,g_1$, a tenth of the true gradient. Dividing
by $1 - \beta_1^t$ removes exactly that.

<Warning>
The correction matters far more for $\hat v$ than for $\hat m$, because
$\beta_2 = 0.999$ decays much more slowly. At step 10, $1-\beta_1^{10} = 0.65$
while $1-\beta_2^{10} = 0.00996$ — so $v$ is under-estimated by a factor of a
hundred, its square root by ten, and an uncorrected update is roughly ten times
too large. Omitting the correction produces a violent first few hundred steps
that people usually respond to by adding a warmup, which is a patch for a bug.
</Warning>

## The weight-decay trap

Adding $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|^2$ to the loss contributes $\lambda\theta$ to
the gradient — and then that term gets divided by $\sqrt{\hat v}$ along with
everything else.

<KeyIdea>
So coordinates with large gradients receive *less* weight decay, which is exactly
backwards: the parameters doing the most work are regularised the least. AdamW
fixes it by applying the decay directly to the parameters, outside the adaptive
scaling. It is a two-line change and it is why AdamW rather than Adam is the
default everywhere.
</KeyIdea>

## What it costs

Two extra tensors the size of the parameters, so in fp32: 4 bytes for the weight,
4 for its gradient, and 8 for the two moments — **16 bytes per parameter**. A 7B
model is 112 GB of optimiser state and parameters before a single activation,
which is why sharding that state is a precondition rather than an optimisation at
any serious scale.

<Warning>
Adam is a strong default and not a universally better optimiser. Well-tuned SGD
with momentum still generalises better on some vision benchmarks, and the gap has
been studied enough to be taken seriously rather than dismissed. The honest
position is that Adam is much more forgiving and transfers far better, which for
most work is worth more than a small final-accuracy difference — and that if you
are chasing the last point on a benchmark, the comparison is worth running.
</Warning>
