## The picture everyone learned

Underfit and the model is too simple to capture the pattern; overfit and it
memorises noise. Test error falls, then rises, and the optimum sits in the
middle. That is a correct description of a regime — models with fewer parameters
than data points — and modern networks are not trained in it.

<Figure src="/figures/double-descent.svg"
        alt="Test error against model capacity. A dashed grey curve shows the classical U with an optimum in the middle. A solid curve continues past it, peaking sharply at the interpolation threshold and then descending again to below the classical optimum."
        caption="The classical U-curve is the left third of the real picture. Past the point where the model can fit the training data exactly, error descends a second time — frequently to below the classical optimum." />

<KeyIdea>
The peak sits at the **interpolation threshold**: exactly enough capacity to fit
the training set and no more. There, the model is forced into the one solution
that fits, however contorted. Add capacity and there are many solutions to choose
among, and the optimiser reliably picks a better-behaved one.
</KeyIdea>

<Intuition>
Fitting a curve through noisy points. With too few degrees of freedom you cannot
follow the trend. With exactly enough, you are forced through every point
including the noise, and the curve whips violently between them.

With far more degrees of freedom than points, there are infinitely many curves
through them — and the smoothest is available. Something has to prefer it, and
that something is the optimiser rather than the capacity.
</Intuition>

## The experiment that broke the old explanation

Take a standard architecture that generalises well. Replace every label with a
random one. Train it. It reaches **zero training error** — memorising a dataset
with no learnable structure at all.

<Warning>
The consequence is about what a bound can say. If the hypothesis class is rich
enough to fit arbitrary labels, then any generalisation guarantee derived from
properties of that class alone must also hold when the labels are noise — where
generalisation is impossible. So such a bound cannot be tight enough to be
informative.

That is not a claim that the bounds are wrong. It is a claim that capacity is the
wrong variable, and the explanation has to involve the data and the optimiser.
</Warning>

## What replaces it

Past the interpolation threshold there are infinitely many parameter settings
achieving zero training error, and they generalise very differently. So the
question changes from *how large is the hypothesis class* to **which member does
the optimiser select**.

<Steps>
<Step title="Gradient descent is not neutral">
It starts at a particular initialisation and takes a particular path, so it
arrives at a particular interpolating solution. Which one is a property of the
algorithm, not of the model class.
</Step>

<Step title="And the selection is systematic">
Networks trained without weight decay or dropout still generalise far better than
capacity alone permits. Explicit regularisation matters and is not the main
effect — the optimiser's preference is.
</Step>

<Step title="Structure in the data is what makes it work">
On real data the easiest solutions to reach are also the ones that generalise; on
random labels no such solution exists, so the model is forced to memorise. The
same optimiser produces both outcomes, which is why the data has to be part of
the explanation.
</Step>
</Steps>

## What still applies

None of this means overfitting is not real, and the practical picture is
unchanged in the places it matters.

<Steps>
<Step title="Small data still overfits">
With a few hundred examples you are in the classical regime, and the U-curve
describes what you will see. Most robot-learning datasets are here.
</Step>

<Step title="Validation still tells you">
A rising validation loss while training loss falls is still overfitting, whatever
the theory says about why. The diagnostic did not change.
</Step>

<Step title="Regularisation still helps">
Augmentation, early stopping, and weight decay all still improve results. What
changed is the *explanation* — they are shaping which solution the optimiser
finds rather than shrinking a hypothesis class.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
The practically useful update is narrower than "everything you learned is wrong".
It is: stop reasoning about parameter count as though it predicts generalisation.
A larger model is not automatically more prone to overfitting, and the intuition
that it is will lead you to make a model smaller when the fix was more data or
better augmentation.
</KeyIdea>
