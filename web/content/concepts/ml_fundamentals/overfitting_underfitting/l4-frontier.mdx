## The demolition

<PaperCard refKey="zhang2017rethinking" />

Take a standard architecture that generalises well, replace every label with a
random one, and train. It reaches zero training error.

<KeyIdea>
The paper's contribution is an argument about what a bound can say, and it is
worth reconstructing rather than remembering. If the hypothesis class can fit
*any* labelling, then a bound of the form "test error $\le$ train error $+$
complexity of the class" must also hold when the labels are noise — where test
error is at chance and train error is zero. So the complexity term must be at
least as large as the worst possible error, which makes it uninformative for the
real-data case too.

This does not say the bounds are wrong. It says capacity is the wrong variable,
and the explanation must involve the data or the optimiser.
</KeyIdea>

The paper also found that explicit regularisation — weight decay, dropout,
augmentation — helps but is not what makes generalisation possible: removing it
still leaves networks generalising far better than any capacity account permits.

## Naming the curve

<PaperCard refKey="belkin2019doubledescent" />

The classical U-curve, extended past the interpolation threshold, has a second
descent. Test error peaks exactly where the model first has enough capacity to
fit the training set, then falls again — frequently below the classical optimum.

<Warning>
The important detail is the *breadth*: the effect appears in random forests and
kernel methods as well as neural networks. That constrains what an explanation
may appeal to. If it were deep-learning-specific, depth or non-convexity would be
candidate causes; appearing across model families rules those out and points at
the geometry of interpolating solution sets — which is exactly what the
minimum-norm analysis captures.

It also reframes the classical picture as *incomplete* rather than wrong. The
U-curve correctly describes the underparameterised regime it was developed for.
</Warning>

## Three axes, one mechanism

<PaperCard refKey="nakkiran2020doubledescent" />

Double descent in model size, in training epochs, and — most surprisingly — in
dataset size.

<Steps>
<Step title="Model size">
The familiar version. Sweep width and the test error peaks at the threshold.
</Step>

<Step title="Training time">
At fixed size, error can rise then fall again as training continues, because
longer training increases the *effective* complexity of the solution being found.
</Step>

<Step title="Dataset size — where more data hurts">
Genuinely counterintuitive. The threshold depends on the ratio of capacity to
data, so growing $n$ at fixed $p$ can walk a comfortably overparameterised model
into the peak. A narrow regime, and a decisive demonstration that the threshold
rather than any monotone quantity governs the curve.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
The unification is an *effective model complexity* — roughly, the largest dataset
the training procedure can interpolate — with the peak occurring when it matches
the actual dataset size. That makes the three axes the same phenomenon viewed
along different directions, which is a more satisfying account than three
separate observations.
</KeyIdea>

## Separating fitting from generalising in time

<PaperCard refKey="power2022grokking" />

On small algorithmic datasets, training error reaches zero and validation
accuracy stays at chance — for tens of thousands of steps — and then rises
sharply to near-perfect.

<Warning>
This rules out an entire family of explanations. If interpolating the training
set were the event determining generalisation, nothing could change afterwards.
That something does change, long after the training loss has stopped improving,
is direct evidence that the optimiser continues *selecting among interpolating
solutions* well after finding one.

It is observed most cleanly on small, structured, algorithmic tasks with weight
decay, so it should not be over-generalised. But as an existence proof separating
the two events it is decisive, and it is the cleanest empirical support for the
implicit-regularisation framing.
</Warning>

## Where this leaves the field

<Steps>
<Step title="Established">
Capacity alone does not explain generalisation. Double descent is real, spans
model families, and appears along several axes. The classical U-curve describes a
regime rather than a law.
</Step>

<Step title="The working account">
Implicit regularisation: among the many interpolating solutions, the optimiser
selects systematically, and which one it selects depends on the initialisation,
the algorithm, and the structure of the data.
</Step>

<Step title="Open">
What gradient descent implicitly minimises for a deep network. It is known
exactly for linear least squares — the minimum-norm interpolator — and only in
restricted cases beyond that. So "implicit regularisation" currently *names* the
phenomenon rather than explaining it, and presenting it as a solved account
overstates what is known.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
The practically actionable residue is narrower than the theoretical upheaval:
stop treating parameter count as a predictor of overfitting, and diagnose from
the train-validation gap and the learning curve rather than from model size. Most
of what changed is the explanation rather than the practice — but the one piece
of practice that did change, reaching for a smaller model first, is a common
reflex worth unlearning.
</KeyIdea>
