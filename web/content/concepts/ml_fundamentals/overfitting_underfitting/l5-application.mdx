interview:
  answer: >-
    The classical picture is that error decomposes into bias, variance, and
    irreducible noise, and that as capacity grows bias falls while variance
    rises — so there's an optimum in the middle and past it you overfit. I'd
    separate two things there, because only one of them fails. The decomposition
    is an identity for squared loss: it holds for any estimator and it says
    nothing about capacity. What produces the U-shape is an additional empirical
    claim, that variance increases monotonically with capacity, and that's what
    turns out to be false in the regime modern networks are trained in. What
    actually happens is double descent. Test error peaks at the interpolation
    threshold — where the model has exactly enough capacity to fit the training
    set — and then descends again, often below the classical optimum. The
    mechanism is about how many interpolating solutions exist. Below the
    threshold there are none, so the model underfits, which is benign. At it
    there's essentially one, so the model is forced into whatever contorted
    function passes through every noisy point, with no smoother alternative
    available. Above it there are infinitely many, and the optimiser selects a
    well-behaved one. What makes that more than a curve-fitting observation is
    the random-label result. The same architectures that generalise well can fit
    completely random labels to zero training error — and that's an argument
    about what a bound can say. If the hypothesis class can fit any labelling,
    then a bound depending only on that class must also hold when the labels are
    noise, where test error is at chance, so the complexity term has to be at
    least as large as the worst possible error. It's vacuous. That doesn't make
    the bounds wrong, it makes capacity the wrong variable — the explanation has
    to involve the data and the optimiser. The replacement is implicit
    regularisation: among the many settings that interpolate, gradient descent
    reliably finds particular ones. You can compute exactly which for linear
    least squares — starting from zero, every update lies in the row space of
    the data, so the iterate never leaves it and you land on the minimum-norm
    interpolator, a regularisation preference with no regulariser in the loss.
    For deep networks that's an analogy rather than a theorem, and I'd say so:
    implicit regularisation currently names the phenomenon more than it explains
    it.
  followUps:
    - q: "So what would you actually do when validation loss is rising?"
      a: >-
        Diagnose from the gap first, because half the time the reflexive
        response is backwards. High training error and high validation error
        with a small gap is underfitting, and adding regularisation there makes
        it worse — the fixes are more capacity, longer training, or a higher
        learning rate. A real gap means overfitting, and then I'd go in order of
        expected value: more data, then augmentation as a cheap approximation to
        it, then early stopping with best-weight restoration, then weight decay
        and dropout. Shrinking the model is last, not first, because past the
        interpolation threshold reducing capacity moves you toward the peak
        rather than away from it. And before any of that I'd check the split for
        near-duplicates, because a leaking validation set produces numbers that
        look like something else entirely.
    - q: "How do you decide whether collecting more data is worth it?"
      a: >-
        A learning curve — error against training set size, at several
        fractions, with a few seeds each because the small-n points are high
        variance. If the train and validation curves are still converging as n
        grows, the model is data-limited and collection is the highest-value
        action, and the curve gives you a rough sense of how much you need. If
        they've flattened with a persistent gap, more data won't close it and
        the limit is model capacity, feature quality, or label noise — and I'd
        check the labels before the other two, because it's cheap and it's
        surprisingly often the answer. That's a question a single train/val
        number simply cannot answer, and it matters most in exactly the domains
        where data is expensive, which is most robotics.
    - q: "Does any of this change how you'd work on a small robot dataset?"
      a: >-
        Not much, and that's worth saying plainly rather than implying the new
        theory overturns everything. With a few hundred demonstrations and a
        modest model you're underparameterised — you're on the left branch, and
        the classical U-curve describes what you'll actually see. So the
        classical intuitions remain the right working model in that domain. What
        I'd carry over is narrower: don't reason about parameter count as though
        it predicts generalisation, especially if you're fine-tuning a large
        pretrained model, because there you're firmly in the overparameterised
        regime and the reflex to shrink it is wrong. And the diagnostic advice is
        regime-independent — the gap and the learning curve tell you what to do
        regardless of which branch you're on.
    - q: "What's grokking, and does it matter?"
      a: >-
        It's the observation that on some small algorithmic tasks, training error
        reaches zero and validation stays at chance for tens of thousands of
        steps, and then generalisation appears suddenly. What makes it
        interesting is that it separates fitting from generalising in *time*,
        which rules out any account where interpolating the training set is the
        event that determines the outcome — something keeps changing in the
        solution long after the training loss has stopped improving. That's
        direct evidence for the implicit-regularisation framing, since the
        optimiser is evidently still selecting among interpolating solutions. I'd
        be careful not to over-generalise it: it's cleanest on small structured
        tasks with weight decay, and I wouldn't claim it's what happens in
        ordinary training. As an existence proof it's decisive; as a description
        of typical behaviour it isn't established.
  traps:

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Both errors high, small gap → underfitting, and do not regularise">
More capacity, longer training, a higher learning rate, better features. Adding
regularisation here makes it worse, and it is the reflexive wrong move.
</Step>

<Step title="Large gap → overfitting, in this order">
More data, augmentation, early stopping with best-weight restoration, then
explicit regularisation. Smaller model last.
</Step>

<Step title="Validation better than training → check for a bug first">
Dropout or batch-norm still in training mode at evaluation, an easier validation
split, or augmentation applied asymmetrically. Occasionally real, usually not.
</Step>

<Step title="Suspiciously good validation → check the split for near-duplicates">
Sequential or session-structured data leaks across a random split, and the gap
you are looking at is then meaningless.
</Step>

<Step title="Cannot decide whether to collect more data → learning curve">
Converging curves mean data-limited. Flat with a gap means the model, the
features, or the labels — check the labels first.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The move that makes this answer precise is separating the identity from the
premise. "Bias-variance is wrong" is fashionable and inaccurate; the
decomposition is exact algebra and what fails is the empirical claim that
variance rises monotonically with capacity. Then give double descent with its
mechanism — the solution set has one element at the threshold and infinitely many
past it — and the random-label result as an argument about *bounds* rather than
as a surprising fact. Finish by being honest that implicit regularisation names
the phenomenon more than it explains it, which is the state of the field and
signals you have read past the summaries.
</Warning>
