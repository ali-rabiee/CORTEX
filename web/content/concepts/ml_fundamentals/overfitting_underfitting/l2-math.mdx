## The decomposition, and what it does not say

For squared loss, with $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$ and $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0$,
$\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2$:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\varepsilon}\Big[\big(y - \hat f_\mathcal{D}(x)\big)^2\Big]
= \underbrace{\big(f(x) - \mathbb{E}_\mathcal{D}[\hat f_\mathcal{D}(x)]\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}
+ \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}_\mathcal{D}\big(\hat f_\mathcal{D}(x)\big)}_{\text{variance}}
+ \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible}}
$$

<Derivation title="It is an identity, and the U-curve is an extra claim">

Add and subtract $\bar f(x) = \mathbb{E}_\mathcal{D}[\hat f_\mathcal{D}(x)]$ inside
the square and expand. The cross terms vanish: $\varepsilon$ is independent of
$\mathcal{D}$ and mean-zero, and
$\mathbb{E}_\mathcal{D}[\hat f - \bar f] = 0$ by definition. What remains is the
three terms above.

**Note what this contains.** It decomposes the error of *any* estimator, exactly.
It makes no reference to model capacity, parameter counts, or hypothesis classes.

**And what the classical picture adds.** The U-shape requires a further,
empirical claim:

$$
\text{variance is monotonically increasing in capacity}
$$

That is true when adding parameters means fitting more of the noise — the
underparameterised regime the decomposition was developed for. It is false past
the interpolation threshold, where additional capacity instead permits a
*smoother* interpolating solution and measured variance falls.

**So the algebra never failed.** What failed is an empirical premise attached to
it, and separating the two is the whole content of the modern correction.

</Derivation>

## Why capacity bounds cannot be the explanation

<Derivation title="The random-label argument, stated as a claim about bounds">

Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a hypothesis class and suppose a generalisation bound of the
form

$$
\text{test error} \;\le\; \text{train error} \;+\; C\big(\mathcal{H}, n\big)
$$

where $C$ depends only on the class and the sample size — VC dimension,
Rademacher complexity, norm-based measures.

**The observation.** For a standard architecture, replacing all labels with
random ones still yields zero training error. So $\mathcal{H}$ contains a
function fitting *any* labelling of the training set.

**The consequence.** The bound applies to the random-label case too. There the
true test error is chance level — say $1 - 1/K$ — while the training error is
zero. So

$$
C(\mathcal{H}, n) \;\ge\; 1 - \tfrac1K
$$

The complexity term must be at least as large as the worst possible error, which
makes the bound vacuous for the real-data case as well.

**What this does and does not establish.** It does not say the bounds are
incorrect — they are valid, just uninformative. It says that any explanation
depending *only* on the model class cannot distinguish the two cases, so the
explanation must involve the data distribution, the optimiser, or both.

</Derivation>

## The interpolation threshold

<Derivation title="Why the peak is where the solution set is a single point">

Consider fitting $n$ points with a model of $p$ parameters and count the
interpolating solutions.

**$p < n$:** generically no exact fit exists. The model underfits, the fitted
function is a compromise, and it is stable to resampling — low variance, high
bias.

**$p = n$:** generically exactly one solution passes through all $n$ points. The
model has no freedom, so it must adopt whatever function that is — and the unique
interpolator through noisy points is typically extreme between them. Small changes
in the data produce large changes in the function, so variance peaks.

**$p > n$:** the interpolating set is an affine subspace of dimension $p - n$,
containing infinitely many solutions. The optimiser now *selects*, and it selects
systematically — so a smoother member becomes reachable and variance falls again.

**A prediction that follows.** Adding a small amount of explicit regularisation
should flatten the peak, because it breaks the uniqueness at $p = n$ by preferring
a particular nearby solution rather than the exact interpolant. This is observed,
and it is a good check that the mechanism is right rather than the description.

</Derivation>

## Implicit regularisation, made concrete

The claim that "the optimiser selects" is vague until you can compute what it
selects. In one case you can, exactly.

<Derivation title="Gradient descent finds the minimum-norm interpolator">

Least squares with $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$, $p > n$, so the system is
underdetermined and infinitely many $\theta$ satisfy $X\theta = y$.

Gradient descent on $\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12\|X\theta - y\|^2$:

$$
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\, X^\top(X\theta_t - y)
$$

**The key structural fact.** The update $X^\top(\cdot)$ lies in the **row space**
of $X$, for any residual. Starting from $\theta_0 = 0$, every iterate is a sum of
such updates, so

$$
\theta_t \in \operatorname{row}(X) \quad \text{for all } t
$$

**And the row space meets the solution set once.** The interpolating solutions
form the affine set $\{\theta : X\theta = y\} = \theta^\dagger + \ker(X)$, and
$\operatorname{row}(X) = \ker(X)^\perp$. An affine set and the orthogonal
complement of its direction space intersect in exactly one point — the one of
minimum $\ell_2$ norm.

$$
\boxed{\;\theta_\infty = \arg\min_{\theta}\ \|\theta\|_2 \quad\text{s.t.}\quad X\theta = y\;}
$$

**A regularisation preference with no regulariser.** Nothing in the loss mentions
$\|\theta\|$; the bias comes entirely from the algorithm's geometry and the
initialisation. Change the initialisation and you converge to a different
interpolator; change the optimiser — to a coordinate-wise adaptive method, say —
and the implicit norm changes too.

**The limitation to be honest about.** This is exact for linear least squares.
For deep networks it is a suggestive analogy, not a theorem: what gradient
descent implicitly minimises there is not known in general, and results exist
only for restricted cases.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
That last caveat is the honest state of the field. We know capacity is the wrong
variable, we know the optimiser's selection is what matters, and we have a
complete characterisation of that selection only in linear cases. "Implicit
regularisation" names the phenomenon rather than explaining it — and saying so is
more accurate than presenting it as a solved account.
</KeyIdea>

## Where the classical picture still holds

<Steps>
<Step title="Small $n$">
With a few hundred examples and a modestly-sized model you are underparameterised
and the U-curve describes what you will observe. Most robot-learning datasets sit
here, which is why the classical intuitions remain useful in that domain.
</Step>

<Step title="High label noise">
Double descent's second descent is weaker when a large fraction of labels are
wrong, because interpolating them is genuinely harmful however smooth the
solution. The peak is also higher and wider.
</Step>

<Step title="Early stopping">
Stopping before interpolation keeps you on the left branch entirely, which is
partly why it works so reliably and why it interacts with the model-size question
in ways the classical picture does not predict.
</Step>
</Steps>
