## The chain rule as a matrix product

A network is a composition $L = f_n \circ \cdots \circ f_1$, and the derivative is
a product of Jacobians:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta} = \underbrace{J_n}_{1\times d_n} \underbrace{J_{n-1}}_{d_n \times d_{n-1}} \cdots \underbrace{J_1}_{d_2 \times d_1}
$$

<Derivation title="Associativity, and why the order costs different amounts">

Matrix multiplication is associative, so this can be bracketed either way:

$$
\underbrace{\big(\cdots\big((J_n J_{n-1})J_{n-2}\big)\cdots\big)}_{\text{reverse mode: left to right}}
\qquad
\underbrace{\big(\cdots\big(J_n\big(J_{n-1}(J_{n-2}\cdots)\big)\big)\cdots\big)}_{\text{forward mode: right to left}}
$$

**Count the work.** Multiplying an $a\times b$ by a $b\times c$ matrix costs
$abc$. Suppose all hidden dimensions are $d$, with $n$ inputs and $m$ outputs.

*Reverse mode* starts from the $m \times d$ end. Each product is
$(m\times d)(d\times d)$, costing $md^2$, and there are $L$ of them:
$O(L\,m\,d^2)$.

*Forward mode* starts from the $d \times n$ end. Each product is
$(d\times d)(d\times n)$, costing $nd^2$, for $O(L\,n\,d^2)$.

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{\text{reverse}}{\text{forward}} = \frac{m}{n}\;}
$$

**Read it.** Neither mode is inherently better — you pay for whichever dimension
you must sweep. Forward mode propagates one *column* of the Jacobian per pass;
reverse mode propagates one *row*.

**Why deep learning uses reverse.** A loss is a scalar, so $m = 1$ and $n$ is the
parameter count. At $10^9$ parameters the ratio is $10^{-9}$ — reverse mode gives
every gradient in one backward pass where forward mode would need a billion.

**Where forward mode is correct.** Sensitivity of many outputs to one input:
propagating a single uncertain parameter through a large simulation. The symmetry
is real, and recognising it stops backpropagation looking like a special trick.

</Derivation>

## Vector-Jacobian products

The derivation above suggests forming Jacobians, and nothing does. A $d = 4096$
linear layer's Jacobian is $4096 \times 4096$ — 64 MiB in fp32, per example.

What is actually computed is a **vector-Jacobian product**: given
$\bar y = \partial L/\partial y$ flowing in, produce $\bar x = \bar y^\top J$
without forming $J$.

<Derivation title="The VJP for a linear layer, and the factor of two">

For $y = Wx$:

$$
\bar x = \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = W^\top \bar y,
\qquad
\bar W = \frac{\partial L}{\partial W} = \bar y\, x^\top
$$

Both are ordinary matrix operations of the same order as the forward matmul, and
neither materialises $J$.

**Two quantities, hence the factor of two.** $\bar x$ is needed to continue
propagating backwards; $\bar W$ is needed to update the weights. So

$$
C_{\text{forward}} \approx 2N,
\qquad
C_{\text{backward}} \approx 4N,
\qquad
C_{\text{train}} \approx 6ND
$$

per token, for $N$ parameters and $D$ tokens — the standard training-compute
rule, and now derived rather than asserted.

**The exception worth knowing.** The first layer needs no $\bar x$, since nothing
precedes it. More usefully, freezing early layers does *not* save the
corresponding backward work if any trainable layer sits below them — you still
need $\bar x$ to propagate through. That is why partial fine-tuning saves less
than the parameter count suggests, and why freezing the *last* layers saves more
than freezing the first.

**Also note $\bar W = \bar y x^\top$ requires $x$.** The layer's input must still
exist when the backward pass arrives, which is the memory cost of reverse mode
stated precisely.

</Derivation>

## The memory schedule

<Derivation title="Why $\sqrt L$">

Store every $k$-th layer's activation. Two contributions to peak memory:

- **Checkpoints kept for the whole backward pass:** $L/k$ of them.
- **The segment being recomputed:** up to $k$ activations, held while replaying
  that segment.

$$
M(k) = \frac{L}{k} + k,
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}k} = -\frac{L}{k^2} + 1 = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
k = \sqrt L,\ \ M = 2\sqrt L
$$

**Memory goes from $O(L)$ to $O(\sqrt L)$** — 96 layers to about 20 activations.

**Compute cost.** Each segment is replayed once, so the total extra work is one
additional forward pass, independent of $k$. Against a $6N$ baseline that is
$2N$ more, about 33%.

**And it is exact.** The recomputed activations are bitwise the same
computation — no approximation, no accuracy question, which is why it can be
switched on by default when memory is tight.

</Derivation>

## Why long products misbehave

The gradient reaching layer $k$ is $\prod_{j>k} J_j$, and a long product of
matrices is governed by the product of their singular values.

$$
\Big\|\prod_{j=k}^{L} J_j\Big\| \;\lesssim\; \prod_{j=k}^{L}\|J_j\|
$$

If typical $\|J\| = \sigma$, the magnitude goes like $\sigma^{L-k}$ —
exponential in depth, vanishing for $\sigma < 1$ and exploding for $\sigma > 1$.
There is no stable middle unless something enforces $\sigma \approx 1$.

<Steps>
<Step title="Initialisation gets $\sigma$ near 1 at step zero">
He and Xavier scaling choose the weight variance so that activation and gradient
variance are preserved layer to layer at initialisation. Necessary and not
sufficient, since it degrades as training moves the weights.
</Step>

<Step title="Normalisation keeps it near 1 during training">
By rescaling activations to a fixed scale at every layer, it removes the
accumulation that initialisation alone cannot prevent.
</Step>

<Step title="Residual connections change the structure">
$\partial(x + F(x))/\partial x = I + J$, so the product expands to
$I + \sum J_j + \cdots$ — a term that passes through no multiplicative factor.
This is the structural fix rather than a correction, and it is why depth became
trainable.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
The first two keep the product's factors near one; the third removes the need for
them to be. That is why residual connections mattered so much more than better
initialisation, and it is the same argument that makes pre-norm the right
placement in a transformer.
</KeyIdea>
