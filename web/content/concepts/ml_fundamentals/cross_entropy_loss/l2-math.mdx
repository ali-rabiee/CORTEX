## The loss

For logits $z \in \mathbb{R}^K$, softmax probabilities $p$, and a target
distribution $q$:

$$
p_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}},
\qquad
\mathcal{L} = -\sum_i q_i \log p_i
$$

With a one-hot $q$ this is $-\log p_{y^\star}$.

<Derivation title="The gradient, and why the pairing is not arbitrary">

**Step 1 — the softmax Jacobian.** Differentiate
$p_i = e^{z_i}/\sum_j e^{z_j}$:

$$
\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_j} = p_i\big(\delta_{ij} - p_j\big)
$$

**Step 2 — the loss derivative.** With $\mathcal{L} = -\sum_i q_i\log p_i$:

$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z_j}
= -\sum_i \frac{q_i}{p_i}\,\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_j}
= -\sum_i \frac{q_i}{p_i}\,p_i\big(\delta_{ij} - p_j\big)
$$

**Step 3 — watch the cancellation.** The $p_i$ from the Jacobian annihilates the
$1/p_i$ from the logarithm:

$$
= -\sum_i q_i\big(\delta_{ij} - p_j\big)
= -q_j + p_j\sum_i q_i
= p_j - q_j
$$

using $\sum_i q_i = 1$. So

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z} = p - q\;}
$$

**Why this makes the pairing necessary.** Each function has a pathology the other
removes. A softmax with squared error keeps the $p_i(1-p_i)$ factor and inherits
saturation. A logarithm with a different output nonlinearity leaves a $1/p$ that
diverges as the prediction gets small. Composed, they produce something bounded
by 1, exactly zero when correct, and maximal when maximally wrong.

</Derivation>

<Derivation title="What squared error would give, with numbers">

For a binary problem with $\hat p = \sigma(z)$ and target $y$:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}} = (\sigma(z) - y)^2,
\qquad
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z} = 2(\sigma(z)-y)\,\underbrace{\sigma(z)\big(1-\sigma(z)\big)}_{\sigma'(z)}
$$

The extra factor $\sigma'$ vanishes exponentially in $|z|$. For $y = 1$ and a
confidently wrong prediction:

| $z$ | $\sigma(z)$ | CE gradient | MSE gradient | ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $-2$ | $1.2\times10^{-1}$ | $-0.881$ | $-1.9\times10^{-1}$ | 4.8x |
| $-4$ | $1.8\times10^{-2}$ | $-0.982$ | $-3.5\times10^{-2}$ | 28x |
| $-6$ | $2.5\times10^{-3}$ | $-0.998$ | $-4.9\times10^{-3}$ | 203x |
| $-10$ | $4.5\times10^{-5}$ | $-1.000$ | $-9.1\times10^{-5}$ | 11,014x |

**The direction of the failure is what disqualifies it.** Squared error corrects
a confidently wrong prediction *least*, and the effect grows with how wrong the
model is. A model that has committed to a wrong answer is nearly stuck.

</Derivation>

## Numerical stability

<Derivation title="Log-sum-exp, and why it is free">

The softmax is invariant to a constant shift of all logits:

$$
\frac{e^{z_i - c}}{\sum_j e^{z_j - c}} = \frac{e^{-c}e^{z_i}}{e^{-c}\sum_j e^{z_j}} = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}
$$

so subtracting any $c$ costs nothing. Taking $c = \max_j z_j$:

$$
\log\sum_j e^{z_j} = c + \log\sum_j e^{z_j - c}
$$

Every exponent is now at most zero, so each term is in $(0,1]$ and overflow is
impossible; the largest is exactly 1, so the sum cannot underflow either.

**The fused loss.** Combining with the definition,

$$
\mathcal{L} = -z_{y} + \log\sum_j e^{z_j} = -z_{y} + c + \log\sum_j e^{z_j - c}
$$

which never forms $p$ at all.

**Practical consequence.** A numerical failure in a softmax is always an
implementation error, never an inherent difficulty — and passing already-softmaxed
probabilities into a function expecting logits both double-applies the softmax
and discards this stabilisation.

</Derivation>

## Label smoothing

Replace the one-hot target with

$$
q_i = (1-\varepsilon)\,\delta_{i,y^\star} + \frac{\varepsilon}{K}
$$

<Derivation title="Why the optimum moves off the boundary">

**Without smoothing.** $\mathcal{L} = -\log p_{y^\star}$ decreases monotonically
as $p_{y^\star} \to 1$, which requires $z_{y^\star} - z_j \to \infty$. The
infimum is not attained: the optimum is at the boundary, so a well-fitted model
keeps pushing logits apart without limit.

**That is the overconfidence mechanism.** Nothing in the objective ever says
"confident enough".

**With smoothing.** Set $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z = p - q = 0$, so the
optimum is $p = q$ exactly. For the correct class and any other:

$$
\frac{p_{y^\star}}{p_j} = \frac{1-\varepsilon + \varepsilon/K}{\varepsilon/K}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
z_{y^\star} - z_j = \log\frac{(1-\varepsilon)(K-1)}{\varepsilon}
$$

approximating for small $\varepsilon$. A **finite** optimal gap — at
$\varepsilon = 0.1$ and $K = 1000$, about $\log 8991 \approx 9.1$.

**Both observed effects follow.** Calibration improves because logits stop
growing without bound. And representations form tighter class clusters, because
every example of a class is pulled toward the *same* finite logit configuration
rather than being pushed outward indefinitely — which is also why smoothing hurts
distillation, since the compressed clusters carry less of the inter-class
similarity information a student learns from.

</Derivation>

## Imbalance

With $99\%$ negatives, the summed loss is dominated by easy examples. Two
standard responses, targeting different things.

<Steps>
<Step title="Class weighting">
$\mathcal{L} = -\sum_i w_{y_i}\log p_{y_i}$ with $w$ inversely proportional to
frequency. Rebalances by *class*, and does nothing about easy versus hard within
a class.
</Step>

<Step title="Down-weighting confident examples">
Multiply each term by $(1 - p_{y})^\gamma$. An example already predicted at
$p = 0.99$ contributes $10^{-4}$ of its usual weight at $\gamma = 2$, while a
hard one at $p = 0.1$ keeps most of it. Rebalances by *difficulty*, which is
usually the more direct problem in dense detection.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
These are not interchangeable. Class weighting says "rare classes matter more";
difficulty weighting says "examples I already get right matter less". With
extreme imbalance the second is usually closer to the real problem, because the
overwhelming majority of easy negatives is what drowns the signal — and combining
both without thinking produces a loss dominated by rare *and* hard examples,
which is often just the label noise.
</Warning>

## Calibration

Minimising cross-entropy well does not produce calibrated probabilities. A
network can be highly accurate and systematically overconfident, and the gap
widened as networks grew.

$$
\text{ECE} = \sum_{b} \frac{|B_b|}{n}\,\Big|\operatorname{acc}(B_b) - \operatorname{conf}(B_b)\Big|
$$

bins predictions by confidence and measures the average gap between confidence
and accuracy within each bin.

<KeyIdea>
The standard fix is a single parameter: divide the logits by a temperature $T$
fitted on held-out data. It cannot change the argmax — dividing all logits by a
positive constant preserves their order — so accuracy is untouched and only the
probabilities move. That a one-parameter post-hoc rescaling fixes most of the
miscalibration says the problem is a systematic scale issue rather than a
per-example one, which is itself informative about what training does to logits.
</KeyIdea>
