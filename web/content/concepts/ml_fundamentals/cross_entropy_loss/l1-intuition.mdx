## It is maximum likelihood

For a model producing a distribution $p_\theta(y \mid x)$ over classes, the
cross-entropy loss on a labelled example is

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\log p_\theta(y^\star \mid x)
$$

Minimising it maximises the log-probability the model assigns to the label that
was actually observed. Nothing more exotic than that — it is maximum likelihood
with a sign flip.

<KeyIdea>
Written against the full label distribution, $H(q,p) = H(q) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$
— the data's own entropy plus the divergence from the model to it. Since the
entropy is a property of the labels and not of $\theta$, **minimising
cross-entropy is minimising KL divergence**. That is what connects classification
to density estimation, variational inference, and distillation, and it tells you
the loss has a floor: with genuinely ambiguous labels, it cannot go below $H(q)$.
</KeyIdea>

## The gradient is the whole story

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z} = p - y
$$

Prediction minus one-hot target. That is remarkably simple, and it is not an
accident: the softmax's derivative cancels *exactly* against the logarithm's.
Each is awkward alone, and composed they produce something bounded, zero when
correct, and maximal when maximally wrong.

<Steps>
<Step title="Cross-entropy through a softmax">
$\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z = p - y$. At a confidently wrong prediction,
$p \approx 0$ for the true class, so the gradient is $\approx -1$: full strength.
</Step>

<Step title="Squared error through a sigmoid">
$\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z = 2(\sigma(z)-y)\,\sigma'(z)$, and
$\sigma'(z) = \sigma(1-\sigma)$ vanishes exponentially as $|z|$ grows. At
$z = -10$ with $y = 1$ that is about $9\times10^{-5}$ — eleven thousand times
smaller.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The direction of the failure is what makes it disqualifying. Squared error
corrects a *confidently wrong* prediction least, which is the case you most need
corrected. A model that has committed to a wrong answer under squared error is
nearly stuck there — the gradient that would rescue it has been multiplied by a
saturated derivative.
</Warning>

<Intuition>
Two teachers marking a confidently wrong answer. One says "no, the answer is
this" with the same force regardless of how sure you were. The other becomes
quieter the more confidently you were wrong — until, at total conviction, they
say nothing at all.
</Intuition>

## Why frameworks fuse it

You will see `cross_entropy(logits, target)` rather than
`nll_loss(log(softmax(logits)), target)`. Two reasons, both practical.

<Steps>
<Step title="Numerical stability">
Computing the softmax then its log means exponentiating logits that may be large
— overflowing — or taking the log of something that underflowed to zero. The
fused version uses the log-sum-exp identity to compute the same quantity without
ever forming the intermediate.
</Step>

<Step title="It computes $p - y$ directly">
The backward rule for the fused operation is the simple expression, rather than
composing two awkward ones and relying on the cancellation to happen numerically.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Where it needs help

<Steps>
<Step title="Class imbalance">
With 99% negatives, the loss is dominated by examples the model already gets
right. Reweighting by inverse frequency, or down-weighting easy examples by their
confidence, restores the signal from the rare class.
</Step>

<Step title="Overconfidence">
Cross-entropy is minimised by pushing the correct logit to infinity, so a model
that fits well becomes confidently wrong on the examples it gets wrong. Label
smoothing caps that by making the target distribution non-degenerate.
</Step>

<Step title="Calibration">
A well-trained modern network's confidence systematically exceeds its accuracy —
and the gap grew as networks got larger. If a downstream decision uses the
probability rather than the argmax, this matters, and a single temperature
parameter fitted on held-out data fixes most of it.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
That last one is the practically important caveat. Cross-entropy trains an
excellent *classifier* and does not by itself produce trustworthy
*probabilities*. If a robot decides whether to act on a perception module's
output, or an ensemble weights members by confidence, the calibration question is
the one that matters — and it is separate from accuracy.
</KeyIdea>
