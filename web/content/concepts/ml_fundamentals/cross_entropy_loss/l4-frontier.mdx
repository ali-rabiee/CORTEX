## A regulariser, and what it was actually doing

<PaperCard refKey="szegedy2016labelsmoothing" />

Label smoothing arrives as one item in an architecture paper, motivated by the
observation that a one-hot target can only be matched as the logits diverge — so
the model is being asked for something attainable only in a limit.

<KeyIdea>
Stated formally, the loss's infimum is not attained: the optimum is at the
boundary. Smoothing sets a non-degenerate target, so there is a *finite* optimal
logit gap of $\log\frac{(1-\varepsilon)(K-1)}{\varepsilon}$ — about 9.1 at
$\varepsilon = 0.1$ with 1000 classes. That is the whole mechanism, and both its
benefits and its costs follow from it.
</KeyIdea>

## Accuracy and calibration came apart

<PaperCard refKey="guo2017calibration" />

Modern networks are substantially more miscalibrated than earlier, less accurate
ones — confidence systematically exceeds accuracy, and the gap widened with
capacity.

<Warning>
This is more surprising than it sounds. Cross-entropy is a *proper scoring rule*:
a model minimising it on infinite data would be calibrated. So the divergence is
a finite-data and optimisation phenomenon rather than a property of the
objective, and it traces to the same boundary optimum — nothing in the loss ever
says "confident enough", so a model with the capacity to fit its training data
keeps pushing logits apart on the examples it already gets right.
</Warning>

The fix is one parameter. Divide logits by a temperature fitted on held-out data;
because that preserves their ordering, accuracy is untouched and only the spread
moves.

<Steps>
<Step title="What temperature scaling can do">
Correct a systematic, uniform over- or under-confidence. That it works so well
tells you the miscalibration is largely a global scale issue.
</Step>

<Step title="What it cannot">
Fix miscalibration that varies across the input space — a model overconfident on
one region and underconfident on another. One parameter cannot represent that,
and the residual after temperature scaling is where the harder problem lives.
</Step>

<Step title="Why it matters beyond benchmarks">
Any decision that consumes the probability rather than the argmax: a
perception module gating a robot action, an ensemble weighting members, a
threshold for abstention. Accuracy is silent about all of them.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Getting the imbalance axis right

<PaperCard refKey="lin2017focal" />

Dense detection has tens of thousands of background anchors per image, and almost
all of them are trivially classified. Each contributes a small loss; summed, they
overwhelm the foreground signal.

<KeyIdea>
Class weighting is the obvious response and the wrong axis. Up-weighting
foreground leaves the background terms as numerous as before, and their sum still
dominates. Down-weighting by $(1-p_t)^\gamma$ targets *easy* examples specifically
— at $\gamma = 2$ an example predicted at $p = 0.99$ contributes $10^{-4}$ of its
weight — while hard negatives near the decision boundary keep theirs. Diagnosing
whether an imbalance is by class or by difficulty is what decides between them.
</KeyIdea>

## One mechanism, two opposite effects

<PaperCard refKey="muller2019labelsmoothing" />

Label smoothing improves accuracy and calibration, and makes the model a *worse*
teacher for distillation. Both trace to the same cause.

<Steps>
<Step title="What it does to the representation">
Every example of a class is pulled toward the same finite logit configuration, so
penultimate-layer representations form tight, roughly equidistant clusters rather
than spreading.
</Step>

<Step title="Why calibration improves">
Logits stop growing without bound, so confidence is capped at something the data
supports.
</Step>

<Step title="Why distillation degrades">
The information a student learns from is precisely the *relative* similarity to
wrong classes — how much a particular dog looks like a cat. Tight equidistant
clusters discard that structure, so the teacher's soft targets carry less than
they would have.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
This is a good general lesson about defaults. Label smoothing is applied almost
universally because it improves the headline metric, and its cost only appears
when the model is used differently — as a teacher, or wherever the geometry of the
representation matters rather than the argmax. A technique's downsides are often
invisible in the metric it was adopted to improve.
</Warning>

## What to take away

<Steps>
<Step title="Settled">
The gradient identity and why softmax and cross-entropy are paired. Log-sum-exp
stabilisation. That cross-entropy is KL divergence up to a constant.
</Step>

<Step title="Well-characterised">
Overconfidence and its boundary-optimum mechanism; temperature scaling as a
cheap, accuracy-preserving fix; the difficulty-versus-class distinction in
imbalance.
</Step>

<Step title="Worth being careful about">
Label smoothing's cost to representation geometry, and the fact that
miscalibration varying across the input space is not addressed by a single
temperature — which is where the open problem sits.
</Step>
</Steps>
