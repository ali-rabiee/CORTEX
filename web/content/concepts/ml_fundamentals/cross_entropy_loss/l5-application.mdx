  keyPoints:
    - "Lead with $\\partial\\mathcal{L}/\\partial z = p - y$ and the cancellation that produces it — that is why softmax and cross-entropy are paired rather than chosen independently."
    - "Give the MSE comparison with numbers: at logit $-10$ for the true class, the gradient ratio is about 11,000, and the direction of the failure is what disqualifies squared error."
    - "Note it is maximum likelihood, and equals KL divergence up to the data's entropy — which also tells you the loss has a floor."
    - "Volunteer the caveat: minimising it produces an excellent classifier and *not* calibrated probabilities, and the two matter for different downstream uses."
interview:
  answer: >-
    Cross-entropy is maximum likelihood — minimising it maximises the
    log-probability the model assigns to the observed label. Written against a
    full target distribution it decomposes into the data's entropy plus the KL
    divergence from the model to the data, and since the entropy doesn't depend
    on the parameters, you're fitting by KL divergence with a constant absorbed.
    That also tells you the loss has a floor: with genuinely ambiguous labels it
    can't go below the data's own entropy. The derivation I'd lead with is the
    gradient. Differentiate through the softmax and you get a Jacobian with a
    factor of p-i in it; differentiate the log and you get one over p-i. They
    cancel exactly, and what's left is p minus y — prediction minus one-hot
    target. That cancellation is why softmax and cross-entropy are paired rather
    than chosen independently: each has a pathology the other removes. And it
    explains the practical question of why not squared error. Squared error
    through a sigmoid gives two times p minus y times sigma-prime, and
    sigma-prime vanishes exponentially when saturated. So for a confidently
    wrong prediction — true label one, logit minus ten — cross-entropy gives a
    gradient of magnitude one and squared error gives about nine times ten to
    the minus five. Eleven thousand times smaller. What disqualifies squared
    error isn't the size of the difference but its direction: it corrects a
    confidently wrong prediction *least*, which is exactly the case you most
    need corrected, so a model that's committed to a wrong answer is nearly
    stuck there. The caveat I'd volunteer is that cross-entropy trains an
    excellent classifier and does not by itself give you calibrated
    probabilities. The loss is minimised only as the correct logit goes to
    infinity — the optimum is at the boundary — so nothing ever says "confident
    enough", and modern networks are systematically overconfident with the gap
    growing as they got more accurate. If a downstream decision uses the
    probability rather than the argmax, that matters, and it's fixed by a single
    temperature fitted on held-out data, which can't change the argmax because
    dividing all logits by a positive constant preserves their ordering.
  followUps:
    - q: "How does label smoothing fit into that picture?"
      a: >-
        It moves the optimum off the boundary. Without smoothing the target is
        one-hot, which can only be matched as the logits diverge, so a
        well-fitted model keeps pushing them apart indefinitely. With smoothing
        the target is non-degenerate, and setting p equals q gives a finite
        optimal logit gap — log of one minus epsilon times K minus one, over
        epsilon, which is about 9.1 at epsilon 0.1 with a thousand classes. So
        calibration improves for a structural reason rather than empirically.
        The cost, which is less well known, is that it makes the model a worse
        distillation teacher: fixing a finite target means every example of a
        class is pulled to the same configuration, so representations form tight
        equidistant clusters and lose the inter-class similarity structure a
        student learns from. Same mechanism, opposite-signed effects — which is a
        good example of a default whose downside only appears when you use the
        model differently.
    - q: "You have 99% background in a detection problem. Class weights or focal loss?"
      a: >-
        Focal, usually, and the reason is what the imbalance actually consists
        of. Class weighting says rare classes matter more, and it leaves the
        background terms as numerous as before — tens of thousands of trivially
        classified anchors per image, each contributing a small loss that still
        sums to dominate. Focal loss down-weights by one minus p to the gamma,
        which targets *easy* examples specifically: at gamma two, something
        predicted at 0.99 contributes ten to the minus four of its weight, while
        a hard negative near the boundary keeps most of its. So the question I'd
        ask first is whether the imbalance is by class frequency or by
        difficulty, because they need different axes. And I'd be careful about
        combining both without thinking, since that produces a loss dominated by
        rare and hard examples, which in practice is often the label noise.
    - q: "How would you know whether your model's probabilities are trustworthy?"
      a: >-
        Measure expected calibration error, not accuracy. Bin the predictions by
        confidence and compare the accuracy within each bin against the average
        confidence in it — a well-trained modern network will typically be
        confident at 0.95 in a bin where it's right 0.85 of the time, and that
        gap is completely invisible in the accuracy number. Then fit a
        temperature on a validation split, never on test, and re-measure. What
        I'd also flag is what temperature scaling can't do: it's one global
        parameter, so it corrects a systematic uniform over-confidence and
        cannot fix miscalibration that varies across the input space. If the
        residual after scaling is still large, the problem is per-region and
        needs something else — which for a robot perception module gating an
        action is exactly the case you care about.
    - q: "Why do frameworks fuse softmax and cross-entropy into one function?"
      a: >-
        Two reasons and both are practical. Numerical stability: computing the
        softmax then its log means exponentiating logits that might overflow, or
        taking the log of something that underflowed to zero. The fused version
        uses log-sum-exp — subtract the row maximum, which is free because the
        softmax is shift-invariant — so every exponent is non-positive and
        nothing overflows. And the backward rule can be the simple p minus y
        directly rather than composing two awkward derivatives and relying on
        the cancellation to happen in floating point. The trap that follows is
        that the fused function takes logits, and passing softmax output into it
        applies the softmax twice — probabilities are valid softmax inputs so
        nothing errors, but logits confined to zero-one give a near-uniform
        distribution and the loss sits near log K barely moving. It trains
        badly and silently, which makes it expensive to find.
  traps:
    - "Saying cross-entropy is used 'because it works better' without the gradient argument. The $p - y$ cancellation and the saturation comparison are the content."
    - "Presenting the MSE comparison as being about magnitude. The disqualifying part is the direction: squared error corrects a confidently wrong prediction least."
    - "Claiming a well-trained network gives calibrated probabilities. Cross-entropy is a proper scoring rule and modern networks are still systematically overconfident."
    - "Passing probabilities to a function expecting logits. It double-applies the softmax and trains badly with no error."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Loss stuck near $\log K$ → probabilities passed where logits were expected">
$\log 1000 = 6.9$. The softmax gets applied twice, the distribution goes
near-uniform, and nothing errors.
</Step>

<Step title="Loss below $\log K$ at step zero → leakage or a shape bug">
A random model cannot beat chance. Check target alignment before anything else.
</Step>

<Step title="Accuracy fine, threshold-based decisions poor → calibration">
Measure ECE. Fit a temperature on held-out data; it cannot change the argmax.
</Step>

<Step title="Model ignores the rare class → wrong imbalance axis">
Class weighting for class frequency, difficulty down-weighting when easy
negatives dominate. Diagnose which before choosing.
</Step>

<Step title="Good teacher became a bad one after adding label smoothing → representation geometry">
Smoothing fixes a finite target, collapsing the inter-class similarity structure
distillation relies on. Expected, and it will not show in the teacher's accuracy.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The derivation carries this answer, so lead with it: the softmax's derivative
cancels the logarithm's, leaving prediction minus target. That single line
explains why the two are paired, why the gradient never saturates, and — by
contrast with squared error, which keeps the factor — why the alternative fails
in the specific direction that disqualifies it. Then volunteer the caveat that
minimising cross-entropy gives you an excellent classifier and not calibrated
probabilities. Candidates who add that unprompted are signalling they have used a
model's confidence for something downstream rather than only reported its
accuracy.
</Warning>
