## Equivariance, from the definition

Write a convolution with the weight indexed by the **offset** between input and
output positions:

$$
y[n] = \sum_{m} w[m]\; x[n + m]
$$

<Derivation title="Shifting the input shifts the output, exactly">

Let $x'[n] = x[n - \delta]$ be the input shifted by $\delta$. Then

$$
y'[n] = \sum_m w[m]\,x'[n+m] = \sum_m w[m]\,x[n + m - \delta] = y[n - \delta]
$$

**Two lines, and note what was used.** Only that $w$ is indexed by $m$ alone. The
kernel size is irrelevant — a $1\times1$ convolution is equally equivariant — and
so is the nonlinearity, since applying the same pointwise function everywhere
preserves the property.

**What breaks it.** A *locally connected* layer, which has a distinct weight per
position, looks similar and is not equivariant. Neither is anything that adds a
position-dependent term, such as a positional encoding. And padding breaks it at
the boundary, which is why the equivariance is exact in the interior and
approximate at the edges — a real effect for small feature maps.

</Derivation>

<Warning>
Equivariance is not invariance. Equivariance: shift the input, the output shifts.
Invariance: shift the input, the output is unchanged. Convolution gives the
first exactly; pooling gives an approximation of the second.

Which you want is a task property. Classification wants invariance. Detection,
segmentation, and any policy producing a spatial target want equivariance,
because *where* is the answer — and a global pooling layer in such a network
destroys exactly the information the task needs.
</Warning>

## The receptive field recursion

Two quantities propagate through a stack: the receptive field $r$, and the
**jump** $j$ — the distance in input pixels between adjacent output positions.

$$
j_\ell = j_{\ell-1}\, s_\ell,
\qquad
r_\ell = r_{\ell-1} + (k_\ell - 1)\, j_{\ell-1}
$$

with $r_0 = 1$, $j_0 = 1$.

<Derivation title="Why the growth rate changes with stride">

**Stride 1 everywhere.** Then $j_\ell = 1$ for all $\ell$, so each layer adds
$(k-1)$:

$$
r_L = 1 + L(k-1)
$$

For $3\times3$ that is $1 + 2L$ — **linear**. Ten layers see 21 pixels.

**With strides.** The jump accumulates multiplicatively, $j_\ell = \prod_{i\le\ell} s_i$,
so each layer extends the field by $(k-1)$ times the *current* jump. After three
stride-2 layers $j = 8$, and each subsequent $3\times3$ adds 16 rather than 2.

**With dilation $d_\ell$.** The effective kernel size becomes
$k_{\text{eff}} = d(k-1) + 1$, so

$$
r_\ell = r_{\ell-1} + d_\ell (k_\ell - 1)\, j_{\ell-1}
$$

Doubling $d$ each layer at stride 1 gives $r_L = 2^{L+1} - 1$ — **exponential**,
at full resolution. Eight layers reach 511 pixels.

| layers | stride 1 | stride 2 alternating | dilation doubling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 5 | 5 | 7 |
| 4 | 9 | 13 | 31 |
| 6 | 13 | 29 | 127 |
| 8 | 17 | 61 | 511 |
| 10 | 21 | 125 | 2047 |

**The practical consequence.** Compute $r_L$ for your own network and compare it
against the spatial extent of what the task requires. A network whose receptive
field is smaller than the structure it must recognise cannot do the task, and no
amount of training or capacity changes that.

</Derivation>

## Parameters and FLOPs

<Derivation title="Where the parameters are and where the compute goes">

A $k\times k$ convolution with $C_{\text{in}}$ input and $C_{\text{out}}$ output
channels:

$$
\text{params} = k^2\,C_{\text{in}}\,C_{\text{out}}
\qquad\text{(independent of } H, W)
$$

$$
\text{FLOPs} = 2\,H\,W\,k^2\,C_{\text{in}}\,C_{\text{out}}
$$

**Note the asymmetry.** Parameters do not depend on spatial size; compute scales
with it. So early layers — large $H\times W$, few channels — are cheap in
parameters and expensive in FLOPs, and late layers are the reverse. A parameter
count tells you almost nothing about where a convolutional network spends its
time.

**Against a dense layer.** Connecting every input activation to every output on a
$56\times56$ grid with 256 channels:

$$
(56 \cdot 56 \cdot 256)^2 \approx 6.4\times10^{11}
\quad\text{versus}\quad
3^2 \cdot 256^2 \approx 5.9\times10^{5}
$$

a factor of about $10^6$, bought entirely by the weight-sharing assumption.

**The separable variant.** Splitting into a depthwise $k\times k$ and a pointwise
$1\times1$ gives $k^2 C_{\text{in}} + C_{\text{in}}C_{\text{out}}$ instead of
$k^2C_{\text{in}}C_{\text{out}}$ — roughly $1/k^2$ of the cost at $C$ large,
about $9\times$ for $3\times3$. That factorisation is what makes mobile
architectures feasible, and it assumes the spatial and cross-channel structure
are approximately independent.

</Derivation>

## Why depth needed residuals

Stacking convolutions deeper eventually made networks *worse on training error* —
not an overfitting problem, since the deeper model could in principle represent
the shallower one plus identity layers.

$$
\text{deeper should be} \ \ge \ \text{shallower, by construction} \qquad \text{yet it was not}
$$

<KeyIdea>
That framing is the useful one: the deeper network was failing to find a solution
it demonstrably contained, so the problem was *optimisation*, not capacity or
generalisation. Residual connections make the identity the default — $F(x) + x$
rather than $F(x)$ — so a layer that should do nothing has only to learn zero.
And the gradient argument follows: $\partial(x + F(x))/\partial x = I + J$, so the
product across layers contains an identity term and no layer is cut off from the
loss.
</KeyIdea>

## Pooling, and what a policy needs instead

Global average pooling computes $\bar f_c = \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{ij} f_{cij}$ —
answering "is this feature present?" and deliberately discarding where.

The alternative for spatial tasks normalises each channel's activation map over
*space* and returns its expectation:

$$
s_{cij} = \frac{\exp(f_{cij}/\alpha)}{\sum_{i'j'}\exp(f_{ci'j'}/\alpha)},
\qquad
(x_c, y_c) = \sum_{ij} s_{cij}\,(x_{ij}, y_{ij})
$$

a differentiable keypoint detector — a soft argmax with a temperature, recovering
the hard argmax as $\alpha \to 0$.

<Warning>
Its assumption is that each channel's map is unimodal. Two equally strong
activations return the midpoint, which is a location with nothing at it — the
same mode-averaging pathology that appears in action regression, one layer lower
in the network. No downstream head can undo it, because the information left the
network at that layer.
</Warning>
