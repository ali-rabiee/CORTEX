## Small kernels, stacked

<PaperCard refKey="simonyan2015vgg" />

Three $3\times3$ layers have the same $7\times7$ receptive field as one large
kernel, use fewer parameters ($3\cdot 9 C^2 = 27C^2$ against $49C^2$), and
interpose two extra nonlinearities.

<KeyIdea>
This settled the kernel-size question for roughly a decade, and it is a clean
instance of a general move: replace one expressive operation with a composition
of cheaper ones that spans the same domain. The same argument appears in
depthwise-separable convolutions and in low-rank factorisations of dense layers.
</KeyIdea>

## The depth problem, correctly diagnosed

<PaperCard refKey="he2016resnet" />

Adding layers eventually made networks worse — and crucially, worse on *training*
error.

<Warning>
That single observation rules out overfitting, which by definition means fitting
the training data too well. And the constructive argument closes it: a deeper
network can represent a shallower one exactly by setting the extra layers to
identity, so a solution at least as good provably exists in the hypothesis class.
The optimiser was failing to find something it could represent.

Diagnosing it as an *optimisation* problem is what dictated the fix. Make the
identity the default — $F(x) + x$ — so a layer that should do nothing has only to
learn zero, rather than having to learn to reproduce its input exactly.
</Warning>

The gradient consequence is the same one that recurs throughout this material:
$\partial(x + F(x))/\partial x = I + J$, so the product across layers contains an
identity term and no layer is cut off from the loss.

## Context without losing resolution

<PaperCard refKey="yu2016dilated" />

Striding grows the receptive field geometrically and destroys resolution, which
is a genuine conflict for dense prediction — segmentation needs both wide context
and precise boundaries.

Dilation resolves it: insert gaps between kernel taps, double the gap each layer,
and the field grows as $2^{L+1}-1$ while every layer stays at full resolution.
Eight layers reach 511 pixels.

<KeyIdea>
Worth carrying because it identifies the constraint being traded. Striding buys
context with resolution; dilation buys it with *sampling density* — the kernel
taps become sparse, so fine texture between them is not seen at that layer. Which
is the right trade depends on whether the task needs high-frequency detail or
wide context, and stating it that way is more useful than treating dilation as a
free upgrade.
</KeyIdea>

## The collision

<PaperCard refKey="dosovitskiy2021vit" />

Cut the image into patches, treat them as tokens, discard translation
equivariance entirely. It loses to convolutions at ImageNet scale and wins with
much larger pretraining.

<Steps>
<Step title="Why the crossover exists">
A built-in inductive bias is structure you did not have to learn from data. When
data is scarce that is a large advantage; when it is plentiful, a model that
learns its own version of the structure fits better than the imposed one.
</Step>

<Step title="Why it is the useful form of the result">
It converts "which architecture is better" into "how much data do I have", which
is a question with an answer. For a robot policy on a few hundred demonstrations
the answer is emphatic.
</Step>

<Step title="The exception">
Wanting to inherit pretrained weights. That is not an argument about inductive
bias — it is a statement that someone else already paid the data cost, and it is
usually decisive when it applies.
</Step>
</Steps>

## The control experiment

<PaperCard refKey="liu2022convnext" />

Transformers arrived alongside a substantially different training recipe — heavier
augmentation, different optimiser and schedule, different normalisation. So the
architecture and the recipe changed together, and the comparison was confounded.

ConvNeXt modernises a ResNet step by step with those choices, and recovers most
of the reported gap.

<Warning>
The conclusion is not that convolutions are better. It is that the earlier
comparison did not isolate what it claimed to, which is a more careful and more
transferable finding — and the same methodological point that appears in
evaluation, in data curation, and in the optimiser literature: hold the resource
fixed and vary only the thing you are studying.

The residual disagreement after the control is much smaller than the original
claims, and it is about long-range interaction, where attention's global receptive
field in one layer is a genuine structural difference rather than a recipe
artefact.
</Warning>

## Where this leaves the choice

<Steps>
<Step title="Small data, spatial output, fixed sensor">
Convolutional trunk. The prior is free and correct, and pooling before any
sequence model cuts the token count attention pays for quadratically.
</Step>

<Step title="Large-scale pretraining available">
Whatever the pretrained backbone is. The data question has already been answered
by whoever trained it.
</Step>

<Step title="Long-range interaction is the task">
Attention, or dilation if resolution matters. A plain convolutional stack grows
context linearly, and that is a hard constraint rather than a tuning matter.
</Step>
</Steps>
