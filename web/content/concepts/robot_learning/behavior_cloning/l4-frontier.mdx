## The idea is 35 years old

<PaperCard refKey="pomerleau1989alvinn" />

ALVINN drove a van on public roads using a neural network with fewer parameters
than a single modern attention head. Camera in, steering out, trained on a human
driving. Every architectural detail has changed since; the framing has not.

## The diagnosis

<PaperCard refKey="ross2011dagger" />

This is the paper that turned "cloning is brittle" from folklore into a theorem,
and gave the standard fix. DAgger's loop: roll out the current policy, ask the
expert what *they* would have done at every state the learner visited, add those
labels, retrain, repeat.

The catch is the one interviewers press on. DAgger needs an expert you can query
**online**, at arbitrary states — including states that are already going wrong.
For a human teleoperator on a real robot, being asked "what would you do from
this half-fallen position?" is awkward at best and impossible at worst. That
practical difficulty is why the field's centre of gravity moved elsewhere.

## The two 2023 papers that changed practice

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="zhao2023act" />
<PaperCard refKey="chi2023diffusion" />
</PaperGrid>

Both keep the plain cloning objective and a fixed offline dataset. Neither
queries an expert online. They attack the *other* two weaknesses instead:

<Steps>
<Step title="Predict chunks, not single actions">
ACT predicts ~100 actions at once. An episode that was 500 independent decisions
becomes 5. Since compounding error scales with the number of decision points,
this is a large constant-factor win for almost no cost.
</Step>

<Step title="Represent the action distribution honestly">
Diffusion Policy's denoising head can put probability mass on "swerve left" and
"swerve right" without inventing "drive straight." That directly removes the
mode-averaging failure that MSE regression guarantees.
</Step>

<Step title="Ensemble across overlapping chunks">
ACT's temporal ensembling averages predictions for the same timestep made from
different observations, smoothing the seams where one chunk hands off to the
next — recovering some reactivity that pure open-loop execution gives up.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
The arc is worth being able to narrate in thirty seconds: 1989 established the
recipe, 2011 explained the failure and fixed the data, 2023 fixed the
representation instead and made it work on real hardware. Where a VLA like
$\pi_0$ or OpenVLA sits on that line — cloning at scale, with a pretrained
vision-language backbone — is a natural follow-up question.
</KeyIdea>
