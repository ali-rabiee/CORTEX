interview:
  answer: >-
    Behaviour cloning is imitation learning reduced to supervised learning. You
    record an expert doing the task, break the trajectories into observation
    and action pairs, and fit a policy to predict the action. No reward
    function, no exploration, no environment interaction — which is exactly why
    it's the default on real robots, where all three of those are expensive or
    dangerous. The catch is that supervised learning assumes your test
    distribution matches your training distribution, and a policy that acts
    violates that assumption by construction. It generates its own inputs, so
    the moment it makes a small error it's in a state the expert never visited
    and has no data for, and the next error is larger. Ross and Bagnell
    formalised that in the DAgger paper — regret scales as epsilon times T
    squared rather than epsilon times T, because one mistake can corrupt every
    remaining timestep. There's a second, independent problem people often
    miss: if the expert is multi-modal — sometimes going left, sometimes right
    — an MSE-trained policy fits the conditional mean and drives straight into
    the obstacle. Those two failures need different fixes. Covariate shift is a
    data problem, so DAgger changes where the data comes from. Mode averaging
    is a representation problem, so diffusion policies and action
    discretisation change how the policy models the action distribution. What
    made cloning genuinely work in the last few years was mostly the second
    route plus action chunking — ACT and Diffusion Policy get dexterous
    real-world manipulation from about fifty demonstrations, without ever
    querying an expert online.
  followUps:
    - q: "So why not just always use DAgger?"
      a: >-
        Because it needs an expert you can query online, at any state the
        learner reaches — including ones that are already going wrong. Asking a
        human teleoperator "what would you do from this half-dropped grasp?" is
        awkward, slow, and sometimes impossible. It's clean in simulation with
        a scripted expert. On real hardware with a human, the cost per label is
        high enough that people generally prefer to collect more demonstrations
        or improve the policy class instead.
    - q: "Your validation loss is very low but the robot fails. What's happening?"
      a: >-
        Almost always one of two things. Either the validation split was made
        per-timestep rather than per-episode, so near-duplicate consecutive
        frames appear in both halves and the number is meaningless. Or the
        split is honest and the number is simply measuring the wrong thing —
        accuracy on the expert's state distribution, which is the distribution
        the policy leaves as soon as it acts. Low action error and task success
        are only loosely related; the only trustworthy metric is rollout
        success rate.
    - q: "How would you get more out of a fixed dataset, with no extra demos?"
      a: >-
        Action chunking first — it's nearly free and directly reduces the
        number of decision points that compounding error scales with. Then an
        expressive action head, diffusion or discretised tokens, if there's any
        multi-modality. Then observation augmentation, random crops and colour
        jitter, which buys robustness to the visual shift you'll actually see.
        If I can perturb during collection, adding small disturbances and
        recording the expert's corrections gives recovery data without needing
        an online expert.
    - q: "When is behaviour cloning the wrong choice entirely?"
      a: >-
        When you have no expert, when the task needs behaviour a human can't
        demonstrate — anything beyond human reaction time or precision — or
        when you need to exceed demonstrator performance rather than match it.
        Cloning has no mechanism to be better than its data. If you have a
        reliable reward and a usable simulator, RL can surpass the expert;
        cloning structurally cannot.

## Reading the symptoms

Interviewers rarely ask "what is behaviour cloning?" They describe a broken
system and watch how you narrow it down.

<Steps>
<Step title="Does it fail at a specific state, or progressively?">
A repeatable failure at one decision point, with a smooth in-between action,
means mode averaging. Errors that start small and grow over the episode mean
compounding error. These have different fixes and confusing them is the most
common mistake.
</Step>

<Step title="Check how the validation split was made">
Per-timestep splits leak near-identical neighbouring frames across the split.
If validation loss looks great and the robot doesn't work, suspect this before
anything else.
</Step>

<Step title="Compare open-loop replay against closed-loop rollout">
Replay the recorded observations through the policy and compare to the expert's
actions. Low error there but failure on the robot isolates the problem to
distribution shift rather than the fit itself.
</Step>

<Step title="Only then reach for architecture">
Chunking, then an expressive action head, then augmentation. Reaching for a
bigger backbone first is the answer that signals you haven't diagnosed anything.
</Step>
</Steps>

<InterviewAnswer />

<Warning>
The single strongest move in this answer is separating the two failure modes and
matching each to its own fix. Most candidates produce one undifferentiated "BC
is brittle because of distribution shift." Naming compounding error *and* mode
averaging, and knowing that DAgger addresses only the first, is what reads as
having actually built these systems.
</Warning>
