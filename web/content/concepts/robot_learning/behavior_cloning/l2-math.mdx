## The objective

Behaviour cloning is maximum likelihood on a fixed dataset. Given demonstrations
$\mathcal{D} = \{(o_i, a_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from the expert's own state
distribution $d^{\pi^*}$:

$$
\theta^* = \arg\max_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{(o,a) \sim \mathcal{D}}
\big[\log \pi_\theta(a \mid o)\big]
$$

Everything else is a modelling choice about $\pi_\theta$:

<Steps>
<Step title="Gaussian policy with fixed variance → MSE">
Model $\pi_\theta(a \mid o) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(o), \sigma^2 I)$ with
$\sigma$ constant. Then $\log \pi_\theta(a\mid o) = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|a -
\mu_\theta(o)\|^2 + \text{const}$, and maximising it is exactly minimising
$\|a - \mu_\theta(o)\|^2$. This is why "BC" and "MSE regression on actions" get
used interchangeably.
</Step>

<Step title="Discretised actions → cross-entropy">
Bin each action dimension into $K$ tokens and predict a categorical
distribution. The loss becomes ordinary cross-entropy, and — importantly — the
policy can now represent more than one good action per observation.
</Step>

<Step title="Anything expressive → the modern versions">
Mixture density networks, normalising flows, and diffusion heads all keep the
same objective and only change how $\pi_\theta(a \mid o)$ is parameterised.
Diffusion Policy is this objective with a denoising head bolted on.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
The objective never changes. What changes across a decade of papers is how
richly you can represent $p(a \mid o)$ — and how many actions you predict at
once.
</KeyIdea>

## Where the extra factor of $T$ comes from

This is the result to be able to reproduce on a whiteboard. Let $\epsilon$ be
the probability that $\pi_\theta$ takes a different action from the expert, on
states drawn from the expert's distribution:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{o \sim d^{\pi^*}}\big[\mathbb{1}\{\pi_\theta(o) \neq \pi^*(o)\}\big]
\;\le\; \epsilon
$$

<Derivation title="From per-step error to $O(\epsilon T^2)$ regret">

Split the rollout on whether the learner has deviated yet.

**Case 1 — it never deviates.** This happens with probability at least
$(1-\epsilon)^T$. The learner's trajectory is distributed exactly like the
expert's, so it incurs no extra cost.

**Case 2 — it first deviates at step $t$.** Up to $t$ it was on-distribution, so
the deviation probability at each step was bounded by $\epsilon$. After step $t$
the learner is off the expert's distribution, and we have *no guarantee at all*
about its behaviour there — the bound assumes the worst, that it incurs maximum
cost at every one of the remaining $T - t$ steps.

Weighting each first-deviation time by its probability $\approx \epsilon$ and
summing the damage:

$$
\text{regret} \;\lesssim\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} \epsilon \cdot (T - t)
\;=\; \epsilon \cdot \frac{T(T-1)}{2} \;=\; O(\epsilon T^2)
$$

</Derivation>

The single assumption doing all the work is *"after deviating we know nothing"*.
That is not pessimism for its own sake — it is literally true, because the
dataset contains no states off the expert's path.

<Warning>
Don't say the bound is $T^2$ "because errors accumulate linearly and then you
sum them." Interviewers probe this. The first $T$ is the horizon; the second $T$
is that **each** mistake can ruin **all** remaining steps, because there is no
data teaching recovery. DAgger's contribution is to change the training
distribution so the bound becomes $O(\epsilon T)$.
</Warning>

## The multi-modality trap

Suppose at observation $o$ the expert goes left half the time and right half the
time, both perfectly good. MSE regression minimises

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[\|a - \mu_\theta(o)\|^2\big]
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mu_\theta(o) = \mathbb{E}[a \mid o]
$$

The minimiser is the conditional **mean** — the average of "swerve left" and
"swerve right", which is "drive straight ahead into the obstacle." The policy
has minimised its training loss by producing an action that appears nowhere in
the data and is worse than every action in it.

<KeyIdea>
Two independent failure modes, often confused in interviews. **Compounding
error** is about the state distribution and is fixed by changing where your data
comes from. **Mode averaging** is about the action distribution and is fixed by
changing how your policy represents it. Naming both, separately, reads as real
understanding.
</KeyIdea>
