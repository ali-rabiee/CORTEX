## The whole idea in one line

Record an expert doing the task. Then train a network to guess what the expert
would have done, using nothing but what the robot can see.

That's it. There's no reward, no exploration, no environment interaction. If you
can do supervised image classification, you can do behaviour cloning — swap the
label "cat" for the label "move the gripper 2 cm left."

<Intuition>
You want to learn to drive, so you watch a hundred hours of dashcam footage from
a good driver. At every frame you ask: *what did they do with the wheel here?*
Then you practice matching that.

You will be an excellent driver — on exactly the roads in the footage, in
exactly the situations the good driver got into.
</Intuition>

## How it actually runs

<Steps>
<Step title="Collect demonstrations">
A human teleoperates the robot through the task, 50–200 times. You record, at
every timestep, what the robot saw (camera images, joint angles) and what the
human did (the commanded action).
</Step>

<Step title="Throw away the time dimension">
Chop the trajectories into independent $(\text{observation}, \text{action})$
pairs and shuffle them. This is the step that makes it supervised learning — and
also the step that causes all the trouble later.
</Step>

<Step title="Fit a network">
Train $\pi_\theta(o) \rightarrow a$ to minimise the difference between its
predicted action and the human's action. Mean squared error for continuous
actions, cross-entropy if you discretised them. This is a completely ordinary
training loop.
</Step>

<Step title="Run it on the robot">
Feed live observations in, send predicted actions out, 10–50 times a second.
Nothing is learning any more — the policy is frozen.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Why it drifts

Here is the part that trips people up, and the part interviewers ask about.

Your policy is *good*. Say it gets each action 99% right. The 1% error nudges
the robot slightly off the path the expert took. Fine — except the robot is now
somewhere slightly unfamiliar, and on unfamiliar input the policy is worse than
99%. So the next error is bigger, which puts it somewhere *more* unfamiliar.

<Figure src="/figures/bc-compounding-error.svg"
        alt="An expert trajectory and a cloned policy's trajectory starting from the same point; the gap between them grows at every step until the learner leaves the region entirely."
        caption="Errors don't cancel out — they feed each other. The learner creates its own input distribution, and it drifts away from the one it was trained on." />

<KeyIdea>
Supervised learning assumes your test data looks like your training data. A
robot policy *creates* its own test data by acting. The moment it makes a
mistake, that assumption is broken — and it broke it itself.
</KeyIdea>

The expert never demonstrates recovery, because a good expert never needs to
recover. So the one behaviour your policy most needs is the one behaviour
guaranteed to be missing from your dataset.

<Warning>
"Just collect more data" does not fix this. More expert data makes the good
states even better covered, and the states you actually fail in are still absent.
You need data from a *different distribution* — either the learner's own states
(DAgger), or deliberately perturbed demonstrations.
</Warning>

## So why is everyone still using it?

Because in practice it works far better than the theory suggests, once you add
a few things: many demonstrations, action chunking (predict the next $k$ actions
instead of one), and expressive policies that can represent multi-modal action
distributions. ALOHA and Diffusion Policy are both, underneath, behaviour
cloning — and they do laundry-folding and coffee-making on real hardware.

The pessimism above is about *naive* cloning. Knowing exactly where the naive
version breaks is what lets you explain why the modern versions are built the
way they are.
