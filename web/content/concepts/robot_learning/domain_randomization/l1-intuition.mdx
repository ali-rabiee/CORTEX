## Reality as one more sample

The obvious response to a simulator that does not match reality is to make it
match better. Domain randomisation does the opposite: it makes the simulator
match *worse*, on purpose, in every direction at once.

Sample the physics parameters — masses, frictions, latencies, motor gains — and
the visual parameters — textures, lighting, camera pose — afresh at every
episode. Train across all of it. If reality falls inside the distribution you
sampled from, then deploying on the real robot is simply one more draw from the
training set.

<Intuition>
Learn to cook in a thousand different kitchens: different stoves, different pans,
different hob temperatures, different knives. You will be slower in any specific
kitchen than someone who has cooked in that one kitchen for a decade.

But drop you in a kitchen neither of you has seen and you will make dinner,
because you never learned to depend on a particular stove. The specialist has
learned things that are true only of their kitchen and cannot tell which ones
those are.
</Intuition>

<KeyIdea>
The reframing is the point. "Is my simulator accurate?" is a question you cannot
answer, because answering it requires knowing the true parameters. "Does my
policy generalise across this distribution?" is an ordinary machine-learning
question — you can test it, hold out ranges, and recognise what failure looks
like. Randomisation trades an unbounded modelling problem for a familiar one.
</KeyIdea>

## What it costs

<Figure src="/figures/dr-support-tradeoff.svg"
        alt="Three performance curves over a parameter axis: a tall narrow one that misses the real robot's parameter, a moderate one that covers it, and a very wide flat one that falls below the threshold needed to do the task."
        caption="One policy has to serve the whole support, so widening it strictly lowers the ceiling. Past some width, no single strategy works for every sampled environment and the task stops being learnable at all." />

This is not a tuning artefact, it is what the objective says. You are optimising
the average return across the distribution using one set of weights, so
environments that demand incompatible strategies cannot both be served well.

<Warning>
"Calibrate what you can measure, randomise what you cannot, and no wider than
plausible." Every parameter you pin down by measurement is one you no longer
have to be robust to, and therefore performance you get back. If you find
yourself needing extreme ranges to transfer, read that as evidence you need an
*adaptive* policy — one that infers the environment from recent history — rather
than a more robust one.
</Warning>

## Two kinds, with very different difficulty

<Steps>
<Step title="Visual randomisation — cheap and effective">
Textures, lighting, camera pose, distractor objects, colour jitter, noise. You
can generate unlimited variation, and the effect is to make appearance
unreliable so the network keys on geometry and relative position instead. This
works well enough that deliberately non-photorealistic training renders transfer
to real cameras.
</Step>

<Step title="Dynamics randomisation — where the difficulty is">
Masses, inertias, friction coefficients, actuator gains, latency, external
disturbances. Harder for a structural reason: the gap is often an effect the
simulator has *no parameter for*. No distribution over masses produces gearbox
backlash. Randomising other parameters harder buys robustness to variation that
was not the problem.
</Step>

<Step title="Observation randomisation — cheap and neglected">
Sensor noise, calibration bias, dropped frames, delayed observations. Often the
single highest-return thing to add, and almost free. Note the distinction that
matters: a calibration bias persists for a whole run, while thermal noise does
not, so they should be sampled at different rates.
</Step>
</Steps>

## The natural next question

If narrow ranges do not cover reality and wide ranges make the task unlearnable,
what do you do? Two answers, and both are live.

**Automate the range selection.** Start narrow, and expand each range whenever
the policy performs well at its current boundary. The distribution grows exactly
as fast as the policy can absorb it, which is a curriculum rather than a fixed
choice — this is what Automatic Domain Randomisation does.

**Stop asking the policy to be robust.** Give it enough observation history to
work out which environment it is in, and let it act as a specialist for that
environment. This is strictly more powerful than robustness and it is what makes
a legged robot recover within a step of walking onto oil. Level 2 shows why it
is a different problem class, not just a better architecture.
