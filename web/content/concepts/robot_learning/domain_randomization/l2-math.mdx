## The objective

Parameterise the simulator by $\xi \in \Xi$ and put a distribution on it:

$$
\max_\theta\; \mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\xi \sim p(\xi)}
\Big[\, J_{\mathcal{M}_\xi}(\pi_\theta) \,\Big]
$$

One set of weights, an average over environments. Every property of the method
follows from those two facts.

<Derivation title="Why widening the support lowers the ceiling">

Compare against the fantasy in which you could deploy a different policy in each
environment. For any fixed $\theta$ and any $\xi$,

$$
J_\xi(\pi_\theta) \;\le\; \max_{\theta'} J_\xi(\pi_{\theta'})
$$

pointwise. Take the expectation over $\xi$ on both sides — the inequality is
preserved — and then maximise the left over $\theta$:

$$
\max_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{\xi}\big[J_\xi(\pi_\theta)\big]
\;\le\;
\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\Big[\max_{\theta'} J_\xi(\pi_{\theta'})\Big]
$$

The gap between the two sides is precisely **the price of using one policy for
the whole family**. It vanishes only when a single policy is simultaneously
optimal everywhere in the support.

**Now widen the support.** Adding environments to $\Xi$ adds terms the single
policy must compromise on, so the gap is non-decreasing in the support. Once the
support contains environments requiring genuinely incompatible strategies — a
gait that works on ice and one that works on gravel — the compromise can fall
below the threshold for doing the task at all.

**The assumption doing the work is that $\theta$ is shared and $\xi$ is
unobserved.** Relax either and the argument changes, which is exactly what the
adaptive formulation does.

</Derivation>

## Three formulations, three different ceilings

<Steps>
<Step title="Randomised (expectation)">
$\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim p}[J_\xi(\pi_\theta)]$. Optimises the average.
Tolerates poor performance on a small-probability region of $\Xi$, which is
usually what you want — but note that "small probability under $p$" is a
statement about *your chosen* $p$, not about reality.
</Step>

<Step title="Robust (minimax)">
$\max_\theta \min_{\xi\in\Xi} J_\xi(\pi_\theta)$. Gives a worst-case guarantee
and is dominated by the least favourable $\xi$ you allowed, so a single
implausible corner of the parameter space sets your whole performance. Almost
always too conservative for robotics; the useful middle ground is a CVaR
objective, optimising the average over the worst $\alpha$-fraction.
</Step>

<Step title="Adaptive (history-conditioned)">
$\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_\xi[J_\xi(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid h_t))]$ where
$h_t = (s_{t-k:t}, a_{t-k:t-1})$. A *different problem class*: the policy can
represent a different behaviour per inferred environment, so its ceiling
approaches the specialist value $\mathbb{E}_\xi[\max_\theta J_\xi]$ rather than
the compromise value.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
The three differ in what they optimise, not in how hard you tune them. The
expectation form buys average-case coverage; the minimax form buys a guarantee
you probably cannot afford; the adaptive form changes the ceiling itself. If a
randomised policy is transferring but sluggish, the answer is usually the third
row, not a wider $p$.
</KeyIdea>

## What history buys, precisely

With $\xi$ unobserved, the true state is $(s,\xi)$ and the agent sees $s$ — a
POMDP. Recent history is informative because the *response* to actions depends
on $\xi$: the same commanded torque yields different accelerations at different
effective masses.

$$
p(\xi \mid h_t) \;\propto\; p(\xi)\prod_{i} P_\xi\big(s_{i+1} \mid s_i, a_i\big)
$$

A policy conditioned on $h_t$ can in principle implement
$\pi(a \mid s, \hat\xi(h_t))$ — estimate, then act as a specialist.

<Warning>
The assumption doing the work is **identifiability**: the history has to contain
information about $\xi$. A parameter that only matters in situations the robot
has not yet encountered cannot be inferred before it matters, and a parameter
whose effect is masked by feedback control may never show up in the history at
all. This is why adaptation results are strongest in locomotion, where every
step is an informative excitation, and weaker in quasi-static manipulation where
the robot may simply not probe the dynamics.
</Warning>

## Choosing $p$ automatically

Fixing the ranges by hand trades two failure modes against each other. A
curriculum removes the choice.

$$
\text{if } \mathbb{E}\big[J_\xi\big] > \tau_{\text{high}} \text{ at the boundary of range } i:
\quad \text{widen range } i
$$
$$
\text{if } \mathbb{E}\big[J_\xi\big] < \tau_{\text{low}}:
\quad \text{narrow it}
$$

Two details make this work. **Sample at the boundaries**, because the question
being answered is "can the policy handle the hardest environment currently in
the support?", and uniform interior sampling dilutes that signal with cases the
policy already handles. And **expand one dimension at a time**, so the resulting
entropy of $p$ is a readable measure of how much variation the policy currently
tolerates — a progress metric that mean return does not give you.

<KeyIdea>
Automatic randomisation is a curriculum in disguise, and it is the practical
answer to "how wide should the ranges be?": as wide as the policy can currently
absorb, discovered rather than guessed. The residual judgement is where to
*start* and which parameters to include at all — a curriculum cannot invent a
dimension of variation you did not implement.
</KeyIdea>

## The limit that no distribution fixes

$$
\Xi \text{ parameterises the simulator's model class, not reality.}
$$

If the simulator has no representation of gearbox backlash, cable stiffness, or
deformable contact, then no $p(\xi)$ produces those behaviours, and widening the
ranges on the parameters you *do* have makes the policy conservative about the
wrong things. The formal statement is the same one as for system identification:
you are working inside a model class, and randomising over it cannot escape it.

The escape is to enlarge the class using real data — a learned actuator model
inserted into the simulator — or to make the policy adaptive so it can
compensate for what the simulator never told it about.
