## Two independent origins, one idea

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="sadeghi2017cad2rl" />
<PaperCard refKey="tobin2017dr" />
</PaperGrid>

Both make the same argument from different tasks. CAD2RL flies a real drone
through real corridors using a collision-avoidance policy trained purely on
randomised CAD renderings — no real images anywhere in training. Tobin et al.
localise objects accurately enough to grasp them, from renders with randomised
textures, lighting, camera poses, and distractors.

<KeyIdea>
The shared insight is that photorealism is the wrong target. Aiming at realism
means aiming at a single point in appearance space that you cannot verify you
have reached. Randomising makes appearance unreliable during training, so the
network is forced to key on geometry and relative position — and reality becomes
one more sample from a distribution it already handles.
</KeyIdea>

## The counterweight

<PaperCard refKey="tan2018agile" />

Worth reading immediately after, because it argues the opposite emphasis. Tan et
al. transferred agile quadruped gaits using an accurate actuator model and
*narrow* randomisation — and make the case that identifying the dynamics
properly is better than being robust to a wide range of wrong ones.

<Warning>
Both are right, and the reconciliation is the rule to carry: randomisation is a
way of covering uncertainty you could not remove. Every source you eliminate by
measurement or modelling is peak performance you keep, because wide ranges buy
robustness by giving it up. Model first, randomise the residual. A candidate who
only knows the randomisation papers will over-randomise; one who only knows this
one will spend months on a simulator instead.
</Warning>

## Randomise the physics, and notice adaptation

<PaperCard refKey="peng2018dynamics" />

Extending randomisation from appearance to dynamics produced the observation
that mattered most: a recurrent policy does not merely average over the sampled
physics, it *infers* them. The response to recent actions is a measurement of the
dynamics, so a policy with memory behaves as a specialist for whatever
environment it deduces it is in. Everything adaptive downstream — teacher-student
distillation, RMA — is a way of making that inference explicit and cheap.

## Learning the distribution instead of guessing it

<PaperCard refKey="chebotar2019simopt" />

The hand-tuning problem is that the ranges are arbitrary. SimOpt alternates
policy training with an update to $p(\xi)$ itself, chosen so that simulated
trajectory statistics match real rollouts — fitting a distribution rather than a
point estimate, which is exactly what randomisation needs and what system
identification does not provide.

<PaperCard refKey="openai2019rubik" />

Automatic Domain Randomisation removes the choice a different way, without real
data. Sample at the boundary of one range at a time; expand it when the policy
handles that boundary and contract when it does not. The support becomes an
*output* of training rather than an input, and its entropy is a progress metric
that mean return cannot give you — because the curriculum keeps return pinned
between its thresholds by construction.

<Steps>
<Step title="What ADR gets right">
It answers "how wide should the ranges be?" with "as wide as the policy can
currently absorb", discovered rather than guessed, and it never pays the
over-randomisation cost up front.
</Step>

<Step title="What it does not answer">
Which dimensions to randomise at all, and where to start. A curriculum cannot
invent a dimension of variation you did not implement — if your simulator has no
latency model, ADR will never discover that latency was the gap.
</Step>

<Step title="The cost, stated honestly">
The Rubik's-cube result required a very large amount of compute, and boundary
sampling means a substantial fraction of episodes are spent in environments
harder than the ones you deploy in. It buys robustness with simulation time.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The whole line has a ceiling worth naming. $\Xi$ parameterises the *simulator's
model class*, not reality. No distribution over masses and frictions produces
gearbox backlash if the simulator has no notion of it, so a gap caused by
missing physics is invisible to every method on this page — ADR will simply
widen the wrong ranges forever. That is why the strongest recent results pair
randomisation with a learned dynamics component fitted on real data, and why
"what does my simulator not model?" is the more useful question than "are my
ranges wide enough?"
</Warning>
