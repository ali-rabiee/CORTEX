  keyPoints:

    - "The max-average inequality is the formal content: one policy for a whole family is strictly worse than a specialist per environment, and the gap grows with the support."

interview:
  answer: >-
    Domain randomisation is training across a distribution of simulators rather
    than one. You sample the physics and appearance parameters fresh each
    episode — masses, frictions, latencies, textures, lighting, camera pose —
    and optimise the expected return over that distribution. The idea is that if
    reality falls inside your support, then deployment is just one more sample
    from the training set. What I like about it as a framing is that it converts
    a question you can't answer into one you can. "Is my simulator accurate?"
    requires knowing the true parameters, which you don't. "Does my policy
    generalise across this distribution?" is an ordinary machine-learning
    question — you can hold out ranges, test outside the support, and recognise
    what failure looks like. The cost is peak performance, and it's structural
    rather than a tuning artefact. You're optimising the average over
    environments with one set of weights, and a max of an average is at most the
    average of maxima — the gap between them is exactly the price of using one
    policy where a specialist per environment would do better. Widening the
    support can only add terms the single policy has to compromise on, so more
    randomisation strictly lowers the ceiling, and past some point no single
    strategy works everywhere and the task stops being learnable. So the
    discipline is calibrate what you can measure, randomise what you can't, and
    no wider than plausible. Tan and colleagues' locomotion work is the
    counterweight to randomising harder — they modelled the actuators carefully
    and needed only narrow ranges, which is the general lesson: randomisation
    covers uncertainty you couldn't remove, so every source you eliminate by
    modelling is performance you keep. Two things I'd add. Whether the policy
    sees observation history changes what you're getting: memoryless, the
    physics parameters are a hidden variable, it's a POMDP, and the best policy
    hedges — that's robustness, and it's why a randomised policy often transfers
    but moves stiffly. With history, the policy can infer the environment from
    how the robot has been responding and act as a specialist, which raises the
    ceiling rather than just tuning it. And ADR removes the range-picking
    problem: sample at the boundary of one dimension at a time, expand when the
    policy handles it, contract when it doesn't. The support becomes an output
    of training. Worth knowing the diagnostic that goes with it — mean return
    stays flat by construction, so what you watch is the entropy of the
    distribution.
  followUps:
    - q: "Your policy still doesn't transfer after widening every range. What now?"
      a: >-
        I'd stop widening, because if wider ranges haven't helped, the gap is
        probably in a dimension I'm not randomising at all — and every extra bit
        of width is costing performance for nothing. The diagnostic I'd run is
        replay: take a logged real rollout, feed the identical actions to the
        simulator from the same initial state, and see where the trajectories
        diverge. If they diverge immediately on any commanded motion, it's
        latency or actuator dynamics. If only at contact, it's the contact
        model. That tells me whether I need a new randomisation dimension or a
        larger model class — a learned actuator network fitted on real data,
        say. The version of this that traps people is randomising the parameters
        you happen to have rather than the ones the gap is in.
    - q: "How do you pick the ranges initially?"
      a: >-
        Measure first. CAD masses, motor constants from the datasheet, control
        latency from a step-response test on the real robot — every parameter I
        pin down is one I don't have to be robust to. Then randomise
        multiplicatively around those nominals, so a range means plus or minus a
        percentage rather than an absolute band I retune per robot. I'd start
        dynamics tight and visuals wide, because they're asymmetric:
        over-randomising appearance costs almost nothing since the policy can
        just learn to ignore it, while over-randomising dynamics costs
        performance directly. And if I can afford the compute, ADR removes the
        starting-point problem for the widths — though not for which dimensions
        to include, which is still on me.
    - q: "Is domain randomisation still relevant given how good simulators have got?"
      a: >-
        For locomotion it's arguably more relevant, because GPU-parallel physics
        made the compute cheap enough that ADR-scale curricula are routine
        rather than a datacentre project. For contact-rich manipulation I'd be
        more sceptical, and I'd say why: the physics that matters there —
        deformation, stiction, precise contact geometry — is what simulators
        model worst, and randomising parameters can't cover an effect with no
        parameter. That asymmetry is a decent part of why the field's
        manipulation results lean on imitation from real demonstrations while
        its locomotion results lean on sim-to-real RL. Where I'd expect it to
        keep mattering in manipulation is for the visual side and for
        pretraining that gets fine-tuned on real data.
    - q: "Isn't this just data augmentation?"
      a: >-
        It's the same idea and the difference is worth naming. Image
        augmentation perturbs a fixed dataset to encode invariances you believe
        in — the label shouldn't change under a small crop. Domain randomisation
        perturbs the data-generating process, so it changes the *dynamics* the
        policy interacts with, not just its inputs. That has a consequence
        augmentation doesn't: the optimal behaviour genuinely differs across the
        randomisation, which is why it becomes a POMDP and why memory changes
        the ceiling. Visual randomisation really is augmentation and behaves
        like it; dynamics randomisation is a different animal.
  traps:
    - "Presenting wider ranges as always safer. The achievable optimum falls monotonically with the support, and past a point the task becomes unlearnable."
    - "Randomising the parameters you happen to have rather than the ones the gap is in. If the mismatch is unmodelled physics, no distribution over existing parameters covers it."
    - "Ignoring whether the policy has observation history. It decides whether you get robustness or adaptation, and it is the usual explanation for a policy that transfers but is sluggish."
    - "Reading a flat return curve in an ADR run as a stall. The curriculum pins return between its thresholds by design; the entropy of $p(\\xi)$ is the progress metric."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Transfers, but stiff and conservative → memoryless hedging">
The policy cannot tell which environment it is in, so it compromises. Add
observation and action history, or a teacher-student adaptation module. This
raises the ceiling; narrowing ranges only moves you back along the trade.
</Step>

<Step title="Widening ranges costs sim performance and buys no transfer → wrong dimension">
The gap is in something you are not randomising. Replay a real rollout through
the simulator and find where divergence starts before touching any range.
</Step>

<Step title="Task stops being learnable in sim → over-randomised">
The support now contains environments needing incompatible strategies. Narrow
it, calibrate more, or go adaptive.
</Step>

<Step title="Flat mean return in an ADR run → expected, check entropy instead">
The curriculum holds difficulty at the edge of ability by design. Log the
per-dimension range widths; a single stalled dimension is invisible in the
aggregate.
</Step>

<Step title="Adaptive architecture, no adaptive behaviour → check identifiability">
Feed the policy a zeroed or shuffled history and measure the drop. Near zero
means it is ignoring history. Then probe whether $\xi$ is predictable from the
latent at all — if the robot never excites the dynamics, there is nothing to
infer.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The move that distinguishes a strong answer is volunteering the cost before
being asked, and being able to say why it is structural — the max-average
inequality, one policy serving a family. Almost everyone can describe
randomisation; the informative question is what you would check *before*
widening a range, and the answer is what the gap actually is. If it is physics
with no parameter, no range covers it and every extra bit of width is
performance spent for nothing.
</Warning>
