  keyPoints:

    - "Visual overfitting is the dominant failure: the encoder keys on lighting, texture, or background that correlate with the action in your data. It shows as a cliff after a visual change, not a gradual decline."

interview:
  answer: >-
    A visuomotor policy is one network from pixels to motor commands, replacing
    the classical detect, estimate pose, plan grasp, execute pipeline. The
    argument for it isn't that neural detectors beat classical ones — it's
    about the interfaces. Every stage boundary in that pipeline is a
    hand-chosen summary, and whatever the summary doesn't carry is
    unrecoverable downstream. A six-DoF pose is a great interface for picking
    up a rigid box and a useless one for a towel or a cable, and no improvement
    to your pose estimator fixes that, because the failure is in the
    representation, not the estimate. Training end-to-end means the control
    loss decides what the visual features should be. Concretely, the standard
    build is a conv or ViT encoder per camera view, a spatial softmax rather
    than global average pooling so you keep position — the layer returns the
    expected image coordinates of each feature channel, which is a
    differentiable keypoint detector — then proprioception concatenated after
    pooling, then a head that predicts a chunk of future actions. Trained as
    behaviour cloning. What you give up is real: data efficiency, and the
    ability to ask which stage was wrong when it fails. And the failure that
    actually dominates is visual overfitting. Nothing in the loss says use the
    object and ignore the tablecloth; it says predict the action, and if the
    tablecloth predicts the action in your dataset the encoder will use it. So
    in practice most of the engineering is defensive — heavy crop and colour
    augmentation, group norm instead of batch norm, splitting validation by
    episode, and evaluating by deliberately changing something visual and
    rolling out, because held-out action error is measured in the same room and
    can't see the failure that will get you.
  followUps:
    - q: "Frozen pretrained encoder, or train from scratch?"
      a: >-
        It depends on the task, and I'd say so rather than pick a side —
        R3M and MVP argue one way and Hansen et al. showed a properly tuned
        from-scratch baseline is often competitive. The mechanism I'd reason
        from is that pretraining objectives reward invariance to small
        viewpoint and translation changes, and precise contact-rich tasks need
        exactly the opposite: the representation has to change sharply with a
        two-millimetre offset. So pretrained features help most with limited
        data and lots of visual and object diversity, and least on precise
        tasks in a fixed scene. It's cheap to run both compared to collecting
        more demos, so I'd measure rather than argue.
    - q: "How do you know whether your policy is actually using vision?"
      a: >-
        Ablate the input. Feed a blank or shuffled image at rollout time and
        see how far success drops — if it barely moves, the policy is running
        on proprioception and the vision pathway is decoration, which happens
        more often than people expect when the demonstrations have consistent
        starting configurations. The second thing I'd do is visualise the
        spatial-softmax keypoints on the frame, since they're literal image
        coordinates. If they track the object and the gripper, the encoder
        found the task; if they're pinned to a table corner, it found a
        shortcut. That's the one bit of interpretability an end-to-end policy
        gives you for free.
    - q: "You have 50 demos and it doesn't work. What do you do, in order?"
      a: >-
        Augmentation first, because it's free and it's the highest-leverage
        thing at that data scale — random crops shared across camera views,
        colour jitter, and check I'm not augmenting the validation split.
        Then action chunking, which cuts decision points and costs nothing.
        Then a pretrained frozen encoder, which is exactly the regime where it
        should help. Then I'd check whether the failure is really a vision
        problem at all — if the demos are multi-modal, no encoder fixes mode
        averaging in the head, and I'd want a diffusion or discretised action
        head instead. What I would not do first is make the backbone bigger.
    - q: "Where would a modular pipeline still be the right choice?"
      a: >-
        When the task genuinely decomposes and you need to certify it. If
        you're picking known rigid parts from a bin in a factory, pose
        estimation is an adequate interface, the modules can be tested and
        validated independently, and when something fails you can point at
        which stage. That inspectability is a real engineering asset and
        safety cases are much easier to write. I'd reach for end-to-end when
        the interface is the thing I can't specify — deformables, contact-rich
        assembly, anything where I can't write down what perception should
        hand to control.

    - "Forgetting that a single frame is not a state. Velocity, contact, and anything occluded are invisible in one image, and a policy with no observation history will fail on tasks that depend on them."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Cliff after a visual change → visual overfitting">
Success drops sharply and immediately when lighting, background, or camera pose
changes, and is stable otherwise. Confirm by restoring the original condition
and watching success return. This is the default hypothesis; make the evidence
argue you out of it.
</Step>

<Step title="Works only from the demonstrated start pose → the vision pathway is dead">
Blank the image and re-run. If success barely moves, the policy is regressing
actions from proprioception and never learned to look. Common when
demonstrations all begin from similar configurations.
</Step>

<Step title="Reaches confidently to the wrong place, repeatably → representation loss">
Overlay the spatial-softmax keypoints. Two identical objects in the scene will
put a keypoint between them, at nothing. So will any channel with a bimodal
activation map.
</Step>

<Step title="Fails only where demonstrations disagree → not a vision problem">
Mode averaging in the action head. The encoder is fine; the head is emitting the
mean of two valid strategies. No encoder change fixes this — the fix is a
multi-modal action representation.
</Step>

<Step title="Jitter or lag → neither">
Jitter is a per-timestep policy re-deciding every tick; chunk the actions. Lag
is looking at the world too rarely; execute fewer actions per re-plan.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The move that separates strong answers here is naming the cost of your own
position without being asked. End-to-end learning buys a representation shaped
by the task and pays for it in data, inspectability, and a strong tendency to
memorise the room. A candidate who only sells the benefit sounds like they have
read the 2016 paper. A candidate who volunteers the bill sounds like they have
deployed one.
</Warning>
