## The founding claim

<PaperCard refKey="levine2016visuomotor" />

The argument is that separating perception from control is a modelling error:
train them jointly and the visual features become the ones control actually
needs. The paper's lasting technical contribution is the spatial softmax, and
its most revealing detail is what it needed to make training work at all —
guided policy search, which supplies local trajectory-optimised controllers as
supervision so the network never has to solve the hard credit-assignment problem
from pixels directly.

<PaperCard refKey="finn2016spatialae" />

The companion result: those spatial feature points can be learned from a
reconstruction objective, with no action labels. That mattered because it
suggested the representation was a property of the images, not something only
the control loss could find.

## Scale, and what it cost

<PaperCard refKey="kalashnikov2018qtopt" />

Seven robots, months of wall-clock, 580k grasp attempts, and a closed-loop
policy that generalises to unseen objects and spontaneously regrasps when the
first attempt slips. It settled whether the approach scales. It also stated the
real problem clearly: almost nobody can afford that, and everything since has
been an attempt to get there for less.

## The deflation

<PaperCard refKey="kostrikov2021drq" />

By 2020 pixel-based control had accumulated a stack of auxiliary machinery —
reconstruction losses, contrastive objectives, world models. DrQ (with RAD
concurrently) showed that random shifts on the input recover most of the benefit
on standard benchmarks.

<KeyIdea>
The right reading is methodological. An encoder trained on a few thousand
correlated frames overfits severely, and much of what the auxiliary losses
bought was regularisation rather than representation. The lesson generalises far
beyond this paper: tune the simple baseline properly before crediting your
method, because in robot learning the simple baseline is usually undertuned.
</KeyIdea>

## The live argument

<PaperGrid>

<PaperCard refKey="radosavovic2022mvp" />

</PaperGrid>

The pretrained-representation position is that vision is a solved-enough problem
to be imported: freeze a backbone trained on human video, learn a small head,
and cut your demonstrations from thousands to tens. The counter-position, argued
directly against those results, is that a from-scratch encoder with ordinary
augmentation is often just as good, and the reported gains reflect a weak
baseline.

<Steps>
<Step title="Where pretrained features tend to win">
Limited data, broad visual variation, semantically-loaded tasks. If the policy
must handle objects it has never seen, features trained on internet-scale images
carry information your 200 demonstrations cannot.
</Step>

<Step title="Where they tend to lose">
Precise, contact-rich tasks in a fixed scene. Pretraining objectives explicitly
reward invariance to small translations and viewpoint shifts — and a peg-in-hole
policy needs the representation to change sharply with a two-millimetre offset.
The invariance you were given is the sensitivity you needed.
</Step>

<Step title="What to actually do">
Run both. The comparison is cheap relative to collecting more demonstrations,
and which side wins is a property of your task rather than of the year.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
This is a good interview question precisely because it has no settled answer.
Candidates who confidently assert one side are revealing which paper they read
most recently. Naming the invariance-versus-precision mechanism, and saying you
would measure it, is the answer that holds up.
</Warning>
