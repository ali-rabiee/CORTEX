## What it replaces

The classical way to make a robot pick something up is a relay race. A detector
finds the object. A pose estimator turns that into a 6-D transform. A grasp
planner turns the pose into a gripper pose. A motion planner turns that into a
trajectory. A controller tracks it.

A visuomotor policy is one network that takes the camera image and emits the
motor command. The relay race is replaced by a single differentiable function
trained on demonstrations.

<Intuition>
Nobody catches a ball by estimating its mass, solving a ballistic trajectory,
and computing an intercept. You look, you move, and the looking and the moving
were learned together — so tightly that you cannot separate them and report the
ball's position in metres.

That coupling is not sloppiness. It is what lets you catch a ball that is
spinning oddly, in a wind, against a background you have never seen.
</Intuition>

## Why the interfaces are the problem

<Figure src="/figures/visuomotor-pipeline-vs-end-to-end.svg"
        alt="Two rows. The top row shows a modular pipeline passing a bounding box, then a 6-D pose, then a grasp between four stages. The bottom row shows an image encoder passing a thick learned feature vector directly to an action head."
        caption="The modular pipeline is easy to debug precisely because a human chose what each stage may pass on. That same choice is what throws away everything the chooser did not anticipate." />

A 6-D pose is a superb interface for picking up a rigid box. It is a hopeless
one for folding a towel, routing a cable, or wiping a surface — objects with no
canonical pose, tasks where contact matters more than position. Improving the
pose estimator does not help, because the failure is in the *representation*,
not the estimate. End-to-end learning does not solve this so much as refuse to
commit to it in advance.

<KeyIdea>
The trade is real in both directions. You gain a representation shaped by the
task instead of by a human's guess about the task. You lose the ability to ask
"which stage was wrong?" — the thing that made modular systems debuggable — and
you take on a large appetite for data.
</KeyIdea>

## How one is actually built

<Steps>
<Step title="Encode the image, keeping spatial structure">
A conv net or ViT over each camera view. The detail that matters is the pooling:
a **spatial softmax** turns each feature channel's activation map into a
probability distribution over pixels and returns its expected $(x,y)$. You get
differentiable keypoints instead of the location-free vector that global average
pooling produces.
</Step>

<Step title="Concatenate proprioception">
Joint angles and gripper state, alongside the visual features. Cheap, exact, and
the policy will lean on it more than you expect.
</Step>

<Step title="Map to actions with a small head">
An MLP or transformer producing end-effector deltas, joint velocities, or — in
anything modern — a chunk of future actions rather than one.
</Step>

<Step title="Train it as behaviour cloning, and augment hard">
Random crops and colour jitter are not optional garnish here. They are the main
defence against the encoder solving your task by reading the tablecloth.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Held-out action-prediction error tells you almost nothing about whether this
will work. It is measured on images from the same room, the same lighting, and
the same cameras, so it cannot see the failure mode that will actually get you.
Evaluate by changing something visual and rolling out — new lighting, a moved
camera, a different table — and count successes.
</Warning>
