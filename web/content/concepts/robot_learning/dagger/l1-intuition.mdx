## The problem it exists to solve

Behaviour cloning trains on states the **expert** visited and is then tested on
states the **learner** visits. Those are different distributions, and the gap
grows every time the policy makes a mistake.

DAgger's answer is almost aggressively simple: *if the problem is that you have
no data where the learner goes, then go there and get some.*

<Intuition>
A driving instructor doesn't only sit in the passenger seat admiring their own
driving. They let you drive, watch where you drift, and tell you what they would
have done at the exact moments you got it wrong.

Behaviour cloning is watching the instructor drive. DAgger is driving while the
instructor narrates.
</Intuition>

## The loop

<Figure src="/figures/dagger-loop.svg"
        alt="A four-stage cycle: train the policy on the dataset, roll it out, have the expert label the visited states, aggregate into the dataset, and repeat."
        caption="Each pass adds labels exactly where the current policy is weakest — because that is where it actually went." />

<Steps>
<Step title="Train the policy on everything you have">
Start with ordinary behaviour cloning on the expert's demonstrations. The first
iteration of DAgger *is* behaviour cloning.
</Step>

<Step title="Let the policy drive">
Roll it out on the real task. It will drift into states the expert never
visited — that is the point, not a failure. Record every state it reaches.
</Step>

<Step title="Ask the expert what they would have done">
For each recorded state, the expert supplies the correct action. Crucially they
label the state, they don't take over — the trajectory is still the learner's.
</Step>

<Step title="Aggregate and retrain">
Append the new labelled states to the dataset and retrain from scratch on the
union. Repeat. Each round the training distribution moves closer to the
distribution the policy actually induces.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
The word "aggregate" is the whole algorithm. You never throw the old data away —
you keep widening the dataset until it covers where the policy goes. That's what
drives regret from $O(\epsilon T^2)$ down to $O(\epsilon T)$: the horizon still
costs you, but each mistake no longer poisons every step after it.
</KeyIdea>

## The catch, stated honestly

DAgger needs an expert you can query **online**, at any state, on demand. Read
that requirement again with a real robot in mind:

- The policy fumbles a grasp and the object is half-tipped. What is the correct
  joint velocity command *right now*? A human teleoperator has never been in that
  state and has no fluent answer.
- Labelling is per-timestep. A 30-second episode at 20 Hz is 600 states to label.
- Early iterations produce policies bad enough that rollouts are unsafe.

<Warning>
In simulation with a scripted or privileged expert — one that can see the full
state — none of these costs apply, and DAgger is excellent. On hardware with a
human, the labelling cost is usually what kills it. Knowing which regime you're
in is the difference between citing DAgger and actually using it.
</Warning>

This is why modern real-robot systems mostly went the other way: keep the fixed
offline dataset, and attack the problem with action chunking and better action
distributions instead. DAgger remains the cleanest *explanation* of the problem,
and the right answer whenever cheap online labels exist.
