## DAgger as online learning

The formal move in the paper is to stop treating imitation as supervised
learning and treat it as **online** learning, where each DAgger iteration is one
round of a repeated game.

At iteration $i$ you hold policy $\pi_i$. Nature reveals a loss function — the
expert's disagreement, measured on the state distribution that $\pi_i$ itself
induces:

$$
\ell_i(\pi) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi_i}}
\big[\,\ell\big(s,\; \pi(s),\; \pi^*(s)\big)\,\big]
$$

Training on the *aggregated* dataset $\mathcal{D} = \bigcup_{j \le i}
\mathcal{D}_j$ is exactly **Follow-the-Leader**: pick the policy that minimises
total loss over every round seen so far.

<KeyIdea>
Once you see it as Follow-the-Leader, the guarantee is inherited rather than
invented. FTL on strongly convex losses is a no-regret algorithm, and no-regret
is precisely the property the imitation bound needs.
</KeyIdea>

## The guarantee

<Derivation title="From no-regret to $O(\epsilon T)$">

Let $\epsilon_N$ be the best achievable average loss in hindsight over the $N$
rounds, and $\gamma_N$ the average regret of the online learner:

$$
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell_i(\pi_i)
\;\le\; \epsilon_N + \gamma_N
$$

No-regret means $\gamma_N \to 0$ as $N \to \infty$. So for large $N$ some policy
in the sequence has loss at most $\epsilon_N$ **on its own state distribution**.

Now compare with behaviour cloning. There the argument stalled at "once we leave
$d^{\pi^*}$ we know nothing," and the worst case charged us $T - t$ for a
mistake at step $t$. Here the loss is already measured under $d^{\pi}$ — the
distribution we're evaluated on — so there is no off-distribution region to
bound pessimistically. Each of the $T$ steps contributes at most $\epsilon$:

$$
J(\pi) \;\le\; J(\pi^*) + O(\epsilon_N T) + O(\gamma_N T)
$$

</Derivation>

The whole gain is the swap from $d^{\pi^*}$ to $d^{\pi}$ in the expectation.
Everything else is bookkeeping.

<Warning>
Don't claim DAgger "removes distribution shift." It removes the *mismatch*, by
moving the training distribution to meet the test distribution — the shift is
still there, you've just collected data on the other side of it. And it says
nothing at all about mode averaging: if the expert is inconsistent, an
MSE-trained DAgger policy still predicts the mean of contradictory labels.
</Warning>

## The mixing schedule

Running the raw $\pi_1$ — trained on one pass of cloning — is often useless or
unsafe. So iteration $i$ rolls out a mixture:

$$
\hat{\pi}_i \;=\; \beta_i\, \pi^* \;+\; (1 - \beta_i)\, \pi_i
$$

meaning at each timestep you flip a $\beta_i$-weighted coin and let the expert
act. Two constraints pull against each other:

<Steps>
<Step title="$\beta_i$ must start high enough to be useful">
Otherwise early rollouts never reach the interesting part of the task and the
labels you pay for are worthless.
</Step>

<Step title="$\beta_i$ must decay to zero">
The guarantee is about $d^{\pi}$. If the expert keeps intervening forever, you
are collecting a blend and never converge to the learner's own distribution.
</Step>
</Steps>

A geometric schedule $\beta_i = p^{\,i-1}$ satisfies both. The paper notes that
the simplest choice — $\beta_1 = 1$ (pure expert on the first pass, i.e. plain
behaviour cloning) and $\beta_i = 0$ afterwards — works well in practice.
