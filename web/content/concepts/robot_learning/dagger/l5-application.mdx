interview:
  answer: >-
    DAgger is the direct fix for behaviour cloning's central problem. Cloning
    trains on the expert's state distribution but gets evaluated on the
    learner's, and once the policy makes a small error it's off-distribution
    with no data to recover from — that's the epsilon T squared regret. DAgger
    closes the loop: you roll out the current policy, have the expert label
    every state the policy actually visited, aggregate those labels into your
    dataset, retrain, and repeat. Ross and Bagnell frame it as online learning
    — training on the aggregate is Follow-the-Leader, and because that's a
    no-regret algorithm you inherit a bound where the error is measured under
    the learner's own distribution. That single substitution takes regret down
    to epsilon T, linear in the horizon. The cost is the part worth being
    honest about: you need an expert you can query at arbitrary states, on
    demand, including states that are already going wrong. In simulation that's
    nearly free — you script an expert with privileged access to full state and
    distil it into a pixel policy, which is how most sim2real pipelines are
    built. With a human teleoperator on real hardware it's a different story:
    nobody can fluently say what the correct joint velocities are from a
    half-dropped grasp, and you'd be labelling every timestep of every rollout.
    That's why real-robot imitation mostly went the other way — ACT and
    Diffusion Policy keep a fixed offline dataset and attack the horizon with
    action chunking instead of buying labels.
  followUps:
    - q: "What's the beta mixing schedule for, and what happens if you don't decay it?"
      a: >-
        Early policies are bad enough that pure rollouts wander off-task or are
        unsafe, so you let the expert take a fraction beta of the actions to
        keep the data useful. But the guarantee is about the learner's own
        state distribution, so beta has to decay to zero — otherwise you're
        forever collecting a blend of expert and learner states and never
        converge to the distribution you're actually evaluated on. Geometric
        decay is typical; the paper notes that beta equals one on the first
        pass and zero after works fine, which just means round one is plain
        behaviour cloning.
    - q: "Does DAgger fix mode averaging too?"
      a: >-
        No, and conflating them is the common error. DAgger changes where the
        data comes from; it does nothing about how the policy represents the
        action distribution. If the expert is genuinely multi-modal — sometimes
        left, sometimes right — an MSE-trained DAgger policy still regresses to
        the mean of contradictory labels and drives straight ahead. Worse, now
        you're collecting *more* contradictory labels. You need a different
        policy class for that: discretised action tokens, a mixture density, or
        a diffusion head.
    - q: "How would you get DAgger's benefit without an online expert?"
      a: >-
        Manufacture recovery data offline. During collection, perturb the robot
        — nudge it off the nominal path, add noise to the commanded action —
        and record the demonstrator's correction. You get labelled states off
        the expert's natural distribution without anyone having to answer
        questions about hypothetical states. It's not the same guarantee, but
        it targets the same gap. In simulation the cleaner answer is a
        privileged scripted expert, which makes labels free and puts you back
        in the regime where real DAgger works.
    - q: "When is DAgger clearly the right call?"
      a: >-
        Whenever the expert is code rather than a person. Distilling a
        full-state RL policy or a motion planner into a vision policy, sim2real
        pipelines for locomotion, anything where you can evaluate the expert at
        an arbitrary state for the price of a function call. In that regime the
        only real objection to DAgger disappears and it's straightforwardly the
        right tool.

## Deciding whether to use it

The interview question is almost never "what is DAgger" — it's whether you can
tell, in a specific setting, if it applies.

<Steps>
<Step title="Is the expert code or a person?">
Code — a planner, a scripted controller, a privileged RL policy — means labels
are a function call and DAgger is straightforwardly right. A person means
labelling is the bottleneck and you should be arguing for something else.
</Step>

<Step title="Can the expert answer at bad states?">
Not "can it do the task well," but "can it say what to do from this half-failed
configuration?" A motion planner can. A teleoperator generally cannot.
</Step>

<Step title="Is the failure actually covariate shift?">
If the policy fails identically at one specific decision point where demos
disagree, that's mode averaging and DAgger will not help — it will collect more
contradictory labels. Check the symptom before prescribing.
</Step>

<Step title="If no queryable expert exists, name the substitute">
Perturbation-and-correction during collection, action chunking to cut decision
points, or an expressive action head. Naming the alternative is what separates
a diagnosis from a recitation.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The highest-signal thing you can do on this question is volunteer the
limitation before you're asked. Most candidates present DAgger as the answer to
behaviour cloning. Saying "and here's why it's rare on real hardware, and
here's what people do instead" is what reads as having actually chosen between
them.
</Warning>
