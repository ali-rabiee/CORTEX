## Two MDPs that share everything but $P$

$$
\mathcal{M}_{\text{sim}} = (\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P_{\text{sim}},R,\gamma),
\qquad
\mathcal{M}_{\text{real}} = (\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P_{\text{real}},R,\gamma)
$$

You optimise $J_{\text{sim}}(\pi)$ and are graded on $J_{\text{real}}(\pi)$. How
much can they differ?

<Derivation title="The simulation lemma: why the gap is quadratic in the horizon">

Assume the dynamics differ by at most $\epsilon_P$ in total variation at every
state-action pair, and the rewards by at most $\epsilon_R$:

$$
\big\|P_{\text{sim}}(\cdot\mid s,a) - P_{\text{real}}(\cdot\mid s,a)\big\|_1 \le \epsilon_P,
\qquad
\big|R_{\text{sim}} - R_{\text{real}}\big| \le \epsilon_R
$$

**Step 1 — write the value difference recursively.** For a fixed policy $\pi$,

$$
V_{\text{sim}}^\pi(s) - V_{\text{real}}^\pi(s)
= \underbrace{\big(R_{\text{sim}} - R_{\text{real}}\big)}_{\le\, \epsilon_R}
+ \gamma\Big(\mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{sim}}}\big[V^\pi_{\text{sim}}(s')\big]
- \mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{real}}}\big[V^\pi_{\text{real}}(s')\big]\Big)
$$

**Step 2 — split the transition term.** Add and subtract
$\mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{real}}}[V^\pi_{\text{sim}}(s')]$:

$$
= \epsilon_R
+ \gamma\underbrace{\Big(\mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{sim}}} - \mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{real}}}\Big)\big[V^\pi_{\text{sim}}(s')\big]}_{\text{different dynamics, same value function}}
+ \gamma\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{real}}}\big[V^\pi_{\text{sim}}(s') - V^\pi_{\text{real}}(s')\big]}_{\text{recursion}}
$$

**Step 3 — bound the first term.** Two expectations of the same bounded function
under distributions $\epsilon_P$ apart in $L_1$ differ by at most
$\epsilon_P \cdot \|V\|_\infty$, and $\|V^\pi_{\text{sim}}\|_\infty \le R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$.
So that term is at most $\gamma\,\epsilon_P R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$.

**Step 4 — unroll the recursion.** The remaining term is $\gamma$ times the same
quantity at the next state, so the whole thing is a geometric series with ratio
$\gamma$, contributing another factor $1/(1-\gamma)$:

$$
\boxed{\;\big|J_{\text{sim}}(\pi) - J_{\text{real}}(\pi)\big|
\;\le\; \frac{\epsilon_R}{1-\gamma}
\;+\; \frac{\gamma\,\epsilon_P\,R_{\max}}{(1-\gamma)^2}\;}
$$

**Where each factor comes from.** One $1/(1-\gamma)$ is the horizon — you accrue
error at every step. The second is that a divergence at *any* step lands you in a
different state, and two states can differ in value by as much as
$R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$. It is structurally the same argument as behaviour
cloning's $O(\epsilon T^2)$.

</Derivation>

<Warning>
Put numbers in it. At $\gamma = 0.99$ the factor $1/(1-\gamma)^2$ is $10^4$, so a
per-step dynamics error of $10^{-3}$ permits a return error of order
$10\,R_{\max}$ — the entire achievable return. The bound is worst-case and real
transfer is far better than it, but the *scaling* is real and it explains an
observation everyone makes: long-horizon dynamic tasks like locomotion need much
tighter dynamics than short quasi-static ones like pick-and-place.
</Warning>

## Domain randomisation, formally

Parameterise the simulator by $\xi \in \Xi$ — masses, frictions, latencies,
actuator gains — and optimise the expected return over a distribution:

$$
\max_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_{\xi \sim p(\xi)}\big[J_{\mathcal{M}_\xi}(\pi_\theta)\big]
$$

If $p$ has support containing the true $\xi_{\text{real}}$, then real deployment
is one more draw from the training distribution — the sim-to-real problem has
been converted into a generalisation problem, which is one we know how to
reason about.

The robust alternative optimises the worst case instead:

$$
\max_\theta\; \min_{\xi \in \Xi}\; J_{\mathcal{M}_\xi}(\pi_\theta)
$$

which gives guarantees and is usually far too conservative, since it is
dominated by the least favourable physics you allowed.

<Derivation title="Randomisation makes it a POMDP, and that is the design fork">

$\xi$ affects transitions and is not in the observation. Formally the state is
$(s, \xi)$ and the agent sees only $s$ — the definition of partial observability.

**Consequence 1 — a memoryless policy must hedge.** $\pi(a\mid s)$ cannot
distinguish a heavy robot from a light one at the same configuration, so the
best it can do is a compromise that is adequate for both. This is genuine
robustness, and it is genuinely conservative: performance is bounded by what a
single strategy can achieve across the whole family.

**Consequence 2 — history is identifying.** How the robot responded to recent
actions is informative about $\xi$: the same torque produces different
accelerations at different masses. A policy $\pi(a \mid s_{t-k:t}, a_{t-k:t-1})$
can therefore infer $\xi$ and behave as a specialist for it.

This is *implicit system identification*, and it is strictly stronger than
robustness. It also explains the RMA architecture directly: train a teacher with
privileged access to the true $\xi$, compress $\xi$ into a latent, then train a
student to predict that latent from recent history alone. The student adapts
online because it is estimating the hidden variable rather than averaging over
it.

**The assumption doing the work is identifiability** — the history has to
actually contain information about $\xi$. A parameter that only matters in
situations the robot has not yet encountered cannot be inferred before it
matters.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
Frame stacking or recurrence is not an implementation detail in domain
randomisation. It changes what the optimal policy *is*: memoryless gives you
robustness, history gives you adaptation. If someone reports that randomisation
made their policy sluggish and conservative, the first question is whether the
policy could observe enough history to identify the environment.
</KeyIdea>

## System identification and its ceiling

Fit parameters to real trajectories:

$$
\hat\xi = \arg\min_{\xi\in\Xi}\;
\sum_t \big\|s^{\text{real}}_{t+1} - f_{\text{sim}}(s^{\text{real}}_t, a^{\text{real}}_t;\, \xi)\big\|^2
$$

This is a projection onto the model class, so it returns the closest member of
that class and no better.

<Steps>
<Step title="What it fixes">
Quantities that genuinely are parameters: link masses, inertias, friction
coefficients, motor constants. Cheap and worth doing first for anything you can
measure directly.
</Step>

<Step title="What it cannot fix">
Effects with no parameter. If the simulator has no model of gearbox backlash,
no assignment of masses and frictions produces backlash — and the optimiser will
distort the parameters it *does* have to partly absorb the residual, which can
make other predictions worse. The tell is a fit whose residual plateaus well
above sensor noise.
</Step>

<Step title="What to do then">
Enlarge the model class using real data. An actuator network — a small network
mapping commanded torque and joint history to realised torque, fitted on real
measurements and inserted into the simulator — is the canonical example, and it
is what made the ANYmal locomotion results transfer.
</Step>
</Steps>
