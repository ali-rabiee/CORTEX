## Randomise the appearance

<PaperCard refKey="tobin2017dr" />

The result that reframed the problem: train an object detector on renders with
random textures, random lighting, random camera poses, and random distractors —
deliberately *not* photorealistic — and it transfers to real images with no real
training data.

<KeyIdea>
Chasing photorealism aims at a single point in appearance space that you cannot
verify you have reached. Randomising makes appearance a nuisance variable the
network is forced to ignore, so it keys on geometry and relative position
instead, and reality becomes one more sample from a distribution it already
handles. An unbounded modelling problem becomes an ordinary generalisation
problem.
</KeyIdea>

## Randomise the physics, and notice what the policy does with it

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="peng2018dynamics" />
<PaperCard refKey="openai2018dactyl" />
</PaperGrid>

Peng et al. carried the idea from pixels to dynamics — masses, frictions, motor
gains — and observed something that turned out to matter more than the transfer
itself: a *recurrent* policy does implicit system identification. It is not
averaging over the randomisation; it is inferring which environment it is in
from how the robot has been responding, then acting accordingly.

Dactyl is the maximal version of the recipe. In-hand reorientation of a block on
a real Shadow Hand, trained entirely in simulation, with an LSTM policy and
aggressive randomisation of physics, appearance, and observation noise. It also
stated the cost honestly: the compute involved put the result out of reach for
almost everyone.

## Learn the physics you are missing

<PaperCard refKey="hwangbo2019anymal" />

The other route. Rather than widening the randomisation until reality is
covered, measure what the simulator gets wrong and add it. An actuator network —
a small model from commanded torque and recent joint history to realised torque,
fitted on real robot data and inserted into the simulator — supplies dynamics
that no assignment of masses and frictions could have produced.

<Warning>
The diagnostic for choosing between these routes is the system-ID residual. If
fitting parameters brings the trajectory error down to sensor noise, the model
class is adequate and randomisation handles what remains. If the residual
plateaus well above it, the mismatch is *structural* — the effect has no
parameter — and widening ranges on the parameters you do have buys robustness to
variation that is not what is hurting you.
</Warning>

## Teacher-student, and adaptation as the goal

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="lee2020terrain" />
<PaperCard refKey="kumar2021rma" />
</PaperGrid>

Both build on the same observation and make it cheap. In simulation you have
privileged information — the true friction, the exact terrain geometry, the real
contact state — that the robot will never have. So train a teacher that uses it,
which is an easy learning problem, then train a student to reproduce the
teacher's behaviour from what the robot can actually sense.

Lee et al. distil to proprioception alone, giving blind locomotion over terrain
the robot cannot see. RMA makes the target explicit: compress the privileged
environment parameters into a latent, then train an adaptation module to predict
that latent from recent history. The robot re-identifies the world within a
fraction of a second, which is why it recovers within a step of walking onto oil.

<KeyIdea>
The pattern generalises well beyond locomotion. When a hard problem has a
privileged solution available only at training time, learn the privileged
solution first and then distil it into something deployable. The student's job
is a supervised regression onto a target the teacher already found, which is
enormously easier than discovering the same representation through a policy
gradient.
</KeyIdea>

## Where it is now

<PaperCard refKey="handa2023dextreme" />

DeXtreme is the accessibility milestone: Dactyl-class in-hand manipulation
reproduced on commodity GPU-accelerated simulation. The significance is not a
new idea but a changed cost structure — massively parallel GPU physics turned a
datacentre-scale result into one a single well-equipped lab can run, which is
what moved sim-to-real from a demonstration into a default.

<Warning>
Keep the two domains apart when discussing results. Locomotion transfers well:
the dynamics are smooth, contact is intermittent, and proprioception is rich and
accurate. Contact-rich manipulation transfers much worse, because the physics
that matters — deformation, stiction, precise contact geometry — is exactly what
simulators model worst. That asymmetry, and not a difference in method, explains
why the field's manipulation results lean on imitation while its locomotion
results lean on sim-to-real RL.
</Warning>
