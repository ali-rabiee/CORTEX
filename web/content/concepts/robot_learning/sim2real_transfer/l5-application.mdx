interview:
  answer: >-
    Sim-to-real is the problem that you train where it's cheap and deploy where
    it matters, and the two have different physics. The framing I'd lead with is
    that this is much worse for RL than for a hand-designed controller, because
    RL optimises against the simulator. Any inaccuracy the simulator has is a
    free source of reward, and the policy will find it — penetrating contacts,
    energy gained from a collision, torque profiles no real actuator can
    produce. Your optimiser is an adversary against your physics engine, and a
    better optimiser makes transfer harder rather than easier. On the size of
    the problem, the simulation lemma is worth knowing: if the dynamics differ
    by epsilon in total variation at every state-action pair, the return gap is
    bounded by something like gamma epsilon R-max over one minus gamma squared.
    One factor of the horizon for accruing error each step, and a second because
    a divergence at any step lands you in a different state, and two states can
    differ in value by the whole return range. At gamma 0.99 that's a factor of
    ten thousand, which explains why long-horizon dynamic tasks like locomotion
    need much tighter dynamics than a short pick-and-place. There are three
    families of fix. Make the simulator match reality — system identification,
    which works for things that genuinely are parameters. Make the policy not
    care — domain randomisation, sampling physics per episode and training
    across the distribution, which converts sim-to-real into a generalisation
    problem provided reality is inside your support. Or adapt with real data —
    fine-tuning, residual policies, or training the policy to infer the
    environment from recent history. My decision rule between the first two is
    the system-ID residual: if fitting parameters brings trajectory error down
    to sensor noise, the model is adequate and I randomise the rest. If the
    residual plateaus well above sensor noise, the mismatch is structural —
    there's physics with no parameter — and widening ranges on the parameters I
    do have is buying robustness to the wrong variation. That's when I'd fit
    something like an actuator network on real data and put it in the simulator.
    And one thing I'd want to be precise about: whether the policy sees
    observation history changes what randomisation gives you. Memoryless, the
    physics parameters are an unobserved hidden variable, so it's a POMDP and
    the best policy hedges — that's robustness, and it's conservative. With
    history, the policy can identify the environment from how the robot has been
    responding and act as a specialist. That's the difference between a
    randomised policy that moves stiffly and RMA recovering within a step of
    walking onto oil.
  followUps:
    - q: "How wide should the randomisation ranges be?"
      a: >-
        Wide enough to contain reality and no wider, and the reason for the
        second half is that it's not a free knob. Domain randomisation asks for
        a single policy that works across the whole support, so widening
        monotonically lowers the achievable optimum — and past some point no
        strategy works for every sampled environment and the task stops being
        learnable. I'd calibrate everything I can measure first, since every
        pinned-down parameter is one I don't have to be robust to, then
        randomise multiplicatively around those nominals so the ranges mean
        plus-or-minus a percentage rather than an absolute band I retune per
        robot. If I genuinely need extreme ranges, I'd read that as an argument
        for an adaptive policy rather than a more robust one.
    - q: "You've deployed and it fails. How do you find out why?"
      a: >-
        Replay before anything else. Take a logged real rollout, feed exactly
        the same action sequence to the simulator from the same initial state,
        and compare the state trajectories. Where they start diverging tells me
        which part of the dynamics is missing, and it's a measurement of the gap
        rather than a guess. If divergence starts immediately on any commanded
        motion, I'd suspect latency or actuator dynamics, which are the two most
        common and the two cheapest to fix. If it only appears at contact, it's
        the contact model. The other cheap test is to evaluate the policy in
        simulation just outside its training randomisation range — if it falls
        off a cliff there, it interpolated rather than generalised, and reality
        had better be inside.
    - q: "Why does locomotion transfer so much better than manipulation?"
      a: >-
        Because the physics that matters is the physics simulators are good at.
        Legged locomotion is mostly rigid-body dynamics with intermittent
        contact, proprioception is rich and accurate, and the contacts that
        matter are foot-ground impacts you can model reasonably. Contact-rich
        manipulation depends on exactly what simulators model worst —
        deformation, stiction, precise contact geometry, friction that varies
        with surface condition — and a millimetre of geometric error changes
        whether a grasp succeeds. That asymmetry, not a difference in
        algorithms, is why the field's locomotion results lean on sim-to-real RL
        and its manipulation results lean on imitation from real demonstrations.
        I'd say that explicitly rather than implying one recipe covers robotics.
    - q: "Would you ever skip simulation entirely?"
      a: >-
        For contact-rich manipulation with a human available to demonstrate,
        often yes — that's essentially what the imitation pipeline is, and if I
        can get fifty demonstrations in an afternoon that's cheaper than
        building a simulator whose contact model I wouldn't trust anyway. Where
        I wouldn't skip it is anything dynamic or dangerous, anything needing
        behaviour a human can't demonstrate, or anything where I need to exceed
        demonstrator performance. The middle path that's become common is to use
        both: pretrain in sim where exploration is free and safe, then fine-tune
        on a small amount of real interaction where the search is local.
  traps:

    - "Generalising locomotion results to manipulation. The transfer difficulty is completely different, and the reason — which physics the simulator models well — is worth stating."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Fails immediately on any commanded motion → latency or actuator dynamics">
The two cheapest gaps to close and the two most commonly left open. Measure the
real step response, add a delay buffer, and fit an actuator model if the
residual is large.
</Step>

<Step title="Fails only at contact → the contact model">
Grasping, sliding, impact. Expected, and the hardest to fix — this is the reason
manipulation leans on real demonstrations rather than sim-to-real.
</Step>

<Step title="Behaviour looks physically implausible in sim → the policy found an exploit">
Energy appearing from collisions, torque profiles no motor could produce,
penetrating contacts. Watch the simulation rollouts before deploying; an
exploit is usually visible to the eye.
</Step>

<Step title="Transfers, but stiff and conservative → memoryless policy hedging">
Partial observability over the randomised parameters. Add observation and action
history, or train a teacher-student adaptation module. This is an architecture
fix, not a range fix.
</Step>

<Step title="Stops learning the task in sim → over-randomised">
The support has grown past what a single strategy can cover. Narrow it, calibrate
more, or move to an adaptive policy.
</Step>

<Step title="Anything unexplained → replay real actions through the simulator">
The single most useful diagnostic. Same initial state, same action sequence,
compare trajectories, and read off where and when the divergence begins.
</Step>
</Steps>

<InterviewAnswer />

<Warning>
Two things distinguish a strong answer. The first is the adversarial framing —
RL searches for the simulator's errors, so accuracy on average is not what
protects you, and improving your optimiser makes the problem worse. The second
is having a *decision rule* rather than a list of techniques: the system-ID
residual tells you whether your model class is adequate, and therefore whether
to randomise or to go and learn the missing physics. Candidates who can name
domain randomisation are common; candidates who can say when it is the wrong
tool are not.
</Warning>
