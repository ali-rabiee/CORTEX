## Why train in simulation at all

Every property that makes simulation attractive is about cost. It is free, it
runs faster than real time, thousands of copies run in parallel, nothing breaks,
episodes reset instantly, and you have exact access to state that no sensor
gives you. A locomotion policy that would take a decade of wall-clock on
hardware trains overnight in Isaac.

Then you deploy it and the robot falls over.

<Intuition>
Training a pilot entirely on a simulator and putting them in a real cockpit. The
simulator taught them the layout, the procedures, the sequence — genuinely
useful. But it taught them that the controls respond instantly, that turbulence
is a specific parametric wobble, and that the engine never has the particular
lag this airframe has.

Worse: if you graded them purely on simulator score for long enough, they would
have discovered that a certain sharp input produces an unphysical performance
bonus, and learned to rely on it.
</Intuition>

## The part that makes RL special

A hand-tuned controller developed in simulation transfers imperfectly. An RL
policy trained in the same simulator can transfer catastrophically, and the
reason is worth stating precisely.

<KeyIdea>
Your optimiser is an adversary against your physics engine. RL searches the
space of behaviours for whatever maximises reward, and any inaccuracy the
simulator has is a free source of reward. Penetrating contacts, energy gained
from collisions, torques no real actuator could supply, exploitation of a
frictionless corner case — the policy will find them, because finding things is
what it does. A *better* optimiser makes sim-to-real harder.
</KeyIdea>

<Steps>
<Step title="Actuator dynamics — the most common culprit">
Simulators typically command torque instantly. Real motors have finite bandwidth,
gearbox friction, backlash, thermal limits, and current controllers with their
own dynamics. A policy that relies on sharp torque reversals is relying on an
actuator that does not exist.
</Step>

<Step title="Latency">
Observation to computation to command is tens of milliseconds on real hardware
and zero in most simulators. For anything dynamic this alone can be the whole
gap, and it is trivially cheap to model — add a delay buffer.
</Step>

<Step title="Contact and friction">
Rigid-body contact is a hard modelling problem, and the coefficients are neither
constant nor known. Anything involving grasping, sliding, or impact is exposed
here.
</Step>

<Step title="Visual appearance">
Rendering versus a real camera: lighting, noise, motion blur, materials. Real,
and the *easiest* of these to fix, because pixels are an input you can flood
with variation.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Three families of answer

<Steps>
<Step title="Make the simulator match reality — system identification">
Fit the simulator's parameters to real trajectories. Effective for things that
*are* parameters — masses, inertias, friction coefficients. Powerless against
effects the model does not contain, which is the usual case.
</Step>

<Step title="Make the policy not care — domain randomisation">
Sample physics parameters afresh each episode and train across the distribution.
If reality is somewhere inside that distribution, the policy has already seen
something like it. The cost is peak performance: one policy must now work for a
whole family.
</Step>

<Step title="Adapt using real data — fine-tuning and online adaptation">
Collect a modest amount of real interaction and either fine-tune, learn a
residual on top of the sim policy, or — most elegantly — train the policy to
*infer* which environment it is in from recent history and adjust. That last one
is RMA, and it is why a robot can walk onto oil and recover within a step.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
"Calibrate what you can measure, randomise what you cannot" is the working
heuristic, and the second half has a limit. Over-randomising does not just cost
performance — it can make the task unlearnable, because a policy required to
work across every friction coefficient may find that no single strategy works
for all of them. Wide enough to contain reality, and no wider.
</Warning>
