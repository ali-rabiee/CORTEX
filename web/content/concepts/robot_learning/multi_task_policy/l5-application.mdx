## Failure modes

Language grounding failure: the policy understands the language (correct text embedding) but fails to ground it in the physical scene (picks wrong object). Negative transfer: adding too many diverse tasks degrades performance on each individual task compared to single-task specialists. Distribution of tasks in training data matters — rare tasks get forgotten. Symptoms: policy follows common instructions well but fails on rare or compositional ones. Fix: balanced task sampling, task-specific evaluation, hierarchical policies (language $\rightarrow$ subgoal $\rightarrow$ action), and grounding modules that explicitly link language to perception.

## Interview-ready answer

Multi-task policies are the path to general-purpose robot systems. Language conditioning via $\pi(a|s, \text{Enc}(l))$ is the most flexible approach — it enables open-vocabulary task specification and compositional generalization. The architecture choices that matter: (1) language encoder — CLIP or SentenceBERT for instruction embedding, (2) fusion mechanism — FiLM conditioning or cross-attention between visual and language features, (3) action space — discretized tokens (RT-2) vs continuous (diffusion). The key challenge is data: you need diverse demonstrations across many tasks with language annotations. Current frontier: RT-2, Octo, and OpenVLA show that internet-scale pretraining dramatically improves multi-task robot performance.
