interview:
  answer: >-
    A multi-task policy is just a policy with a second input naming the task —
    pi of a given o and z — where z can be a one-hot index, a goal image, or an
    embedding of a natural-language instruction. The architecture barely
    changes; what changes is what the shared trunk gets to amortise. Gripper
    control, object-centric visual features, collision avoidance: every task
    needs those, and separate policies learn them separately from separate
    data. The choice of z matters more than people expect. A one-hot vector
    makes "pick up the red block" exactly as different from "pick up the blue
    block" as from "wipe the table" — there's no structure in the geometry — so
    nothing transfers through the conditioning and adding a task means changing
    the architecture. Language embeddings put related instructions near each
    other, and you can describe a new task by typing a sentence. The thing that
    goes wrong is negative transfer. Tasks whose gradients disagree compete for
    shared capacity, and adding tasks can make every task worse. Meta-World
    established that empirically, and PCGrad is the clean statement of the
    optimisation-side fix — detect gradients with negative inner product and
    project the conflicting component out. But that's mostly not how the field
    solved it. RT-1 and Open X-Embodiment showed that at seven hundred tasks
    and a large enough model, joint training beats per-task training outright,
    and adding data from a different robot helps rather than hurts. So my
    working answer is that negative transfer is a small-scale phenomenon: real
    if you have ten tasks and a modest network, largely outgrown if you have
    enough data and capacity. And practically, the first thing I check on any
    multi-task policy is conditioning sensitivity — hold the scene fixed, swap
    the instruction, and see whether the predicted action actually moves. It
    very often doesn't.
  followUps:
    - q: "Why would the conditioning be ignored? Isn't it an input?"
      a: >-
        Because in most datasets the scene already reveals the task. If "open
        the drawer" was only ever collected in front of a drawer and "fold the
        towel" only in front of a towel, then the observation is a perfect
        predictor of the instruction and reading z buys the network nothing —
        it minimises the training loss just as well by learning a scene-to-
        action map. It looks flawless until you ask for a different task in the
        same scene. The fix isn't architectural; it's collecting demonstrations
        of several different tasks from the same starting configuration. FiLM
        conditioning at multiple layers makes ignoring z more expensive than
        concatenating once at the head, but it can't manufacture a signal the
        data doesn't contain.
    - q: "You have budget for 5,000 more demonstrations. More per task, or more tasks?"
      a: >-
        More tasks, if I care about generalising to new instructions. That's
        BC-Z's finding — zero-shot success on held-out tasks tracked the number
        of distinct training tasks far more than demos per task. The mechanism
        is that with few tasks, memorising sentence-to-behaviour is the
        cheapest solution the network can find, and it generalises to nothing.
        Only when verbs and objects recur in varied combinations does
        decomposing the instruction become the economical representation. The
        caveat is a floor — if I'm below roughly fifty demos on a task it won't
        work at all, so I'd top up the thin tasks first and spend the rest on
        breadth.
    - q: "How would you tell negative transfer from just not having enough data?"
      a: >-
        Run the scaling comparison in the task axis rather than the data axis.
        Hold demonstrations-per-task fixed and train on two, five, and ten
        tasks, watching each task's success separately. If per-task performance
        falls as you add tasks, that's interference. If it's flat or rising,
        you have a data problem, not a transfer problem. The other half is the
        N-single-task baseline at matched total compute — if the joint policy
        doesn't beat separate policies, you've paid for interference and got
        nothing. That comparison is the one multi-task papers are weakest on,
        and it's what I'd want to see before believing a result.
    - q: "Does language conditioning actually require a language model?"
      a: >-
        Not for the conditioning itself — a frozen sentence encoder is enough,
        and in practice you cache the embeddings since they never change. What
        a large pretrained model buys you is semantic structure you didn't pay
        to collect: "grab the mug" landing near "pick up the cup" without ever
        having demonstrated both phrasings. That's real, and it's cheap. What
        it does not buy you is compositional generalisation to new tasks —
        that comes from the data varying verbs and objects independently. RT-2
        is the interesting counterexample, because there the web-scale
        vision-language pretraining does carry over genuinely new semantic
        knowledge, but that's a much bigger claim than "we used a text
        encoder."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Same behaviour regardless of instruction → the conditioning is dead">
Run the sensitivity check: fix the observation, sweep $z$, measure the spread of
predicted actions. Near zero and you are looking at a scene-to-action map. Then
look at your data, not your architecture — the usual cause is that each task was
collected in its own scene.
</Step>

<Step title="Per-task success falls as you add tasks → negative transfer">
Confirm it by scaling in the *task* axis with demonstrations-per-task held fixed.
Falling per-task curves mean interference; flat ones mean you have a data
problem. Compare against separate policies at matched total compute before
concluding anything.
</Step>

<Step title="One task dominates → the sampler is your weighting">
Pooling and shuffling sets each task's influence proportional to its dataset
size. Check the per-task example counts before blaming the model; a specialist
wearing a multi-task label is the most common version of this.
</Step>

<Step title="Trained instructions fine, new ones hopeless → the dataset never demanded composition">
One instruction per task makes whole-sentence lookup optimal. No encoder change
fixes it. You need verbs and objects varying independently.
</Step>

<Step title="Small tasks memorised, large tasks fine → over-replay from balanced sampling">
The mirror image of the imbalance problem. Equalising tasks replays a 200-demo
set 25x more often than a 5,000-demo one. Watch per-task validation separately.
</Step>
</Steps>

<InterviewAnswer />

<Warning>
The distinguishing move in this answer is regime-awareness. Negative transfer is
neither a myth nor a law — it is what happens when tasks compete for capacity,
and whether that dominates depends on how much capacity and data you have. A
candidate who says "multi-task learning causes negative transfer" and one who
says "scale solved it" have each memorised half the story. Saying which regime
you are in, and how you would tell, is the answer that holds up under
follow-ups.
</Warning>
