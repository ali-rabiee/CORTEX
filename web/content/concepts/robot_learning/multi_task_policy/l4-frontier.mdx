## Making the problem measurable

<PaperCard refKey="yu2019metaworld" />

Fifty manipulation tasks on one simulated arm with one action space, deliberately
built so that transfer *ought* to work. The baselines then showed multi-task
training losing to per-task training. That combination is what turned negative
transfer from an anecdote into a target: with the task mismatch controlled away,
the remaining explanation is how the objectives interact during optimisation.

## The optimisation-side answer

<PaperCard refKey="yu2020pcgrad" />

Name the mechanism — gradients with negative inner product — and delete it by
projection. Clean, cheap to state, with a provable pairwise guarantee. It is the
most quotable version of a family that also includes loss reweighting, uncertainty
weighting, and per-task normalisation, all of which treat interference as
something to be *managed* at the optimiser.

## The data-side answer

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="lynch2019play" />
<PaperCard refKey="lynch2021lang" />
</PaperGrid>

A different reading of the problem: the shortage is not optimisation but
coverage. Let a human play with the objects for hours with no task in mind,
relabel every segment in hindsight by the state it reached, and you have
multi-task data without ever defining a task list. The follow-up shows language
can be bolted on by annotating only a small fraction of that play — the rest
still trains the policy through a shared latent goal space.

<PaperCard refKey="jang2022bcz" />

Then the test that mattered: 100 tasks, conditioned on a sentence or a human
video, evaluated on tasks held out from training entirely. The finding —
generalisation tracks the *number of distinct tasks* far more than the
demonstrations per task — is the most actionable result in this whole cluster,
because it tells you what to go collect.

<KeyIdea>
With few tasks, mapping each entire instruction to a behaviour is the cheapest
thing the network can do, and it generalises to nothing. Varied verbs and objects
in varied combinations are what make decomposition the economical solution. The
language encoder does not give you compositionality; the dataset does.
</KeyIdea>

## The scale-side answer, which mostly won

<PaperCard refKey="brohan2022rt1" />

130k demonstrations, 700+ instructions, one transformer emitting discretised
action tokens. The result that reframed the field: joint training **beats**
per-task training, and folding in simulation data and data from a different robot
raises performance rather than degrading it. The interference that Meta-World
measured had not been solved by cleverness — it had been outgrown.

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="oneill2023openx" />
<PaperCard refKey="ghosh2024octo" />
</PaperGrid>

Open X-Embodiment pushed the same logic past tasks to bodies: pool 22 embodiments
and a single policy beats per-embodiment training. Octo made a generalist policy
of that kind open and extensible, so new sensors and action spaces can be
attached without starting over.

<Warning>
Do not read this arc as "gradient conflict was a myth". PCGrad's guarantees are
correct, and negative transfer is easy to reproduce at small scale — which is the
regime most people's projects are actually in. The honest summary is regime
dependence: with 10 tasks and a modest network, tasks compete and the
optimisation-side tools earn their keep; with 700 tasks and a large one, shared
structure dominates and the scale-side answer subsumes them. Diagnosing which
regime you are in is the useful skill.
</Warning>
