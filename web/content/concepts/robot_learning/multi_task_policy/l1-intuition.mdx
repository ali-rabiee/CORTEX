## One brain, many jobs

A single-task policy is a function of what the robot sees. A multi-task policy
takes a second input: something that says *which task*.

$$
\pi(a \mid o) \quad\longrightarrow\quad \pi(a \mid o, z)
$$

That $z$ can be a one-hot index over a fixed task list, an embedding of the
sentence "put the sponge in the drawer", or an image of the desired final state.
The architecture barely changes. What changes is everything about how the system
behaves as you add tasks.

<Figure src="/figures/multitask-conditioning.svg"
        alt="Left: three tasks each with a separate encoder and head, nothing shared. Right: one shared trunk taking an image and a task descriptor z, producing an action."
        caption="The conditioning variable is the whole design. Everything the tasks have in common gets learned once in the trunk; z is only asked to select among behaviours, not to build them." />

<Intuition>
Cooking is the useful analogy. You do not learn "make an omelette" and "make a
risotto" as two unrelated skills — knife control, heat management, and knowing
when a pan is hot enough are shared, and the recipe just says which of them to
apply in which order.

The recipe is $z$. It is short because most of the competence lives elsewhere.
</Intuition>

## Why bother

<Steps>
<Step title="Amortisation">
Holding an object without crushing it, keeping the arm out of its own way,
finding the gripper in the image — every task needs these, and separate policies
learn them separately from separate data. One trunk learns them once, from the
union of all your data.
</Step>

<Step title="Data you could not otherwise use">
Twenty tasks with 50 demonstrations each is 1000 demonstrations for the shared
representation. No individual task could have justified collecting them.
</Step>

<Step title="A usable interface">
Adding a task to a one-hot policy means changing the architecture and
retraining. Adding a task to a language-conditioned policy means typing a
sentence. That is the difference between a research artefact and something a
non-expert can direct.
</Step>

<Step title="Generalisation across, not just within, tasks">
Trained on "pick up the sponge" and "put the block in the drawer", a policy that
has genuinely decomposed language can attempt "put the sponge in the drawer".
This is the payoff people actually want — and the one that most often fails to
materialise.
</Step>
</Steps>

## What goes wrong

**Negative transfer.** Tasks are not automatically friends. When two tasks need
opposite actions from similar-looking observations, their gradients point in
opposing directions and the shared update partly cancels. Adding tasks can make
every task worse. This is the phenomenon that made multi-task learning a
research problem rather than a free lunch.

**The conditioning gets ignored.** If the observation already reveals the task —
only a drawer is present, so the task must be "open the drawer" — the policy can
solve the training set without ever reading $z$. It looks perfect until you ask
for a different task in the same scene.

**Task imbalance.** Sum the per-task losses and the task with ten times the data
gets ten times the gradient. Your policy quietly becomes a specialist wearing a
multi-task label.

<Warning>
The single most useful diagnostic is cheap: hold the scene fixed and change only
$z$. If the behaviour does not change, the policy is not conditioned on anything
— it has learned a scene-to-action map and the instruction is decorative. Run
this before believing any multi-task result, including your own.
</Warning>

<KeyIdea>
Negative transfer is real at small scale and largely dissolves at large scale.
RT-1 and its successors find that one policy over hundreds of tasks *beats*
per-task policies — but only once data and capacity are big enough that the
tasks stop competing for room. Being able to say which regime you are in is more
useful than any opinion about whether multi-task learning "works".
</KeyIdea>
