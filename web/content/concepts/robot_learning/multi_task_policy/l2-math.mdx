## The objective, and the weighting hidden inside it

With tasks $i = 1 \ldots N$, per-task datasets $\mathcal{D}_i$, and conditioning
variables $z_i$:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \;
\mathbb{E}_{(o,a)\sim\mathcal{D}_i}\Big[\ell\big(\pi_\theta(o, z_i),\, a\big)\Big]
$$

Nothing here is new — it is a sum of single-task losses. The interesting content
is entirely in $w_i$ and in the fact that all tasks share $\theta$.

<Warning>
There is no default. Pooling every demonstration and shuffling sets
$w_i \propto |\mathcal{D}_i|$, which means your task weighting is whatever your
data collection happened to produce. Sampling a task uniformly and then an
example within it sets $w_i = 1/N$, which equalises influence at the cost of
replaying small datasets many times per epoch. Both are choices; the failure is
making one by accident and reporting it as neutral.
</Warning>

## Gradient conflict

Write $g_i = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_i(\theta)$. Tasks $i$ and $j$ **conflict**
at $\theta$ when

$$
g_i^\top g_j < 0
$$

The shared update $\sum_i g_i$ then contains components that undo each other, and
in the worst case the sum is shorter than any individual gradient — you take a
small step that helps nobody.

        alt="Left: two gradient vectors more than ninety degrees apart, with their sum drawn shorter than either. Right: task A's gradient projected onto the plane normal to task B's, yielding a vector that no longer opposes B."
        caption="Conflict is a statement about an inner product at the current parameters. PCGrad removes exactly the offending component and keeps the rest." />

<Derivation title="PCGrad's projection, and the two things it guarantees">

**Step 1 — remove the conflicting component.** Decompose $g_i$ into the part
along $g_j$ and the part orthogonal to it, and keep only the orthogonal part:

$$
g_i^{\perp} \;=\; g_i - \frac{g_i^\top g_j}{\|g_j\|^2}\, g_j
$$

**Step 2 — check it no longer opposes $j$.**

$$
g_i^{\perp\top} g_j = g_i^\top g_j - \frac{g_i^\top g_j}{\|g_j\|^2}\,\|g_j\|^2 = 0
$$

Exactly orthogonal, not merely improved.

**Step 3 — check it still helps $i$.**

$$
g_i^{\perp\top} g_i = \|g_i\|^2 - \frac{(g_i^\top g_j)^2}{\|g_j\|^2} \;\ge\; 0
$$

non-negative by Cauchy–Schwarz, with equality only when $g_i$ and $g_j$ are
exactly anti-parallel — the one case where the tasks are in total opposition and
no shared direction exists.

**The assumptions doing the work.** The analysis is *pairwise* and *local*. With
$N$ tasks, PCGrad projects each gradient against the others in random order, and
the result depends on that order — orthogonality to the last one projected
against is not preserved by the earlier projections. And every statement holds
at the current $\theta$; nothing is claimed about the trajectory of optimisation
or about reaching a Pareto-optimal point.

</Derivation>

## How the task actually enters the network

<Steps>
<Step title="Concatenation">
Append $z$ to the pooled features before the head. Simplest, and the easiest for
the network to ignore — the head can learn near-zero weights on those inputs if
the observation already determines the task.
</Step>

<Step title="FiLM modulation">
Predict per-channel scale and shift from $z$ and apply them at several layers:
$h \leftarrow \gamma(z) \odot h + \beta(z)$. The task now modulates the
computation rather than being a late-arriving side input, which makes ignoring
it far more expensive.
</Step>

<Step title="Tokens in a sequence model">
Encode the instruction as tokens and let the policy transformer attend over
them jointly with visual tokens. This is what RT-1 and every VLA do, and it is
the only option that scales to instructions with internal structure worth
attending to.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
Whatever the mechanism, the property you need is that $\pi(a \mid o, z)$
actually *depends* on $z$. Measure it: hold $o$ fixed, vary $z$, and look at how
much the action distribution moves. A policy with near-zero sensitivity has
learned a scene-to-action map, and every multi-task claim you make about it is
unsupported.
</KeyIdea>

## Goal conditioning and the relabelling trick

Let $z = g$ be a goal state or goal image, with $\phi$ mapping states to goals.
Then any trajectory carries a free label: whatever it was *trying* to do, it is
a demonstration of reaching $\phi(s_T)$.

$$
\tau = (s_0, a_0, \ldots, s_T) \;\longrightarrow\;
\big\{(s_t, a_t, \; g = \phi(s_T))\big\}_{t<T}
$$

This is hindsight relabelling, and it is why goal-conditioned methods can learn
from unstructured "play" data that has no task labels at all: every segment of
every trajectory is training data for some goal.

<Warning>
The trick is sound about *reachability* and silent about *quality*. Behaviour
cloning imitates whatever it is shown, so relabelling a trajectory that wandered
for 200 steps before stumbling onto a state teaches the policy to wander toward
that state. Relabelling fits far more comfortably in reinforcement learning,
where a reward still penalises the wandering. In the imitation setting it needs
filtering — short segments, or a preference for trajectories that reached their
endpoint efficiently.
</Warning>
