## Contact is a different regime

<Intuition>
In free space the robot's dynamics are smooth and the environment is irrelevant.
The instant it touches something, the equations change: a constraint appears,
forces spike, and the relationship between what you command and what happens is
governed by a stiffness you did not choose and cannot measure. Treating
manipulation as "motion planning, but more precise" fails because contact is not
a harder version of free space. It is a different regime with different
mathematics.
</Intuition>

The number that makes this concrete: pressing 1 mm into a hard plastic surface at
$10^6$ N/m generates **1000 N**. A Franka Panda's payload is 3 kg. So a
position-controlled arm that commands a target 1 mm past a surface is not
slightly inaccurate — it is commanding a force two orders of magnitude beyond
what the parts survive.

## Compliance, and what it actually buys

Robot and environment behave as springs in series, so $1/K_{\text{eff}} =
1/K_r + 1/K_e$, and **the softer element sets the force**. Everyone knows this
buys lower forces. The more valuable consequence is different:

<Figure src="/figures/contact-compliance-decouples.svg" alt="Left: robot and environment stiffnesses as springs in series, with the sensitivity of the effective stiffness to the environment's. Right: contact force at 1 mm penetration versus environment stiffness — a stiff robot spans 100 N to 91 kN while a compliant one stays between 9.1 and 10.0 N." caption="Compliance does more than reduce force. It makes the force nearly independent of a parameter you never knew." />

With a stiff robot, sweeping the environment stiffness across three decades moves
the contact force from 100 N to 91 kN. With a compliant robot at $10^4$ N/m, the
same sweep gives 9.1 N to 10.0 N — a spread of 1.1×.

<KeyIdea>
Compliance converts an unknown parameter into an irrelevant one. That is the
sim2real argument for contact in one sentence: you cannot identify the stiffness
of every surface your robot will touch, and if you are much softer than all of
them, you do not have to.
</KeyIdea>

## Why the policy cannot be the contact controller

<Steps>
<Step title="Contact transients are about a millisecond">
The natural timescale of a contact is $\sqrt{m/k}$: 10 ms against soft rubber,
1 ms against hard plastic, 0.03 ms against steel.
</Step>

<Step title="A learned policy runs at 10–50 Hz">
Vision-based policies are limited by the camera and the network. A 10 Hz policy
has a 100 ms period — a hundred times slower than the plastic transient, three
thousand times slower than steel.
</Step>

<Step title="So contact is handled below the policy">
A 1 kHz impedance loop reacts to contact; the policy cannot. This is not an
engineering shortcut, it is a consequence of the timescales.
</Step>

<Step title="The policy commands setpoints and stiffness">
It says where to aim and how firmly to insist, at its own leisurely rate. The
inner loop resolves what happens on contact. The action space is the interface
between two regimes running three decades apart.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
This is why "just add a force sensor to the observation" underdelivers. Sampling
force at the policy's rate aliases a signal whose interesting structure lives two
decades higher — you see that contact happened, long after the moment when
reacting would have mattered. The sensor belongs in the fast loop; what reaches
the policy should be an aggregate over the interval, not an instantaneous sample.
</Warning>
