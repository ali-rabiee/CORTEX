interview:
  answer: >-
    The framing I'd start with is that contact isn't a harder version of
    free-space motion, it's a different regime, and most of the engineering
    follows from that. In free space the robot's dynamics are smooth and the
    environment doesn't enter. The moment you touch something, a constraint
    appears and position error turns into force through the environment's
    stiffness — which you neither chose nor know. The number that makes it
    concrete: a millimetre of penetration against hard plastic at ten to the six
    newtons per metre is a thousand newtons. A Franka's payload is three
    kilograms. So a position-controlled arm commanding a target a millimetre past
    a surface isn't slightly inaccurate, it's commanding a force two orders of
    magnitude beyond what the parts survive. That's why you go to impedance
    control, but I'd be careful about why. The usual reason given is that
    compliance lowers the forces, which is true and is the less interesting half.
    The robot and the environment are springs in series, so the softer one sets
    the force, and if you differentiate the effective stiffness with respect to
    the environment's you get the ratio of the two, squared. At ten to the four
    against ten to the six that's ten to the minus four — a hundred percent error
    in your assumed environment stiffness moves the realised force by a hundredth
    of a percent. So compliance doesn't just reduce the force, it removes the
    parameter you had no way of identifying. That's robustness by insensitivity
    rather than by estimation, and it's the reason contact sim2real is tractable
    at all. The last piece is timescales. A contact transient is root m over k —
    about a millisecond against plastic, thirty microseconds against steel. A
    vision-based policy runs at ten to fifty hertz. So the policy is two to three
    orders of magnitude too slow to react to contact and cannot be the contact
    controller, no matter how good it is. What it can do is command a setpoint
    and a stiffness to a one-kilohertz inner loop — where to aim and how firmly
    to insist — and let that loop resolve what happens on contact. That split
    isn't an engineering convenience, it's dictated by the physics, and it's the
    thing I'd want to get across.
  followUps:
    - q: "How would you pick the stiffness?"
      a: >-
        From a force budget rather than by tuning. I ask what force the parts and
        the arm tolerate, and what position error I actually expect given
        calibration and repeatability, and take K as the ratio. Five newtons at
        two millimetres of expected error is twenty-five hundred newtons per
        metre. Then per-axis, because the axes have different jobs: soft on the
        ones that must comply, stiff on the one that must make progress. And I'd
        damp critically, because overshoot against a stiff surface is an impact,
        and impact forces aren't bounded by K times delta at all — that's the
        case the stiffness calculation doesn't cover.
    - q: "Your policy works in sim and fails on the real robot. Contact-specific reasons?"
      a: >-
        The first one I'd check is that simulated contact is much softer than
        real contact, and for a numerical reason rather than a modelling
        oversight. Explicit integration needs a timestep under two root m over k,
        which is sixty microseconds for steel, so everybody softens contact by
        orders of magnitude. The policy then learns against an environment that
        yields where the real one doesn't, and anything that leaned on
        penetration or on the soft rebound doesn't transfer. Second is friction —
        it's set-valued at zero velocity, it's the least identifiable parameter
        you have, and it varies with surface condition on the same parts. Both
        argue for randomising contact parameters specifically, not just visuals
        and masses, and for making the policy compliant enough that the
        difference stops mattering.
    - q: "Differentiable simulators give exact gradients. Doesn't that solve this?"
      a: >-
        Not for contact, and the reason is nice. Contact makes the objective
        piecewise smooth — sliding, sticking, separated are different pieces — so
        the pathwise gradient is exact within a mode and tells you nothing about
        crossing between modes, which is exactly the decision that matters. It's
        low variance and biased. The zeroth-order estimator is unbiased with
        variance falling as one over root N. Variance is a sample-budget problem;
        bias isn't, and it points confidently in a wrong direction no matter how
        much compute you spend. Suh and Tedrake have a paper giving the criterion
        for when each wins. It's the principled reason model-free methods stayed
        competitive here, rather than differentiable sim just being immature.
    - q: "What if the clearance is tighter than the robot's repeatability?"
      a: >-
        Then it stops being a positioning problem, and I'd say that explicitly,
        because the instinct is to improve the estimate. Fifty microns of
        clearance against plus or minus a hundred microns of repeatability means
        the target is smaller than the error bar, so no open-loop command can be
        trusted and no amount of better perception closes it. What works is
        substituting a different information source: make contact deliberately
        and let force do the measuring. A chamfer converts lateral offset into a
        restoring force; a spiral search sweeps the uncertainty disc while
        compliance bounds the forces; and the success signal is axial position
        dropping, which is a direct observation of alignment. Vision gets you to
        the neighbourhood and contact finishes it.
  traps:
    - "Describing contact as needing 'more precision'. It needs different control — a more accurate arm pressing into a jam still generates the jam force."
    - "Giving compliance only as force reduction. The insensitivity result is the half that explains why contact sim2real works at all."
    - "Proposing that the learned policy handle contact reactively. At 10 Hz against a 1 ms transient it is a hundred times too slow, and no architecture fixes a timescale."
    - "Assuming exact gradients through a differentiable simulator are strictly better. Discontinuity makes them biased, and bias does not shrink with more samples."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Large forces, parts damaged → position control in contact">
Check the effective stiffness. If the arm is stiffer than the environment, the
environment is setting the force and you have no authority over it.
</Step>

<Step title="Jams part-way in → contact-mode mismatch or a friction gap">
The classic sim2real failure for insertion. Suspect softened simulated contact
and unidentified friction before suspecting perception.
</Step>

<Step title="Works at high stiffness, fails at low → gravity compensation or actuator friction">
Sag makes every setpoint mean something else; stiffness below the arm's own
friction makes the setpoint a suggestion. Both read as a policy failure.
</Step>

<Step title="Policy 'ignores' the force channel → it is being aliased">
An instantaneous sample at 10 Hz misses a 1 ms impact. Aggregate over the
interval — max, mean, contact fraction — before concluding it is uninformative.
</Step>

<Step title="Cannot find the hole at all → the tolerance is below the uncertainty">
Stop improving the estimate. Switch to a compliant search where contact supplies
the measurement.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The strongest version of this answer refuses the "manipulation needs precision"
framing and replaces it with three physical facts: the force a millimetre of
error produces, the squared insensitivity that makes compliance more than
softness, and the three-decade timescale gap that puts contact handling below the
policy. Each one implies a design decision, and giving the implication rather
than the fact is what separates having read about contact from having debugged
it.
</Warning>
