## Why the soft element wins

Model the robot's controlled stiffness $K_r$ and the environment's $K_e$ as
springs in series under an imposed displacement $\delta$.

<Derivation title="Series compliance and the sensitivity to what you do not know">

The two springs share the force and split the deflection:

$$
F = K_r \delta_r = K_e \delta_e, \qquad \delta = \delta_r + \delta_e
$$

Substituting $\delta_r = F/K_r$ and $\delta_e = F/K_e$ gives

$$
\frac{1}{K_{\text{eff}}} = \frac{1}{K_r} + \frac{1}{K_e},
\qquad
K_{\text{eff}} = \frac{K_r K_e}{K_r + K_e}
$$

As $K_e \to \infty$, $K_{\text{eff}} \to K_r$. The soft element sets the force.
Now differentiate with respect to the parameter you cannot measure:

$$
\frac{\partial K_{\text{eff}}}{\partial K_e}
= \frac{K_r^2}{(K_r + K_e)^2}
= \left(\frac{K_{\text{eff}}}{K_e}\right)^{2}
$$

**The assumption doing the work is $K_r \ll K_e$** — that the robot is the softer
element. Under it, $K_{\text{eff}}/K_e$ is small and the sensitivity is its
*square*. At $K_r = 10^4$ against $K_e = 10^6$ the sensitivity is $10^{-4}$: a
100% error in the assumed environment stiffness moves the realised force by 0.01%.

At $K_r = 10^6$ against the same $K_e$, the sensitivity is $0.25$ and the
environment is back in your problem.

</Derivation>

Numerically, at 1 mm of penetration across $K_e \in \{10^5, 10^6, 10^8\}$:

| $K_r$ | force range | spread |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $10^4$ N/m | 9.1 → 10.0 N | 1.1× |
| $10^6$ N/m | 91 → 990 N | 10.9× |
| $10^9$ N/m | 100 N → 91 kN | 909× |

<KeyIdea>
This is robustness by insensitivity rather than by estimation. Domain
randomisation makes a policy tolerate a parameter's variation; compliance removes
the parameter's influence. When you can arrange the second it is strictly better,
because nothing has to be learned, identified, or covered by a sampling range.
</KeyIdea>

## Why contact is numerically hard

<Derivation title="The stiffness bound on the timestep">

A mass $m$ against a contact spring $k$ has natural frequency
$\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$. Explicit integration of $\ddot{x} = -(k/m)x$ is stable only
for

$$
\Delta t < \frac{2}{\omega} = 2\sqrt{m/k}
$$

For $m = 1$ kg:

| material | $k$ (N/m) | transient $\sqrt{m/k}$ | max $\Delta t$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| soft rubber | $10^4$ | 10 ms | 20 ms |
| hard plastic | $10^6$ | 1 ms | 2 ms |
| aluminium | $10^8$ | 0.1 ms | 0.2 ms |
| steel | $10^9$ | 0.03 ms | 63 µs |

Honest steel demands roughly 16 kHz. Nobody pays that, so simulators either
soften the contact by orders of magnitude or move to an implicit or
complementarity formulation with its own approximations.

</Derivation>

The same $\sqrt{m/k}$ sets the physical timescale, which is where the control
architecture comes from: a 10 Hz policy is 100× slower than a plastic contact
transient and 3000× slower than steel.

## The non-smoothness

Coulomb friction is not a function of velocity — it is a *set-valued* relation:

$$
f_t \in
\begin{cases}
\{-\mu f_n \,\hat{v}\} & \|v_t\| > 0 \quad \text{(sliding)}\\[2pt]
\{f : \|f\| \le \mu f_n\} & \|v_t\| = 0 \quad \text{(sticking)}
\end{cases}
$$

At zero relative velocity the friction force is whatever the constraint requires,
up to the cone limit $\|f_t\| \le \mu \|f_n\|$ — half-angle $\arctan\mu$, so
16.7° at $\mu = 0.3$. Sticking is a constraint, not an equation, which is why
contact simulation is a complementarity problem rather than an ODE.

<Warning>
The practical consequence for learning: the objective is piecewise smooth with
discontinuities at mode changes. A differentiable simulator's pathwise gradient is
exact *within* a mode and carries no information about switching modes — low
variance, real bias. The zeroth-order estimator is unbiased with high variance.
Since more samples cure variance and never cure bias, the apparently primitive
estimator is often the better one here, which is the opposite of the usual
ordering.
</Warning>

## When positioning stops being the problem

If the clearance $c$ is below the positioning uncertainty $\sigma$, no open-loop
command is trustworthy — 50 µm of clearance against ±100 µm of repeatability
means the target is smaller than the error bar. The task becomes a search under
compliance: a chamfer converts lateral offset into a restoring force, and a
spiral sweeps the uncertainty disc while compliance bounds the forces. Perception
supplies the neighbourhood; contact supplies the measurement.
