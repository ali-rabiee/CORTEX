## Keep what already works

<PaperCard refKey="johannink2019residual" />

The framing that makes contact tractable is that you already have a good
controller for most of the task. Free-space motion, gravity compensation and a
nominal impedance are solved problems. What is not modelled is what happens on
contact — friction, jamming, the exact geometry of a chamfer.

So write $\pi(s) = \pi_{\text{base}}(s) + \pi_\theta(s)$ and learn only the
second term. Smaller function, fewer hardware hours, and a safety property worth
noting: with $\pi_\theta \approx 0$ at initialisation the system runs the base
controller, so early exploration perturbs a working policy rather than commanding
arbitrary torques.

<PaperCard refKey="schoettler2020insertion" />

The industrial case, and a useful reality check on tolerances. Connector
insertion has clearances well below what a camera plus calibration delivers,
which is why the interesting part of the work is the reward and the contact
handling rather than the perception.

## The two modalities hand off

<PaperCard refKey="lee2019visiontouch" />

<Steps>
<Step title="Approach: vision only">
Nothing is touching. Force reads zero and carries no information; the camera has
an unobstructed view of the target.
</Step>

<Step title="Contact: the handover">
The gripper and workpiece occlude precisely the region of interest, exactly as
the reaction wrench becomes informative. Both signals change character at the
same instant.
</Step>

<Step title="Insertion: force only">
Alignment is now measured by the wrench and by axial progress. Vision has nothing
left to contribute at the relevant scale.
</Step>

<Step title="Why a joint representation rather than concatenation">
Naive concatenation lets whichever modality had larger training variance dominate
throughout. Self-supervised objectives that relate the streams give the policy a
representation that stays meaningful across the handover.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Why the obvious tool does not work

<PaperCard refKey="suh2022diffsim" />

If simulation is differentiable, differentiate the objective and optimise — that
is the reflex, and for smooth dynamics it is right. Contact makes the objective
piecewise smooth, and the pathwise gradient is exact only within one piece.

<KeyIdea>
The trade is bias against variance, and the asymmetry is what decides it. The
pathwise estimator is low-variance and biased at discontinuities; the zeroth-order
estimator is unbiased with variance falling as $1/\sqrt{N}$. Variance is a budget
problem. Bias is not, and it points confidently in a wrong direction. That is the
principled reason model-free methods stayed competitive on contact tasks long
after differentiable simulators became available — and it is a much better answer
than "differentiable sim is immature".
</KeyIdea>
