interview:
  answer: >-
    Action chunking means the policy predicts the next k actions from one
    observation instead of a single action, and executes them before
    re-planning. The reason it helps is directly tied to why behaviour cloning
    fails. Compounding error scales with the number of independent decisions
    the policy makes, because each one is a chance to nudge itself
    off-distribution — so an episode of T steps with chunk size k has only T
    over k such moments, and the regret term goes from epsilon T squared to
    roughly epsilon T squared over k. It's a constant-factor win rather than a
    change in horizon order, which is what separates it from DAgger, but for k
    around a hundred that constant is enormous. There are two secondary
    benefits that matter as much in practice. Teleoperated data is full of
    pauses and jitter, where nearly identical observations map to contradictory
    tiny actions, and a single-step policy learns to hesitate; predicting a
    whole segment averages over that. And chunking makes the policy commit — if
    the expert was multi-modal, a per-step policy can pick left then right on
    consecutive frames and dither in place, where a chunk commits to one
    coherent plan. The cost is reactivity: during a chunk you're blind, acting
    on an observation that's up to k steps stale, so a mid-chunk disturbance
    gets ignored until the chunk ends. ACT's answer is temporal ensembling —
    predict a fresh chunk every timestep and average all the predictions that
    cover the current one, weighted exponentially by how stale each is. That
    recovers most of the responsiveness. For picking k, I'd tune it against
    rollout success rate rather than validation loss, because longer chunks
    trivially reduce per-step loss by predicting smooth averages while
    performing worse on the robot.
  followUps:
    - q: "Does chunking give you the same guarantee as DAgger?"
      a: >-
        No, and the distinction is worth being precise about. DAgger changes
        which distribution the per-step error is measured under, so the bound
        improves in order — epsilon T squared becomes epsilon T. Chunking
        leaves the T squared structure intact and divides by k, so it's a
        constant factor. What makes it attractive anyway is the cost: DAgger
        needs an online queryable expert, chunking needs a wider output layer.
        On real hardware that trade almost always favours chunking, and the two
        compose fine if you happen to have both.
    - q: "Why not just make k enormous?"
      a: >-
        Because epsilon grows with k. The later actions in a chunk are
        conditioned on an increasingly stale observation, so predicting the full
        sequence accurately gets harder, and you're also blind for longer. The
        optimum is interior — ACT uses around a hundred steps at 50 Hz, so
        roughly two seconds of motion. Past that the tail of the chunk is
        essentially open-loop guessing about a world it hasn't seen.
    - q: "How does chunking interact with multi-modality?"
      a: >-
        It helps with the symptom but doesn't fix the cause. The worst
        expression of mode averaging is per-step dithering — picking left, then
        right, then left as the policy re-decides on nearly identical
        observations, and going nowhere. Chunking removes that by forcing
        commitment for k steps. But the chunk itself is still predicted by
        whatever head you have, and an MSE head still regresses to the mean of
        contradictory demonstrations — you'd just get one smoothly averaged
        wrong trajectory instead of a jittery one. Fixing the cause needs an
        expressive action distribution, which is exactly what Diffusion Policy
        adds on top.
    - q: "Why does chunking matter for large VLA models specifically?"
      a: >-
        It decouples control rate from model size. You cannot run a
        multi-billion-parameter policy at 50 Hz per action, but if one forward
        pass emits two seconds of motion, the inference rate you need drops by
        two orders of magnitude. So chunking isn't just an accuracy trick there
        — it's what makes large policies deployable at all.
  traps:
    - "Saying chunking 'solves' compounding error. It divides the constant by k; the $T^2$ structure is unchanged. DAgger is the one that changes the order."

## Diagnosing chunk-size problems

<Steps>
<Step title="Fails on disturbances, smooth otherwise">
Classic over-chunking. The robot is executing a stale plan. Reduce $k$, or raise
the ensembling decay $m$ so recent predictions dominate.
</Step>

<Step title="Hesitant, jittery, stalls mid-task">
Under-chunking, or no chunking. The policy is re-deciding on near-identical
observations and dithering. Raise $k$ first — it's the cheapest thing you can do.
</Step>

<Step title="Smooth but consistently wrong at one decision point">
Not a chunking problem at all — that's mode averaging. Changing $k$ will produce
a smoothly wrong trajectory instead of a jittery one. Change the action head.
</Step>

<Step title="Validation loss improved but the robot got worse">
Expected when $k$ increased. Believe the robot.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The strongest version of this answer connects chunking back to the
$O(\epsilon T^2)$ bound explicitly, and is honest that it's a constant-factor
improvement. Candidates who present it as "the fix for compounding error"
without that qualifier get asked how it compares to DAgger, and usually
discover mid-sentence that they don't have a crisp answer.
</Warning>
