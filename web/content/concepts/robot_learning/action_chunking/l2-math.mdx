## Reformulating the policy

A single-step policy models

$$
\pi_\theta(a_t \mid o_t)
$$

Chunking simply widens the output to a sequence:

$$
\pi_\theta(a_{t:t+k} \mid o_t)
\qquad\text{where } a_{t:t+k} = (a_t, a_{t+1}, \ldots, a_{t+k-1})
$$

The training objective is unchanged — maximum likelihood on demonstrations, MSE
under a Gaussian head — just summed over the $k$ predicted steps:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{(o_t,\, a_{t:t+k}) \sim \mathcal{D}}
\left[ \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \big\| \pi_\theta(o_t)_j - a_{t+j} \big\|^2 \right]
$$

<KeyIdea>
No new machinery. The loss, the optimiser, the architecture are all the same —
the output layer got $k$ times wider and the dataset returns slices instead of
single actions. That's why chunking is nearly free to adopt.
</KeyIdea>

## Effect on the bound

<Derivation title="Why the gain is a factor of $k$, not a factor of $T$">

Recall the cloning argument: with per-step error $\epsilon$ measured on the
expert's distribution, a first deviation at decision point $t$ can corrupt every
remaining step, giving

$$
\text{regret} \;\lesssim\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} \epsilon (T - t) \;=\; O(\epsilon T^2)
$$

Under chunking, the policy makes an independent decision only every $k$ steps —
there are $T/k$ decision points, not $T$. Within a chunk the actions are jointly
predicted from one observation, so no *new* distribution shift is introduced
mid-chunk. Re-running the sum over decision points $n = 1 \ldots T/k$, where a
deviation at decision $n$ still damages the remaining $T - nk$ steps:

$$
\text{regret} \;\lesssim\; \sum_{n=1}^{T/k} \epsilon\,(T - nk)
\;=\; \epsilon\left(\frac{T^2}{k} - k\cdot\frac{(T/k)(T/k+1)}{2}\right)
\;=\; O\!\left(\frac{\epsilon T^2}{k}\right)
$$

</Derivation>

<Warning>
This is a constant-factor win, not an order-of-horizon win. DAgger changes
$O(\epsilon T^2)$ to $O(\epsilon T)$ by fixing the distribution the error is
measured under; chunking divides by $k$ while leaving the $T^2$ structure intact.
Both are useful and they compose — but claiming chunking "solves" compounding
error is the kind of overstatement an interviewer will push on.
</Warning>

There is a countervailing effect worth naming: as $k$ grows, predicting the whole
sequence accurately gets harder, and the later actions in a chunk are conditioned
on an increasingly stale observation. So $\epsilon$ itself grows with $k$. The
optimum is interior — in ACT, chunk sizes around 100 at 50 Hz, i.e. roughly two
seconds of motion.

## Temporal ensembling

Running chunks back to back means a discontinuity every $k$ steps, and total
blindness in between. Instead, predict a fresh chunk at **every** timestep and
combine all predictions that cover the current step.

At time $t$ you hold predictions for $a_t$ made from observations at
$t, t-1, \ldots, t-k+1$. Index them by age $i$ and average:

$$
\hat{a}_t \;=\; \frac{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1} w_i \, a_t^{(t-i)}}{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1} w_i},
\qquad w_i \;=\; \exp(-m\,i)
$$

<Steps>
<Step title="Small $m$ — heavy averaging">
Old and new predictions weighted almost equally. Very smooth motion, slow to
react to anything new.
</Step>

<Step title="Large $m$ — fresh predictions dominate">
Approaches per-step closed-loop control. Responsive, but recovers less of
chunking's smoothing benefit.
</Step>

<Step title="The cost is compute, not correctness">
You now run inference every timestep instead of every $k$ — the same rate as an
unchunked policy. What you keep is the coherence of predicting a whole segment.
</Step>
</Steps>
