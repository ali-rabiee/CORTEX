## The idea

A standard visuomotor policy looks at an image and outputs one action. Then it
looks again and outputs one more. At 20 Hz for 30 seconds that's 600 separate
decisions, each an opportunity to drift.

Action chunking predicts the next $k$ actions from a single observation and
executes them before looking again.

<Intuition>
Reaching for a mug, you don't re-plan your arm trajectory every 50 milliseconds
based on fresh visual input. You glance once, commit to a motion, and only look
again near the end — or if something surprises you.

Single-step policies re-decide constantly, and that constant re-deciding is
where hesitation, jitter, and drift come from.
</Intuition>

## Why it helps so much

<Steps>
<Step title="It divides the number of decision points">
Behaviour cloning's error compounds because each independent decision can nudge
the robot off-distribution. With chunk size $k$, an episode of $T$ steps has only
$T/k$ such moments. The $O(\epsilon T^2)$ term becomes roughly
$O(\epsilon T^2/k)$ — for $k = 100$, a large win for no extra data.
</Step>

<Step title="It absorbs pauses and jitter in human demonstrations">
Teleoperated data is full of moments where the human hesitated or held still. A
single-step policy sees near-identical observations mapped to contradictory
tiny actions, and learns to stall. Predicting a whole segment averages over that
noise instead of being confused by it.
</Step>

<Step title="It makes the policy commit">
Multi-modality hurts most when the policy re-decides constantly — it can pick
"left" then "right" on consecutive frames and dither in place. A chunk commits
to one coherent plan for its duration.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
Chunking is the cheapest fix in imitation learning. It needs no extra data, no
online expert, and no architectural sophistication — just a wider output layer
and a slightly different dataset index. That cost-to-benefit ratio is why it
appears in essentially every modern real-robot policy.
</KeyIdea>

## What it costs

<Warning>
During a chunk the robot is blind. It executes a plan formed from an observation
that may be up to $k$ steps stale. If the object slips or a human intervenes
mid-chunk, the robot finishes the wrong plan first.
</Warning>

So chunk size is a genuine trade-off: larger $k$ means less drift but slower
reaction. The usual resolution is **temporal ensembling** — predict overlapping
chunks at every timestep and average all the predictions that apply to the
current one. You get the commitment of chunking with something closer to
per-step responsiveness, at the price of running inference every step.
