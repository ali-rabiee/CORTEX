## What it is

Reinforcement learning hands you a score and asks you to discover good behaviour.
Imitation learning hands you **examples of good behaviour** and asks you to
reproduce it.

That swap is why imitation dominates real-robot work. Reward engineering is
notoriously fiddly, and exploration on hardware is slow, expensive, and
occasionally destructive. Showing a robot what you want is usually easier than
writing down what you want.

<Intuition>
Two ways to teach someone to make an omelette. Give them a scoring rubric and
let them cook a thousand omelettes, telling them only the score each time. Or
cook a few in front of them.

The second is imitation learning. It's faster, it needs no rubric — and the
learner will never get better than you.
</Intuition>

## The three families

They differ in *what* you copy from the demonstrations.

<Steps>
<Step title="Copy the actions — behaviour cloning and DAgger">
Treat demonstrations as labelled data and fit a policy directly. Simple,
supervised, and by far the most used. Its weakness is distribution shift: the
policy is trained on the expert's states and tested on its own.
</Step>

<Step title="Infer the intent — inverse reinforcement learning">
Assume the demonstrations are near-optimal for some unknown reward, recover that
reward, then run RL on it. This *can* exceed the demonstrator and transfers to
new situations, because a reward generalises further than a set of trajectories.
The price is a hard, under-determined inference problem — many rewards explain
the same behaviour — plus an RL loop inside every iteration.
</Step>

<Step title="Treat demos as a dataset — offline RL">
If your demonstrations carry rewards, or you have failures as well as successes,
you can run RL entirely on the fixed dataset. Unlike cloning, this can be better
than the average demonstration by stitching together good segments from
different trajectories.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
Choosing between them is really a question about what you have. Only good demos
and no reward → cloning. Demos plus a queryable expert → DAgger. Demos with
rewards, or mixed-quality data → offline RL. Need to exceed the demonstrator and
can afford the machinery → inverse RL, or imitation as initialisation followed
by RL.
</KeyIdea>

## What imitation cannot do

Two limits are structural and worth stating before an interviewer draws them out:

<Warning>
**It cannot exceed the demonstrator.** Cloning has no notion of "better" — only
"same." If your human teleoperator is clumsy, your policy is clumsy.

**It has no data where the demonstrator never went.** Good experts don't make
mistakes, so they never demonstrate recovery — which is precisely the behaviour a
deployed policy most needs.
</Warning>

Both are why the field keeps returning to the same two moves: get data from
somewhere other than a flawless expert (DAgger, perturbation, offline RL on mixed
data), or add a reward once imitation has given you a competent starting point.
