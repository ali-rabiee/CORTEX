## The problem statement

You are given demonstrations $\mathcal{D} = \{\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_N\}$, where
each $\tau = (s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \ldots, s_T)$ was produced by an expert policy
$\pi^*$. Find a policy $\pi$ that behaves like $\pi^*$.

Note what is absent: no reward function appears anywhere. Every method below is
a different answer to *"behaves like" in what sense?*

## Three objectives

<Steps>
<Step title="Behaviour cloning — match actions, on the expert's states">

$$
\min_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi^*}}
\Big[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi^*(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)\big) \Big]
$$

The expectation is over $d^{\pi^*}$, the expert's state distribution — which is
the only distribution you have samples from. That is both why it reduces to
supervised learning and why it says nothing about states the learner reaches.
</Step>

<Step title="Distribution matching — match state-action occupancy">

Define the occupancy measure $\rho^\pi(s,a) = (1-\gamma)\sum_t \gamma^t
P(s_t = s, a_t = a \mid \pi)$: how often the policy visits each state-action
pair. Then

$$
\min_\theta \; D\big(\rho^{\pi_\theta} \,\|\, \rho^{\pi^*}\big)
$$

for some divergence $D$. GAIL takes $D$ to be a Jensen-Shannon divergence
estimated by a discriminator, which turns imitation into a GAN. Because
$\rho^{\pi_\theta}$ can only be estimated by rolling out $\pi_\theta$, this
family requires environment interaction.
</Step>

<Step title="Inverse RL — recover the reward">

Find $r$ such that

$$
\pi^* \in \arg\max_\pi \; \mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\textstyle\sum_t \gamma^t\, r(s_t, a_t)\Big]
$$

then run ordinary RL on the recovered $r$. A reward is a far more compact and
transferable description of intent than a set of trajectories — which is the
appeal, and why it can generalise to new dynamics or new initial states.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
Reading the three side by side: cloning conditions on the expert's states and
matches actions; distribution matching abandons conditioning and matches the
joint. That is precisely the move that fixes covariate shift — and precisely why
it costs environment interaction.
</KeyIdea>

## Why inverse RL is ill-posed

<Derivation title="Two rewards that explain any demonstration">

**Constant reward.** Let $r(s,a) = c$. Every policy achieves identical return,
so every policy is optimal, so $\pi^*$ is optimal. Any demonstration whatsoever
is explained.

**Potential-based shaping.** For any $\Phi: \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}$, define

$$
r'(s,a,s') \;=\; r(s,a,s') \;+\; \gamma\,\Phi(s') \;-\; \Phi(s)
$$

Along any trajectory the added terms telescope, changing every policy's return
by the same constant $-\Phi(s_0)$. So $r'$ and $r$ have exactly the same optimal
policies, for every $\Phi$ — an infinite family of rewards producing identical
behaviour.

</Derivation>

The fix is to add a selection principle. **Maximum-entropy IRL** picks the reward
under which the demonstrations have highest likelihood while the induced policy
is as high-entropy as possible — formally, model the expert as
$p(\tau) \propto \exp\big(\sum_t r(s_t,a_t)\big)$ and fit $r$ by maximum
likelihood. That resolves the ambiguity by preferring explanations that don't
assume the expert was more deliberate than the data supports.

<Warning>
"Imitation learning" and "behaviour cloning" are not synonyms, and using them
interchangeably is a cheap tell. Cloning is one algorithm inside a family that
also contains DAgger, GAIL, and the whole IRL literature — each defined by a
different notion of what it means to match the expert.
</Warning>
