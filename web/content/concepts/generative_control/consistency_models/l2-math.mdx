## Definition

Let $\{x_\sigma\}$ be a trajectory of the probability-flow ODE, running from a
noise sample at $\sigma_{\max}$ down to $\sigma = \epsilon \approx 0$. The
consistency function is

$$
f(x_\sigma, \sigma) = x_\epsilon \quad \text{for all } \sigma \text{ on that trajectory}
$$

Two conditions follow. **Self-consistency**: any two points on one trajectory map
to the same output. **Boundary**: $f(x, \epsilon) = x$, since a point already at
the clean end is its own endpoint.

## Why the second condition carries the objective

<Derivation title="Self-consistency is satisfied by every constant function">

Take $f(x,\sigma) = c$ for any fixed $c$. For any trajectory and any two levels
$\sigma_a, \sigma_b$:

$$
f(x_{\sigma_a}, \sigma_a) - f(x_{\sigma_b}, \sigma_b) = c - c = 0
$$

The consistency loss is identically zero, for every trajectory, exactly. Not
approximately — the constraint is blind to whether the map depends on its input
at all.

Verified numerically on trajectories generated by integrating the PF-ODE, taking
$\sigma_a = 8$ and $\sigma_b = 2$ on the same path:

| | residual | sample $W_1$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| exact $f$ | $3.3\times10^{-4}$ | 0.007 |
| $f \equiv 0$ | $0.0$ | 2.00 |

The degenerate solution beats the correct one on the objective, since the correct
one's residual is only nonzero from finite-step integration.

Now impose the boundary condition. $f(x,\epsilon) = x$ forces $c = x$ for every
$x$, which no constant satisfies. **The assumption doing the work is that the
trajectory is deterministic**, so that "the endpoint" is well defined and the
anchor at $\epsilon$ propagates back along the path.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
Because the failure mode is a perfect minimiser of one term, the anchor cannot
safely be a second loss term with a weight — under-price it and collapse is
optimal. The standard parameterisation makes it an identity:

$$
f_\theta(x,\sigma) = c_{\text{skip}}(\sigma)\,x + c_{\text{out}}(\sigma)\,F_\theta(x,\sigma),
\qquad c_{\text{skip}}(\epsilon) = 1,\; c_{\text{out}}(\epsilon) = 0
$$

Every parameter setting satisfies the boundary condition, so the optimiser only
ever searches functions that already do.
</KeyIdea>

## Training

Two routes, differing in where the pair of points on a trajectory comes from.

<Steps>
<Step title="Distillation: the teacher supplies the pair">
Take $x_{\sigma_{n+1}}$, run one solver step of a pretrained diffusion model to
get $\hat{x}_{\sigma_n}$, and minimise
$d\!\left(f_\theta(x_{\sigma_{n+1}}, \sigma_{n+1}),\, f_{\theta^-}(\hat{x}_{\sigma_n}, \sigma_n)\right)$
against a stop-gradient target.
</Step>

<Step title="Standalone: an unbiased estimate replaces the teacher">
Using the fact that the score can be estimated from a single noisy sample, adjacent
points can be constructed without a teacher — at the price of a noisier target.
</Step>

<Step title="Either way the target is a moving copy of the network">
$\theta^-$ is an EMA of $\theta$. Training against your own current output is the
part that is delicate, and it is why the schedule of noise levels and the EMA
rate get so much attention in the follow-up work.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Distillation error is additive rather than averaged. The student's target is
whatever the teacher's solver produces between adjacent levels, so the teacher's
discretisation error is not noise around a correct answer — it *is* the answer
being taught. The student then adds its own approximation error. This is why the
teacher's step count inside the distillation loop sets a quality floor, and why a
second-order solver there is usually worth its extra evaluation.
</Warning>

## What multi-step sampling can and cannot fix

The sampler alternates $x_0 \leftarrow f(x,\sigma)$ with re-noising
$x \leftarrow x_0 + \sigma' z$ at a smaller $\sigma'$. Its only lever is the level
at which the next evaluation happens, so it helps precisely to the extent that
the model's error depends on $\sigma$.

| error model | 1 step | 2 steps | 3 steps |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| bias growing with $\sigma$ | 1.102 | 0.299 | 0.143 |
| stronger bias at high $\sigma$ | 1.602 | 0.430 | 0.206 |
| i.i.d. noise, sd 0.15 | 0.0245 | 0.0281 | 0.0306 |
| i.i.d. noise, sd 0.30 | 0.0878 | 0.0912 | 0.0944 |

A $7.7\times$ improvement in the first case and a slight *degradation* in the
last. The mechanism is that the corrective pass runs where the map is easy; an
error that is redrawn identically at every level has nowhere easier to go.
