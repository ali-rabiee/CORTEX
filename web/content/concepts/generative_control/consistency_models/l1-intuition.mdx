## Skip the integration

<Intuition>
Diffusion and flow models both give you a trajectory from noise to data, and
sampling means following it. Every technique for making that cheaper — better
integrators, straighter paths — is still about following it well. Consistency
models ask a different question: if the trajectory is deterministic, then every
point on it has the same endpoint, so why not learn the map from any point
directly to that endpoint? Then generation is one function evaluation and there
is no trajectory to follow at all.
</Intuition>

Write $f(x, \sigma)$ for that map. Self-consistency is the property that makes it
well-defined: for any two noise levels $\sigma_a, \sigma_b$ on the same
trajectory, $f(x_{\sigma_a}, \sigma_a) = f(x_{\sigma_b}, \sigma_b)$. Train the
network to agree with itself along trajectories and you never need to know the
endpoint in advance.

## The constraint that isn't one

<Figure src="/figures/consistency-map-vs-steps.svg" alt="Left: several points on one probability-flow trajectory all mapping to the same endpoint, with the measured finding that a constant function has a self-consistency residual of exactly zero while producing a sample error of 2.0. Right: error against sampling steps, falling 7.7-fold for a bias that grows with noise level and rising slightly for independent noise." caption="Self-consistency is satisfied perfectly by a function that ignores its input. The boundary condition is what carries the objective." />

Here is the catch, and it is worth checking rather than taking on faith. Take
$f \equiv 0$. For any two points on any trajectory, $f$ returns zero for both, so
they agree exactly. Measured on a real trajectory:

| | self-consistency residual | sample $W_1$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| exact $f$ | 0.00033 | 0.007 |
| constant $f \equiv 0$ | **0.00000** | **2.00** |

The degenerate solution scores *better* on the constraint than the correct one —
whose residual is nonzero only because of integration error.

<KeyIdea>
So the boundary condition $f(x, \epsilon) = x$ at the clean end is not a technical
detail; it is the entire content of the objective. In practice it is enforced by
the parameterisation, $f_\theta(x,\sigma) = c_{\text{skip}}(\sigma)\,x +
c_{\text{out}}(\sigma) F_\theta(x,\sigma)$ with $c_{\text{skip}}(\epsilon)=1$ and
$c_{\text{out}}(\epsilon)=0$, so the constraint holds by construction rather than
by a penalty the optimiser could trade away.
</KeyIdea>

## Why anyone takes a second step

If one evaluation is the whole point, a two-step sampler looks like an admission
of defeat. It is really a targeted repair.

<Steps>
<Step title="Denoise, re-noise, denoise again">
Apply $f$ to get an estimate of $x_0$, add noise back at a *smaller* $\sigma$,
and apply $f$ again.
</Step>

<Step title="The second application is an easier problem">
It runs at low noise, where the map is a short extrapolation rather than a jump
from pure noise — so wherever the model is weakest, it is used least.
</Step>

<Step title="Which fixes bias concentrated at high noise">
Against a systematic error growing with $\sigma$, the measurement gives 3.7×
improvement at two steps and 7.7× at three.
</Step>

<Step title="And does nothing for independent error">
Against per-evaluation noise, error crept slightly *upward* with more steps —
each application contributes a fresh draw rather than cancelling the last.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
That asymmetry makes the two-step result a diagnostic rather than a default. If
adding a step helps a lot, your model's weakness is at the high-noise end and
more capacity or training there should pay. If it changes nothing, the error is
not $\sigma$-dependent and you should look elsewhere — at the conditioning path,
or at the teacher if you distilled.
</Warning>
