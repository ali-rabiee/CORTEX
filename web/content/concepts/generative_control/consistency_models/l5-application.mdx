  prompt: "Give your interview answer on consistency models. What is the objective, and what is the trap in it?"
  keyPoints:
    - "Learn the map from any point on a PF-ODE trajectory to its endpoint, so sampling is one evaluation rather than an integration."
    - "The trap: self-consistency alone is vacuous — a constant function scores exactly zero and generates noise."
    - "The boundary condition carries the objective, and it is enforced by the parameterisation so it cannot be traded away."
    - "Multi-step sampling corrects $\\sigma$-dependent bias (7.7× at three steps) and slightly worsens independent noise — so it is a diagnostic, not a default."
interview:
  answer: >-
    The setup is that a diffusion or flow model gives you a deterministic
    trajectory from noise to data, and every technique for making sampling
    cheaper — better integrators, straighter paths — is still about following
    that trajectory well. Consistency models ask a different question: if the
    trajectory is deterministic, every point on it has the same endpoint, so
    learn the map from any point directly to that endpoint. Then generation is
    one function evaluation and there's no path to follow. The property that
    defines it is self-consistency — two points on the same trajectory must map
    to the same output — which is nice because you can train it without knowing
    the endpoint in advance, just by making the network agree with itself along
    a path. And that's where the trap is, which I think is the most interesting
    thing about the method. Self-consistency on its own is vacuous. Take f
    identically zero: for any two points on any trajectory it returns zero for
    both, so they agree exactly. I checked this numerically — the constant
    function scored a self-consistency residual of exactly zero, better than the
    true solution's three-times-ten-to-the-minus-four, which is only nonzero
    because of integration error. And its samples had a Wasserstein distance of
    2.0 against 0.007 for the real map. So the degenerate solution is a perfect
    minimiser of the constraint. What rules it out is the boundary condition,
    that f of x at near-zero noise returns x. That's the entire content of the
    objective, and it's why it's built into the parameterisation — f equals
    c-skip times x plus c-out times the network, with c-skip one and c-out zero
    at the clean end — rather than being a loss term. As a loss term it's one
    objective among two with a weight, and since the collapse is optimal for the
    other one, under-pricing the anchor makes collapse the right answer. As an
    identity it can't be traded. The related detail is that the training target
    is an EMA copy of the network under a stop-gradient, for the same reason: if
    you differentiate through the target, the model can satisfy the objective by
    moving both sides onto a constant instead of getting the map right. It's the
    same argument as a target network in value-based RL. And the last thing I'd
    mention is when a second sampling step helps, because it isn't always. The
    multi-step sampler denoises, re-noises at a smaller sigma, and denoises
    again — so its only lever is putting the next evaluation where the map is
    easier. I measured that: against a bias that grows with sigma it gave seven
    point seven times lower error at three steps, and against error that's
    independent noise at every level, more steps made it slightly worse. So a
    flat two-step result is informative — it tells you the weakness isn't at the
    high-noise end.
  followUps:
    - q: "When would you pick this over just using a better ODE solver?"
      a: >-
        It depends where the step budget is. A second-order solver like Heun is
        free — no retraining — and in a controlled test it reached Euler's
        hundred-and-sixty-evaluation error in about forty, so a fourfold saving.
        But it floors out: below roughly twenty evaluations higher order stops
        helping and actually starts hurting, because the corrector evaluates at a
        point the oversized step has made unreliable. So if twenty to forty
        evaluations fits your budget, use the solver and don't add a training
        stage. If you need one or two — which for a robot policy is set by the
        control rate, not by patience — then you're in distillation territory and
        consistency or rectification is what gets you there.
    - q: "Distillation or standalone consistency training?"
      a: >-
        Distillation if you already have a good diffusion model, because the
        target is well-defined and the optimisation is much better behaved. The
        thing to watch is that distillation error is additive rather than
        averaged — the student's target is whatever the teacher's solver produces
        between adjacent levels, so the teacher's discretisation error isn't
        noise around a correct answer, it is the answer being taught, and the
        student adds its own on top. That makes the teacher's step count inside
        the distillation loop a real hyperparameter with a quality floor
        attached. Standalone training removes the teacher, which is the more
        striking claim, but it took a follow-up paper reworking the noise
        schedule, the distance metric and the EMA before it matched distillation.
        That's worth knowing as a caution: a lot of whether it works lives in
        details the original framing doesn't foreground.
    - q: "How would you debug a distilled policy that underperforms its teacher?"
      a: >-
        Profile the error as a function of noise level rather than looking at the
        aggregate loss, because the aggregate can't distinguish two cases that
        want opposite responses. If the error rises with sigma, the hard end of
        the map is undertrained — add a sampling step, weight those levels more,
        or use a better solver in the distillation loop. If it's flat, extra
        steps will buy nothing and can cost a little, so the problem is capacity,
        conditioning, or the teacher itself. It's one cheap sweep and it decides
        the next week of work.
    - q: "Does this cost you multimodality? One step from noise to a single output sounds like a mode collapse risk."
      a: >-
        In principle no, because different noise inputs map to different modes —
        the map is deterministic but the input is random, so the pushforward is
        still multimodal. The stochasticity moved from the sampling loop to the
        initial draw. In practice it's a fair worry, since the student is fitting
        a map that has to change sharply between basins, and any smoothing
        appears exactly as an averaged action between modes — which is the
        failure diffusion policies were adopted to avoid. So I'd measure it
        rather than assume: sample many actions for one observation and check
        the distribution is still multimodal, not just that individual samples
        look plausible.
  traps:
    - "Presenting self-consistency as the objective. On its own it is satisfied exactly by any constant function; the boundary condition is what determines the map."
    - "Making the boundary condition a weighted loss term. Since collapse is a perfect minimiser of the other term, the weight becomes the difference between a model and a failure."
    - "Assuming more sampling steps always help. They correct $\\sigma$-dependent bias and slightly worsen independent error."
    - "Treating distillation error as noise that averages out. The teacher's solver error is the target, so it composes with the student's."

Folding the condition into $c_{\\text{skip}}$ and $c_{\\text{out}}$ removes the trade-off entirely, since every reachable parameter setting already satisfies it."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Samples are constant or near-constant → the anchor is not binding">
Check that the boundary condition is in the parameterisation, and that the
distillation target has a stop-gradient. Both failures produce the same collapse.
</Step>

<Step title="Two steps help a lot → the high-noise end is undertrained">
Weight those levels more, add capacity, or improve the teacher's solver.
</Step>

<Step title="Two steps change nothing → the error is not $\sigma$-dependent">
Extra evaluations will not pay. Look at conditioning, capacity, or the teacher.
</Step>

<Step title="Student below teacher by a fixed margin → distillation floor">
The teacher's discretisation error is the target. Raise the solver order inside
the distillation loop before touching the student.
</Step>

<Step title="Actions look averaged between modes → measure, do not assume">
Sample many actions for one observation and check the distribution, since a
smoothed map reintroduces exactly the failure diffusion was adopted to avoid.
</Step>
</Steps>

<InterviewAnswer />

<Warning>
The single strongest move here is volunteering that the objective is vacuous
without the boundary condition, with the constant-function argument. It takes ten
seconds, it is checkable on paper, and it explains three design decisions at once
— the skip parameterisation, the stop-gradient, and the EMA target. An answer
that describes self-consistency as *the* objective is the common one, and it
misses the part where the method is actually interesting.
</Warning>
