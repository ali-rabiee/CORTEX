## The move that makes it work

Every policy so far in this cluster has been a function: observation in, action
out. Diffusion Policy breaks that. Its network does not know how to produce an
action. It only knows how to **improve a guess**.

You hand it a garbage action sequence — literally Gaussian noise — along with
the current camera image and a number saying how corrupted you believe the
guess is. It hands back a correction. You apply the correction, get a slightly
less garbage sequence, and hand it back in. After ten or so passes you have a
trajectory you can send to the robot.

<Intuition>
Think of the network as a compass rather than a map. It cannot tell you where
the destination is. Standing anywhere in action space, it can only tell you
which way is *downhill toward plausible expert behaviour*. Follow the compass
from a random starting point and you arrive somewhere sensible.

Start from a different random point and you may arrive somewhere else sensible —
which is exactly the property we could not get out of regression.
</Intuition>

## How you train it

The surprising part is that learning a compass is easier than learning a map,
and the training loop is *shorter* than behaviour cloning's.

<Steps>
<Step title="Take a real action chunk from a demonstration">
Say the next 16 actions the human commanded, stacked into one vector. Call it
$a^0$ — the clean sample.
</Step>

<Step title="Corrupt it by a random amount">
Draw a noise level $k$ uniformly from $1 \ldots K$, draw Gaussian noise
$\epsilon$, and blend: mostly-clean at low $k$, essentially pure noise at $k=K$.
You now have a corrupted chunk $a^k$ and you know exactly what noise made it,
because you drew it.
</Step>

<Step title="Ask the network to name the noise">
Feed it $a^k$, the level $k$, and the observation. Train it to output
$\epsilon$. That is a plain mean-squared-error regression — the same loss as
behaviour cloning, pointed at a different target.
</Step>

<Step title="At test time, run it backwards">
Start from pure noise. Predict the noise, subtract a step of it, repeat. Each
pass corresponds to one $k$, counting down. What falls out at $k = 0$ is a
sample from the distribution of expert action chunks for this observation.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
The objective is still supervised learning on a fixed set of demonstrations. No
reward, no environment interaction, no online expert. Diffusion Policy is
behaviour cloning — it just swapped a head that predicts *the* action for a
procedure that samples *an* action.
</KeyIdea>

## Why anyone bothered

Two failures of naive cloning, both fixed at once.

**Mode averaging.** Regression is pinned to the conditional mean. If your
demonstrations disagree — and human demonstrations always disagree, because
there are usually several fine ways to do a thing — the mean is a compromise
that may be worse than every option it compromises between. Sampling does not
average. It picks.

**Jittery, incoherent motion.** A per-timestep policy re-decides from scratch at
every control tick, and small observation changes can flip it between modes
mid-motion, producing a shudder that looks like indecision because it *is*
indecision. Diffusion Policy denoises the whole chunk as one object, so temporal
consistency is structural rather than something you hope for.

<Warning>
The cost is compute at inference. A hundred denoising steps at 10 Hz is not
happening. The practical systems use DDIM sampling to get down to roughly 10
steps, receding-horizon execution so you re-plan only every few actions, and —
more recently — consistency distillation to get to one or two steps. If an
interviewer asks about diffusion policies and you never mention latency, you
have signalled that you have not deployed one.
</Warning>
