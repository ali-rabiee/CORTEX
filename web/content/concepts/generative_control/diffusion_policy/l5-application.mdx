interview:
  answer: >-
    Diffusion Policy is behaviour cloning where the regression head is replaced
    by a conditional generative model over action chunks. Instead of mapping an
    observation to an action, the network takes a noisy action sequence, a
    noise level, and the observation, and predicts the noise that was added.
    You train it by taking a real chunk from a demonstration, corrupting it by
    a random amount, and regressing on the noise — so it's still plain
    supervised learning on a fixed dataset, same objective family as ordinary
    cloning. At test time you start from Gaussian noise and run the network
    maybe ten times, each pass removing a bit of noise, and what falls out is a
    sample from the distribution of expert action chunks for that observation.
    Two things that buys you. First, multi-modality: the predicted noise is a
    scaled estimate of the score, the gradient of the log density, so sampling
    is annealed gradient ascent that lands in a mode. Mean squared error can't
    do that at any capacity, because its unique minimiser is the conditional
    mean — if half your demonstrations go left around an object and half go
    right, regression is obliged to emit the average, which goes through it.
    Second, it denoises a whole chunk jointly, so you get temporal coherence
    for free and the same reduction in decision points that action chunking
    gives you. The cost is inference: you're paying K forward passes per
    decision instead of one. That's managed with DDIM sampling to cut a hundred
    steps to ten, receding-horizon execution so the cost amortises over several
    actions, and more recently consistency distillation or flow matching to get
    down to one or two steps — which is what pi-zero does to keep an action
    head running at fifty hertz under a VLM.
  followUps:
    - q: "Does diffusion fix compounding error?"
      a: >-
        Only indirectly, and I'd be careful not to overclaim it. The chunking
        helps — fewer decision points means fewer chances to step
        off-distribution, which is the same win ACT gets. But the training
        distribution is still the expert's own state distribution, and nothing
        in the diffusion formulation adds data where the policy actually fails.
        If the policy drifts into a state no demonstration ever visited, the
        score model is being queried off-distribution and will confidently
        sample something wrong. Mode averaging and covariate shift are separate
        problems, and diffusion is squarely a fix for the first.
    - q: "How do you pick the number of denoising steps?"
      a: >-
        Empirically, against a latency budget, not from the paper's number. I'd
        fix the control rate I need and work backwards: one vision encoder pass
        plus K denoiser passes has to fit in the time between re-plans, divided
        by however many actions I execute per chunk. Then I'd sweep K down from
        a hundred and watch task success, not sample likelihood. What you
        typically see is that success is flat down to around ten DDIM steps and
        then degrades — the samples get smoother and start to look like the
        mean again, which is exactly the failure you adopted diffusion to
        avoid. If ten is still too slow, that's when distillation is worth the
        extra training complexity.
    - q: "When would you not use a diffusion policy?"
      a: >-
        When the demonstrations are effectively unimodal, which happens when a
        single operator does a well-constrained task the same way every time.
        Then there's no mean to average badly and you're paying ten times the
        inference cost for nothing — a chunked regression policy is the right
        baseline and often matches it. Also when the latency budget is hard and
        the compute is embedded, and when I have very little data, since a
        generative model over sequences has more to learn than a regressor. I'd
        want the regression-with-chunking baseline measured first; if it fails
        specifically at the states where demonstrations disagree, that's the
        evidence that justifies the switch.
    - q: "Your diffusion policy chatters — it reaches toward an object, retracts, and reaches again. What's happening?"
      a: >-
        Almost certainly mode switching across re-plans. Each chunk is a fresh
        sample, so if the observation sits near a decision boundary the policy
        can draw the left-hand strategy at one re-plan and the right-hand one
        at the next, and the two partially-executed chunks fight each other.
        That's the flip side of representing modes honestly. The fixes are
        raising the number of actions executed per chunk so a decision commits
        for longer, temporally ensembling overlapping chunk predictions the way
        ACT does, or warm-starting the sampler from the previous chunk's tail
        so consecutive samples are correlated rather than independent.

## Reading the symptoms

Diffusion policies fail in ways that look nothing like regression policies
failing, and the symptom usually names the cause.

<Steps>
<Step title="Chattering or oscillation between two behaviours">
Mode switching across re-plans. Every chunk is an independent sample, so near a
decision boundary consecutive re-plans can commit to different strategies.
Raise $T_a$ so a decision holds for longer, ensemble overlapping chunks, or
correlate consecutive samples by warm-starting from the previous chunk's tail.
</Step>

<Step title="Smooth, confident, wrong — right at the ambiguous state">
Under-resolved sampling. Too few denoising steps relaxes the sample toward the
mean, which only bites where the density is multi-modal. Put the step count
back up and confirm the failure disappears before reaching for anything else.
</Step>

<Step title="Nonsense output from the very first control step">
Suspect normalisation. The sampler starts by drawing $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, which
is only the right terminal distribution if the training actions were order-1.
Check that `act_mean` / `act_std` were computed over the training set, saved,
and applied in both directions.
</Step>

<Step title="Lag against anything that moves">
Reactivity, not sample quality. Set by how often you look at the world, so it
is $T_a$ — not $K$ and not $T_p$. Both of those make the sample better and the
lag worse.
</Step>

<Step title="No better than the regression baseline">
Possibly correct, and worth saying out loud. If the demonstrations are
unimodal, there is no averaging failure to fix and diffusion is paying $K\times$
inference for nothing. Diagnose by checking whether the baseline fails
*specifically* at states where demonstrations disagree.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The strongest single move in this answer is being exact about what the network
predicts. "It predicts the noise, which up to a scale is the score of the
conditional action distribution, and the action comes out of the sampler" tells
an interviewer you have implemented one. "It generates actions with diffusion"
does not distinguish you from someone who has read the abstract — and the
follow-up will be about latency, where that distinction becomes visible
immediately.
</Warning>
