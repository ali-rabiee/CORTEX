## The objective

Pick an interpolant. The simplest is linear:

$$
x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1, \qquad x_0 \sim p_{\text{noise}},\; x_1 \sim p_{\text{data}}
$$

Conditioned on the endpoints, the velocity is immediate:
$u_t(x \mid x_0, x_1) = x_1 - x_0$. What generates the correct marginals, though,
is the *marginal* field

$$
u_t(x) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\,u_t(x \mid x_1) \;\middle|\; x_t = x\,\right]
$$

which is a posterior expectation over which data point you are heading towards —
not something you can evaluate.

<Derivation title="Why regressing the conditional target suffices">

Compare the two objectives:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_t}\left\|v_\theta(x_t,t) - u_t(x_t)\right\|^2,
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_1, x_t}\left\|v_\theta(x_t,t) - u_t(x_t \mid x_1)\right\|^2
$$

Expand both. The quadratic term $\|v_\theta\|^2$ is identical. The cross terms:

$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\langle v_\theta, u_t(x_t \mid x_1)\rangle\right]
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[\langle v_\theta, \mathbb{E}[u_t(x_t \mid x_1) \mid x_t]\rangle\right]
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[\langle v_\theta, u_t(x_t)\rangle\right]
$$

by the tower property, since $v_\theta(x_t,t)$ is measurable with respect to
$x_t$. So the two differ only in their last terms:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}} - \mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}
= \mathbb{E}\left[\|u_t(x_t \mid x_1)\|^2\right] - \mathbb{E}\left[\|u_t(x_t)\|^2\right]
$$

a conditional variance — positive, and with **no $\theta$ in it**. Hence
$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}} = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}$.

**The assumption doing the work is that $v_\theta$ depends only on $x_t$ and $t$**,
never on $x_1$. That is what licenses the conditioning step, and it is why you
cannot pass the endpoint to the network as a shortcut.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
The minimum of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}$ is not zero — it is the conditional
variance, which can be large. A flow matching loss that plateaus well above zero
is behaving correctly, and reading that plateau as underfitting is a standard
misdiagnosis. It is the same situation as a denoising loss that cannot go below
the noise variance.
</KeyIdea>

## Why the trajectory bends

The conditional paths are straight. The field they induce is not.

<Derivation title="Averaging directions that disagree">

For the linear interpolant with $x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$, the marginal
velocity works out to

$$
u_t(x) = \frac{\mathbb{E}[x_1 \mid x_t = x] - x}{1-t}
$$

Consider a symmetric two-mode target and a point $x$ near the origin at small
$t$. Conditional paths through it are heading to the left mode and to the right
mode in roughly equal proportion, so $\mathbb{E}[x_1 \mid x_t] \approx 0$ and the
marginal velocity is small — while every individual conditional velocity is
large. As $t$ grows the posterior sharpens onto one mode and the velocity turns
towards it.

That turn is the curvature. Integrating an averaged field is not averaging the
integral curves, and the two differ exactly where the fields disagree.

</Derivation>

Measured on such a target, with $u_t$ computed in closed form so there is no
network error: the trajectory's mean distance from its own chord is $0.254$
against a mean displacement of $1.202$ — **21% relative curvature**.

And the consequence, against a variance-preserving diffusion path on the same
target:

| NFE | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| diffusion (VP) | 1.201 | 0.303 | 0.063 | 0.027 | 0.0146 | 0.0086 |
| linear interpolant | 1.999 | 0.636 | 0.244 | 0.123 | 0.0623 | 0.0314 |

<Warning>
Clustering the integration grid near $t=1$, where the $1/(1-t)$ factor makes the
velocity large, makes this *worse* rather than better — 0.593 against 0.244 at
four steps. So the penalty is genuine path geometry and not a discretisation
artefact that a smarter grid would remove. It is worth checking this kind of
alternative explanation before attributing a result to the mechanism you expected.
</Warning>

## Rectification

Reflow changes the coupling, not the interpolant. Run the current ODE to obtain
matched pairs $(x_0, \Phi(x_0))$, then retrain on the linear interpolant between
each matched pair. Because $\Phi$ is a deterministic map, the paths no longer
cross, the marginal velocity at a point equals the conditional one, and the
trajectory *is* the straight line.

One Euler step then reproduces the target to $W_1 = 0.0036$, against a
sampling-noise floor of $0.0037$ — exact to the resolution of the measurement,
and roughly $550\times$ better than one step on the original coupling.
