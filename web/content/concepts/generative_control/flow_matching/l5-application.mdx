interview:
  answer: >-
    Flow matching learns a velocity field that carries noise onto data, rather
    than a score. They're close relatives — the probability-flow ODE of a
    diffusion model is exactly such a field — but starting from transport makes
    the path an explicit choice instead of something you inherit from a noising
    process. The real contribution is the training objective. The velocity you
    want is a posterior expectation over which data point you're heading towards,
    which you can't evaluate. But conditioned on a data point, the velocity along
    your interpolant is closed-form algebra — for the linear path it's just x1
    minus x0. The theorem is that regressing the conditional target has the same
    gradient as regressing the intractable marginal one, because when you expand
    both squares the quadratic terms are identical, the cross terms agree by the
    tower property, and what's left over is a conditional variance with no
    parameters in it. So it vanishes under differentiation. That's the same
    manoeuvre that lets denoising score matching stand in for score matching, and
    it's what made continuous normalising flows trainable at scale — previously
    you had to integrate the ODE inside the training loop. Now, the thing I'd
    push back on is the usual sales pitch, which is that flow matching gives you
    straight paths so you can take a few large steps. The first half is true and
    the second doesn't follow. Each conditional path is a straight line, but the
    sampler follows the marginal field, which at any point averages over every
    conditional path through it — and averaging directions that disagree gives a
    field whose integral curves bend. I measured this on a one-dimensional
    mixture with the field computed exactly, so there's no network error: the
    trajectory sits twenty-one percent of its total displacement away from its
    own chord. And on the same target, plain linear interpolation was worse than
    a variance-preserving diffusion path at every step budget from one to
    thirty-two — by about a factor of three. I also checked whether that was a
    discretisation artefact by clustering the grid where the velocity is largest,
    and it got worse, so it's genuine geometry. Where one-step generation
    actually comes from is rectification. Reflow runs the ODE to get matched
    pairs, then retrains on the straight line between each matched pair. Since
    that's a deterministic map, distinct points go to distinct points, the paths
    stop crossing, and the marginal velocity equals the conditional one — so the
    trajectory really is the straight line and one Euler step is exact rather
    than approximate. In my measurement, one step after rectification hit the
    sampling-noise floor, about five hundred times better than one step before
    it. So the speed is real, it comes from the coupling rather than the
    interpolant, and it costs a full generation pass plus a retraining stage.
  followUps:
    - q: "So would you use flow matching or diffusion for a robot policy?"
      a: >-
        For the first version I'd pick flow matching, but not for speed — for
        simplicity. The sampler is a plain ODE integration with no schedule
        object, no conversion from predicted noise to score, and no sigma
        spacing to get wrong, and a whole class of quiet bugs comes from
        training and sampling deriving noise levels independently. Then if the
        control rate needs single-digit steps I'd add rectification as a second
        stage, with the measurement to justify it. What I wouldn't do is claim
        flow matching is faster out of the box, because on the plain interpolant
        it isn't — and pi-zero using a flow model is about the training and
        conditioning story, not about the interpolant being faster.
    - q: "Your flow matching loss plateaus well above zero. Is the model underfitting?"
      a: >-
        Almost certainly not. The conditional target is a noisy estimate of the
        field you're learning, and the objective's minimum is exactly the
        conditional variance of that estimate, not zero. A perfectly fitted
        network sits on that plateau by construction. The practical consequence
        is that absolute loss values carry no information for this objective —
        only comparisons at a fixed interpolant and data distribution do — so I'd
        judge it by sample quality and by the curvature diagnostic rather than by
        the number. It's the same situation as a denoising loss that can't go
        below the noise variance, and people misread both the same way.
    - q: "How would you know in advance whether few-step sampling will work?"
      a: >-
        Measure the straightness directly rather than sweeping step counts.
        Integrate finely, then compute the mean distance from the trajectory to
        the chord between its own endpoints, normalised by the displacement. It's
        one two-hundred-step run and it predicts what a full sweep would tell
        you. On my mixture it gave twenty-one percent for the independent
        coupling and essentially zero after rectification. It's also the right
        number to decide whether reflow is worth a training stage — if curvature
        is already low, it isn't.
    - q: "Are diffusion and flow matching actually different models?"
      a: >-
        Largely not, and I think that's the honest position. Both learn a
        time-dependent vector field whose integral curves transport noise to
        data; you can map between the score parameterisation and the velocity
        parameterisation, and a diffusion model's probability-flow ODE is a flow.
        The genuine differences are in framing and in what each framing makes
        easy to vary. Flow matching puts the interpolant in your hands, which is
        what makes rectification even expressible — it's a statement about the
        coupling, and diffusion's formulation doesn't have a natural place to say
        it. So I'd call it the same family with a more convenient
        parameterisation, rather than a rival.

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Few-step samples poor, many-step samples good → curvature">
Measure deviation from the chord before changing anything. If it is high,
rectification is the fix and a better solver is not.
</Step>

<Step title="Loss plateaus above zero → expected">
The minimum is the conditional variance. Compare runs to each other, never to
zero.
</Step>

<Step title="Quality degrades at HIGH step counts after reflow → over-rectification">
The model is training on its own outputs. One round is usually the limit; watch
the high-budget number, not the low one.
</Step>

<Step title="Flow matching no faster than diffusion → nothing is wrong">
That is the measured baseline for the plain interpolant. The speed was always in
the rectification.
</Step>

<Step title="Samples collapse to an average → check the conditioning path">
The formulation represents multimodality fine. Suspect an MSE head elsewhere or a
conditioning signal that is being ignored.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
This is a question where the strongest move is disagreeing with the common
summary, carefully. Nearly everyone repeats "straight paths, hence few steps".
Separating the conditional path from the marginal path, giving a measured
curvature, and then locating the speed correctly in the coupling shows you have
looked at the object rather than the abstract. Just make sure to give flow
matching its actual due — the simulation-free objective and the explicit
interpolant — so it reads as precision rather than contrarianism.
</Warning>
