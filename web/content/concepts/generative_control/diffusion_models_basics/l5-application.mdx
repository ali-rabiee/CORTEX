interview:
  answer: >-
    The description everyone gives is add noise gradually and learn to remove it,
    which is right and hides the structure that matters. The useful framing is
    that the network only ever learns one object — the score of the noised data
    distribution at each noise level — and everything else is a separable choice
    made around it. How fast you add noise, whether you undo it stochastically or
    deterministically, how many steps, which integrator. Most of those can be
    changed after training, which is why the field moved so fast: a lot of what
    look like competing model families are different answers to how you get from
    noise to a sample given a score. The result that makes that concrete is the
    probability-flow ODE. The forward process is an SDE, the reverse is an SDE
    with a score term, but there's also a deterministic ODE with exactly the same
    marginals, and it's the reverse drift with half the score coefficient. The
    half isn't a fudge — you write the Fokker-Planck equation, express the
    second-order diffusion term as the divergence of a probability current, and
    half falls out of turning a Laplacian into a gradient. The trajectories are
    completely different from the SDE's; only the distributions match at each
    time. That single equation is what DDIM, higher-order solvers, consistency
    distillation and the flow matching connection all act on. Then two things I'd
    bring numbers for. First, the noise schedule is a capacity allocation, not a
    detail, because timesteps are sampled uniformly during training — so the
    schedule decides the distribution of difficulties the network sees. Under a
    linear beta schedule alpha-bar is down to 0.08 by the halfway point, so the
    data is ninety-two percent destroyed and about fifty-four percent of your
    training steps are at a signal-to-noise ratio where the optimal prediction is
    nearly the prior. The cosine schedule cuts that to twenty-three and moves
    roughly thirty percent of the steps into the informative band. Same model,
    same thousand steps. And it's easy to miss because those wasted steps produce
    small losses — the average looks healthy. Second, sampling is a numerical
    integration problem, so the step count is an accuracy question rather than a
    hyperparameter. I tested this on a one-dimensional mixture with an exact
    score so the only error was discretisation: Euler halved its error per
    doubling of steps, first order, while a second-order Heun sampler fell about
    fivefold and matched Euler's hundred-and-sixty-evaluation error at forty. So
    a better integrator is worth about four times the step count, for free, after
    training.
  followUps:
    - q: "If better integrators help that much, why did anyone need consistency models?"
      a: >-
        Because the order argument is asymptotic in the step size, and it stops
        applying exactly where you need it. In the same test Heun was worse than
        Euler at ten evaluations — more than twice as bad — because the corrector
        evaluates the score at a point the oversized predictor step has already
        made unreliable. So higher order extends the usable budget downwards and
        can't reach single digits: the trajectory genuinely curves, and no
        integrator resolves a curve in one step. Distillation attacks something
        different — it learns the endpoint map directly instead of trying to
        integrate the path better, which is why it can get to one or two steps
        when solvers plateau around twenty.
    - q: "How would you pick a schedule for a new domain?"
      a: >-
        I'd stop parameterising by t and sample log-SNR directly with explicit
        bounds, because that makes the allocation a stated choice instead of a
        side effect of the schedule's algebra. Then the question becomes what
        range of signal-to-noise actually matters for the data, which is
        domain-specific and answerable — for robot action chunks the useful band
        sits differently than for images, partly because the data dimension is so
        much lower. And I'd check the loss per SNR bucket rather than the
        aggregate, since a schedule wasting half its steps still shows a healthy
        average loss. The aggregate can't distinguish capacity well spent from
        capacity spent on a trivial regime.
    - q: "What's the practical difference between the stochastic and deterministic samplers?"
      a: >-
        The ODE is deterministic, so it's invertible — you can map a sample back
        to its latent, which is what makes editing and interpolation work, and
        the paths are smooth enough to take large steps. The SDE injects noise at
        every step, which gives some self-correction: it can recover from
        discretisation error the way a deterministic path can't, so at high step
        counts stochastic sampling is often slightly better on quality while
        being useless for anything requiring an inverse. In robot policies I'd
        default to the ODE, because the step budget is the binding constraint at
        control rate and determinism is worth having when you're debugging why a
        policy did something.
    - q: "Is the score the only thing being learned? What about conditioning?"
      a: >-
        Conditioning changes which score — you learn the score of the conditional
        distribution rather than the marginal, so the network takes the
        observation as an extra input and everything else is unchanged. The
        interesting wrinkle is guidance: you can push samples toward stronger
        conditioning by extrapolating along the difference between the
        conditional and unconditional score. That's a sampling-time knob, so it
        trades diversity against fidelity to the condition without retraining —
        and for action generation it's a knob to use carefully, because
        collapsing diversity is precisely the failure that made people reach for
        diffusion policies in the first place.
  traps:

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Low loss, poor samples → look per noise level">
An aggregate is dominated by the easy low-SNR regime. Bucket the loss by
log-SNR before touching the architecture.
</Step>

<Step title="Samples plausible but distribution wrong → schedule mismatch">
Sampler and training levels derived separately. Both must read one schedule
object.
</Step>

<Step title="Quality collapses below ~20 steps → integrator order, not model">
Higher-order methods lose their advantage at large steps. Fix the spacing first,
then consider distillation.
</Step>

<Step title="Blurry or averaged outputs → check this is a diffusion failure at all">
Mode averaging is what diffusion prevents. If it persists, suspect the
conditioning path or an MSE head elsewhere in the stack.
</Step>

<Step title="Sampling slow but quality fine → spacing before solver">
$\rho$-spaced levels are free and often worth more than the integrator at small
budgets.
</Step>
</Steps>

The framing does most of the work in this answer. Saying that the network learns
only the score, and that schedule, sampler and step count are separable, lets you
answer half a dozen follow-ups from one idea — and it is what makes "you can
change that after training" a principle rather than a trick. Adding the two
measured numbers, the 54% of wasted schedule and the fourfold integrator saving,
turns a conceptual answer into one that sounds like it came from having run the
experiments.
