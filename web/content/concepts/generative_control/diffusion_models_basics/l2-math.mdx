## The forward process as an SDE

Write the noising process in continuous time:

$$
dx = f(x,t)\,dt + g(t)\,dw
$$

DDPM's discrete chain is one discretisation of this with a particular $f$ and
$g$; the variance-exploding formulation is another. Anderson's result gives the
reverse-time process:

$$
dx = \left[f(x,t) - g(t)^2 \nabla_x \log p_t(x)\right] dt + g(t)\,d\bar{w}
$$

The only unknown is $\nabla_x \log p_t(x)$, the score. That is the whole learning
problem, and it is what a noise-prediction network computes up to a scaling.

## Why the deterministic sampler exists

<Derivation title="The probability-flow ODE, and where the one half comes from">

The density under the forward SDE evolves by Fokker–Planck:

$$
\frac{\partial p_t}{\partial t} = -\nabla \!\cdot\! \left(f p_t\right) + \frac{1}{2} g^2 \nabla^2 p_t
$$

We want a *deterministic* process with the same $p_t$. A deterministic flow with
velocity $v$ obeys the continuity equation

$$
\frac{\partial p_t}{\partial t} = -\nabla \!\cdot\! \left(v\, p_t\right)
$$

so we need to write the diffusion term as a divergence. Using
$\nabla p_t = p_t \nabla \log p_t$:

$$
\frac{1}{2}g^2 \nabla^2 p_t
= \frac{1}{2}g^2 \nabla \!\cdot\! \left(\nabla p_t\right)
= \nabla \!\cdot\! \left(\frac{1}{2}g^2 p_t \nabla \log p_t\right)
$$

Substituting and matching:

$$
v(x,t) = f(x,t) - \frac{1}{2} g(t)^2 \nabla_x \log p_t(x)
$$

**The assumption doing the work is that we only need the marginals to match**,
not the trajectories. The ODE's individual paths are nothing like the SDE's — they
are smooth and non-crossing — but at every $t$ the distribution is identical.

</Derivation>

Compare the two coefficients: $g^2$ in the reverse SDE, $\tfrac{1}{2}g^2$ in the
ODE. Half the score compensates exactly for the removed noise.

<KeyIdea>
This is the structural fact the rest of the field builds on. One trained score
gives you a stochastic sampler and a deterministic one; the deterministic path is
smooth enough to integrate with large steps, to invert for editing, and to
distil into a direct map. DDIM is a discretisation of this ODE, flow matching
learns a straighter version of the same kind of velocity field, and consistency
models learn its endpoint map — three ideas that look unrelated until you see
they all act on this one equation.
</KeyIdea>

## The schedule, in log-SNR

Define $\lambda(t) = \log\frac{\bar\alpha_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}$, the log
signal-to-noise ratio. Since $t$ is drawn uniformly during training, the schedule
determines the induced distribution over $\lambda$ — that is, over how hard each
training example is.

| band | linear $\beta$ | cosine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\lambda > 5$ (nearly clean) | 2.1% | 4.5% |
| $-2 < \lambda \le 5$ (informative) | 43.5% | 72.8% |
| $\lambda \le -2$ (nearly pure noise) | 54.4% | 22.7% |

At $t = T/2$: $\bar\alpha = 0.079$ under the linear schedule, $0.494$ under the
cosine one.

<Warning>
The reason this is easy to miss is that the loss looks fine either way. Training
at $\lambda \ll 0$ is *easy* — predicting the noise from almost pure noise is
nearly the identity — so those steps produce small losses and contribute a
healthy-looking average while teaching very little. A loss curve cannot
distinguish capacity well spent from capacity spent on a trivial regime, which is
why the schedule deserves inspection rather than inheritance.
</Warning>

## Sampling is numerical integration

Solving the probability-flow ODE from noise to data costs one network evaluation
per function evaluation, so sample quality against NFE is a convergence question.

<Figure src="/figures/sampler-order-convergence.svg" alt="Log-log plot of Wasserstein error against network evaluations for Euler and Heun samplers on a mixture with an exact score. Euler halves per doubling; Heun falls about fivefold per doubling and reaches Euler's 160-evaluation error at 40. Below 20 evaluations Heun is worse." caption="Measured with the score known exactly, so every bit of the error is discretisation." />

| NFE | 10 | 20 | 40 | 80 | 160 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Euler | 0.175 | 0.080 | 0.039 | 0.021 | 0.012 |
| Heun | 0.392 | 0.062 | 0.011 | 0.0022 | at floor |

Euler's error falls by about $2\times$ per doubling — first order, as expected.
Heun falls by about $5\times$, and reaches Euler's 160-evaluation error at 40:
**a fourfold saving in evaluations at matched quality**, from changing the
integrator alone.

And the crossover at the left end is the honest part. At 10 evaluations Heun is
more than twice as bad as Euler, because its correction is evaluated at a point
the oversized predictor step has already made unreliable. Order is an asymptotic
statement about small $h$, so better integrators push the usable budget down and
cannot reach the single-digit regime — which is the gap distillation was built to
close.
