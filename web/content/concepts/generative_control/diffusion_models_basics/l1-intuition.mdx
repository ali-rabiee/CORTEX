## One process, several algorithms

<Intuition>
The standard description — add noise gradually, then learn to remove it — is
right and it hides the structure that matters. What the network learns is a
single object: the score of the noised data distribution at each noise level.
Everything else is a choice made around it. How fast you add noise, whether you
undo it stochastically or deterministically, how many steps you take, and which
numerical integrator you use are all separable decisions, and most of them can be
changed after training.
</Intuition>

That separation is why the field moved so fast. DDIM, higher-order samplers, flow
matching and consistency distillation are not competing model families so much as
different answers to "given a score estimate, how do you get from noise to a
sample".

## The schedule is a capacity allocation

Timesteps are sampled uniformly during training, so the noise schedule decides
what fraction of the model's training is spent at each difficulty.

<Figure src="/figures/diffusion-schedule-capacity.svg" alt="Left: surviving signal fraction against normalised time — the linear schedule falls to 0.08 by the halfway point while the cosine schedule is at 0.49. Right: share of training steps per signal-to-noise band, with the linear schedule spending 54 percent on nearly pure noise against the cosine schedule's 23 percent." caption="Same thousand steps, about thirty percent of them reallocated from where nothing is learnable to where the denoising problem is informative." />

Under the linear $\beta$ schedule, $\bar\alpha$ is already down to **0.08** by
$t = T/2$ — the data is 92% destroyed at the halfway point, and the remaining
half of the schedule works on what is nearly pure noise. Counting steps by
signal-to-noise band:

| schedule | nearly clean | informative | nearly pure noise |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| linear | 2.1% | 43.5% | **54.4%** |
| cosine | 4.5% | **72.8%** | 22.7% |

<KeyIdea>
Nothing about the model changed. The cosine schedule simply moves about 30% of
the training steps out of a regime where the optimal prediction is nearly the
prior and into one where the input actually determines the answer. That is why a
schedule change — usually presented as a minor detail — produced one of the
larger single improvements in the DDPM line.
</KeyIdea>

## Where the cost is

<Steps>
<Step title="Training is cheap per sample">
One forward pass at one randomly drawn noise level. No sampling loop, no
adversarial game, no simulation of the reverse process.
</Step>

<Step title="Sampling is a differential equation">
Generating one sample means integrating the reverse process from noise to data,
which needs one network evaluation per step. The original formulation used 1000.
</Step>

<Step title="So the step count is an accuracy question">
It is not a hyperparameter to tune by feel — it is how finely you discretise an
ODE, with a discretisation error that shrinks in a known way.
</Step>

<Step title="Which means a better integrator buys steps">
In a controlled test where the exact score was available, a second-order Heun
sampler reached Euler's 160-evaluation error in about 40 — roughly a fourfold
saving for a change of a few lines.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The same test showed the opposite at very low budgets: at 10 evaluations Heun was
*worse* than Euler, because the second-order correction is evaluated at a point
the large step has already moved somewhere unreliable. Higher order helps once
the steps are small enough to be locally accurate, and hurts before that — which
is why single-digit-step generation needed a different idea rather than a better
integrator.
</Warning>
