interview:
  answer: >-
    The framing I'd start with is that generating an action chunk is generating
    one joint object, not H predictions — and the reason that distinction earns
    its keep is that you can get every per-timestep marginal exactly right and
    still produce something no robot could execute. The cleanest way to see it is
    to hold the marginals fixed and vary only the dependence structure. Suppose
    the expert reaches one of two goals with a smooth profile, so at each
    timestep the correct action is one of two values. If you sample each timestep
    independently from its correct marginal, the probability that the whole chunk
    stays on one mode is M to the one minus H — two to the one minus H for two
    modes. That's twelve and a half percent at a four-step horizon and about
    three thousandths of a percent at sixteen. I measured what the other
    ninety-nine point nine nine seven percent look like: thirty-one times the
    expert's jerk, and because the sign flips cancel, a net displacement of 0.24
    against the expert's 1.00. The robot vibrates in place and goes almost
    nowhere, while every marginal check passes. So it's the same independence
    failure you get from per-dimension action bins, with time as the dimension —
    except far worse, because the penalty compounds exponentially in the horizon
    rather than sitting at a fixed fraction. And the consequence I'd want to draw
    out is that action chunking and generative action heads aren't two
    independent design decisions. At horizon one there's no temporal structure to
    get wrong, so a regression head loses nothing on that axis. At horizon sixteen
    ignoring the joint destroys almost every sample. Chunking is what turns a
    generative head from a refinement into a requirement, which is why ACT and
    Diffusion Policy adopted both at the same time rather than by coincidence. I'd
    add one caution about vocabulary: sequential isn't the same as independent. An
    autoregressive head over timesteps factorises by the chain rule, which is
    exact — each step conditions on what was already committed to, so the first
    sample effectively picks the mode and the rest follow. Its costs are H
    sequential forward passes and exposure bias, not incoherence. A diffusion or
    flow head over the whole chunk avoids both by generating every timestep
    together.
  followUps:
    - q: "How would you validate an action-generation head?"
      a: >-
        Not with per-timestep marginals, because that check has no power — a
        model with the failure matches them by construction. I'd measure
        sequence-level statistics: jerk, net displacement, and the fraction of
        sampled chunks that violate velocity or acceleration limits. The most
        direct test is the distance from each sampled chunk to its nearest
        demonstrated trajectory, treating the whole chunk as one vector, because
        a chunk that switches modes has every step near some demonstration and
        the whole sequence near none. And I'd calibrate it by running the
        demonstrations against themselves first, so the threshold isn't what's
        producing the result.
    - q: "How do you handle the seam between consecutive chunks?"
      a: >-
        The problem is that consecutive chunks are independent samples from a
        multi-modal conditional, so a policy can commit to going left and then,
        eight steps later, to going right. Temporal ensembling — averaging the
        overlapping predictions for a given timestep — spreads that transition
        over several steps instead of concentrating it in one. But I'd be careful,
        because blending is itself an averaging operation, so averaging across a
        genuine mode switch produces the action neither mode would take. That's
        the failure the generative head exists to prevent, reintroduced at the
        seam. Weighting newer predictions more heavily limits it, and if seams
        are frequently the problem, the better fix is usually replanning less
        often so each chunk runs further.
    - q: "Absolute or delta actions?"
      a: >-
        It depends, and I'd resist the reflex that deltas are simply better. The
        argument for them is that an absolute target is start pose plus motion,
        so its distribution convolves the skill with wherever the arm happened to
        be. I measured that for one motion — reach eight centimetres forward,
        four millimetres of execution noise, start poses over a thirty-centimetre
        workspace — and the absolute parameterisation had about nineteen hundred
        times the variance, eleven nats more entropy, to describe the same
        behaviour. So the model spends capacity on location rather than skill.
        Against that, deltas are cumulative: an error at step three displaces
        every later target, so open-loop execution of a long chunk drifts where
        absolute targets self-correct. And anything anchored to a fixed frame —
        place this in that bin — is more natural absolutely. It's a real trade, so
        it deserves a decision rather than a default.
    - q: "Does any of this change if you use a VLA with discrete action tokens?"
      a: >-
        The requirement is identical and the mechanism differs. A token-per-
        timestep model generated autoregressively is fine, because the chain rule
        factorisation is exact and each token conditions on the previous ones —
        so coherence comes from the conditioning. What would break it is
        predicting all the timesteps' tokens independently in one shot, which is
        the same 2-to-the-1-minus-H failure wearing different clothes. The other
        thing to get right is that the tokens should quantise joint actions —
        clustered action vectors or a codebook over chunks — rather than binning
        each dimension separately, since per-dimension bins reintroduce the
        independence failure across dimensions while you're busy fixing it across
        time.

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Jittery motion, little progress, marginals fine → temporal independence">
The signature of modelling timesteps separately. Check sequence statistics, not
per-step ones.
</Step>

<Step title="Motion reverses every few steps at a fixed period → seam switching">
Consecutive chunks are choosing different modes. Blend the overlap or replan less
often.
</Step>

<Step title="Chunks exceed velocity or acceleration limits → no feasibility check">
Individually plausible actions can be an infeasible path. Measure it on samples
before hardware does.
</Step>

<Step title="Good early in the chunk, drifting later → cumulative delta error">
The cost of the delta parameterisation. Shorten the horizon or move to absolute
targets for that task.
</Step>

<Step title="Model is accurate but slow to sample → this is the latency question">
Step budget is set by the control rate. That is the integrator, coupling and
distillation trade, not an action-representation problem.
</Step>
</Steps>
