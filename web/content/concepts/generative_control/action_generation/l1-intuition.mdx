## A chunk is one object, not $H$ predictions

<Intuition>
Predicting a sequence of actions looks like predicting several actions, and that
framing is what breaks. What makes a chunk a *trajectory* is the relationship
between its timesteps: consecutive actions are strongly correlated, the sequence
has to be dynamically feasible, and — crucially — the whole chunk has to commit
to one way of doing the task. A model that gets every timestep right in isolation
can still produce something no robot could execute and no expert would attempt.
</Intuition>

The cleanest way to see it is to take the marginals as given and vary only the
dependence structure.

<Figure src="/figures/action-sequence-independence.svg" alt="Left: two smooth expert modes reaching opposite goals, and an independently sampled chunk that dithers around the middle with 31 times the jerk and a quarter of the distance travelled. Right: the probability an independently sampled chunk stays on one mode, falling as two to the one minus H from 12.5 percent at horizon 4 to 0.003 percent at horizon 16." caption="Every per-timestep marginal is exactly correct in the red trajectory. That buys nothing." />

Suppose the expert reaches one of two goals with a smooth minimum-jerk profile,
so at each timestep the action is one of two values. Sample each timestep
independently from its correct marginal and the chunk stays on a single mode with
probability

$$
P(\text{coherent}) = 2^{\,1-H}
$$

| $H$ | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| coherent | 12.5% | 0.78% | **0.003%** | $5\times10^{-10}$ |

What the incoherent 99.997% look like, measured: **31×** the expert's jerk, and a
net displacement of **0.24** against the expert's 1.00. The robot dithers and
goes almost nowhere, while every marginal check passes.

<KeyIdea>
This is the per-dimension independence failure from multimodal action
distributions, with time as the dimension — and it is much more severe, because
the penalty compounds exponentially in the horizon instead of sitting at a fixed
50%. Which yields the connection worth remembering: **action chunking and
generative action heads are not two independent design choices.** At $H=1$ there
is no temporal structure to lose. At $H=16$ ignoring it destroys almost every
sample. Chunking is what turns a joint model from a refinement into a
requirement, and it is why ACT and Diffusion Policy adopted both at once.
</KeyIdea>

## What the model has to get right

<Steps>
<Step title="Mode coherence across the chunk">
One way of doing the task, held for the whole horizon. This is the failure that
scales as $2^{1-H}$.
</Step>

<Step title="Dynamic feasibility">
Bounded velocity, acceleration and jerk. A sequence can be individually plausible
at every step and exceed the arm's limits as a path.
</Step>

<Step title="A parameterisation that does not fragment the distribution">
The same reaching motion executed from different start poses has **1877×** the
variance as an absolute target pose than as a delta. Under absolute actions the
spread of the distribution is the workspace rather than the skill.
</Step>

<Step title="Seams between consecutive chunks">
Chunks are re-predicted before the previous one finishes, and independent mode
choices across chunks reverse the motion. Temporal ensembling averages
overlapping predictions to smooth the handover.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The delta parameterisation is not a free win, and presenting it as one is a
mistake. Deltas accumulate: an error early in the chunk shifts everything after
it, so open-loop execution drifts in a way absolute targets do not. Tasks
anchored to a fixed frame — place the object in *this* bin — are also more
naturally expressed absolutely. The honest statement is that the parameterisation
determines what the model must learn, and deltas move workspace variation out of
the learning problem at the cost of making errors cumulative.
</Warning>
