## Coherence under independence

Let the expert have $M$ discrete ways of doing the task, and suppose a model
predicts each timestep of an $H$-step chunk independently with the correct
marginal.

<Derivation title="Why the penalty is exponential in the horizon">

At each timestep the marginal places mass $1/M$ on each mode's value (taking them
equally likely). Sampling independently, the probability that all $H$ steps
select the same mode is

$$
P(\text{coherent}) = M \cdot \left(\frac{1}{M}\right)^{H} = M^{\,1-H}
$$

For $M = 2$:

| $H$ | 1 | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $2^{1-H}$ | 1 | 0.125 | 0.0078 | $3.1\times10^{-5}$ | $4.7\times10^{-10}$ |

**The assumption doing the work is that the modes remain distinguishable at every
timestep.** Where they coincide — at the start of a reach, before the paths
separate — a wrong choice costs nothing, so this is an upper bound on the damage
rather than an exact account. It is the right shape regardless: coherence decays
geometrically in $H$.

Measured on minimum-jerk trajectories to two opposite goals, $H = 16$, 200k
samples: coherent fraction $2\times10^{-5}$ against the predicted $3\times10^{-5}$
— consistent at this sample size, where about six hits were expected.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
Note what the incoherent samples do rather than just how many there are. Measured
mean absolute second difference — a jerk proxy — was $0.143$ against the expert's
$0.0046$, a factor of **31**. And because the sign flips cancel, net displacement
was $0.24$ against $1.00$. The robot vibrates in place. This is not a mildly
degraded trajectory; it is a qualitatively different behaviour, produced by a
model whose every marginal is exactly right.
</KeyIdea>

## The same statement in nats

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p(a_{1:H}) \,\big\|\, \textstyle\prod_t p(a_t)\right)
= \sum_{t} H(a_t) - H(a_{1:H})
$$

the total correlation across time. For a correlated sequence each additional
timestep contributes roughly a constant amount of redundancy, so the gap grows
about linearly in $H$ — which is the $M^{1-H}$ result read as information rather
than as a probability.

<Warning>
Sequential and independent are different things, and conflating them is a common
error. The chain rule
$p(a_{1:H}) = \prod_t p(a_t \mid a_{<t})$ is exact and discards nothing, so an
autoregressive head over timesteps has no independence failure — the mode is
decided by the first sample and the conditioning carries it forward. Its costs
are elsewhere: $H$ sequential forward passes at inference, and exposure bias from
training on ground-truth prefixes. A diffusion or flow head over the whole chunk
avoids both by generating all timesteps together.
</Warning>

## What the parameterisation costs

Write the action as an absolute target pose, $a_t = x_{\text{start}} + \Delta_t$,
versus as the delta $\Delta_t$ itself.

<Derivation title="Absolute actions carry the workspace distribution">

If start pose and motion are independent,

$$
\mathrm{Var}[a] = \mathrm{Var}[x_{\text{start}}] + \mathrm{Var}[\Delta]
$$

The first term has nothing to do with the skill. Measured for one motion — reach
8 cm forward, execution noise 4 mm — performed from start poses spread over a
$\pm 30$ cm workspace:

| parameterisation | total variance | differential entropy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| absolute pose | $0.0900$ | $-1.00$ nats |
| delta | $0.00005$ | $-12.31$ nats |

A ratio of **1877×** in variance, and 11.3 nats of entropy the model must
represent to describe the same behaviour.

</Derivation>

So under an absolute parameterisation the generative head spends capacity
modelling where the arm happened to be. The conditioning can in principle recover
this — the observation contains the current pose — but it is a harder function
to learn for no task-relevant reason.

<Warning>
Deltas are not strictly better and it matters to say why. They are *cumulative*:
an error at step 3 displaces every subsequent target, so open-loop execution of a
long chunk drifts where absolute targets self-correct. Tasks anchored to a fixed
frame are also more naturally absolute. The real content of the result is that
the parameterisation determines what the model must learn, so it deserves a
decision rather than a default.
</Warning>
