  prompt: "Give your interview answer on multimodal action distributions. What are the two measurements you bring?"
  keyPoints:
    - "MSE's optimum is the conditional mean, which for two modes has no expert support — 0.00% of demonstrations within 0.3 of it."
    - "It is a specification problem: more data and more capacity compute the wrong quantity better."
    - "No loss escapes it, since every $\\mathbb{E}[\\ell(a-f(o))]$ has a minimiser that is a summary of the conditional."
    - "Per-dimension bins get every marginal right and put 50% of joint mass on combinations that never occurred."
interview:
  answer: >-
    The situation is that at the same observation the expert sometimes does one
    thing and sometimes another — goes left or right around an obstacle, grasps
    from either side — and both are correct. A regression head can't express
    that. If you train with squared error, the optimum is the conditional mean of
    the action given the observation, which you can show in two lines: expand the
    expected square, differentiate, and the minimiser is E of a given o
    regardless of the shape of that distribution. So for two modes it's the
    average of them. The part I'd emphasise is that this isn't a compromise
    between two good actions — it's an action with no support. I measured it on
    a two-mode distribution with modes at plus and minus one: not a single expert
    action fell within 0.3 of the mean, while forty-eight percent fell that close
    to a mode. And in a real task the valley between "go left" and "go right" is
    usually the obstacle. The framing that follows is that this is a
    specification problem rather than an estimation problem. More demonstrations
    estimate the conditional mean more precisely; a bigger network represents it
    more faithfully. Both make you better at computing the wrong quantity, which
    is why "collect more data" is the wrong instinct here even though it's right
    for most other failures. Then there are two fixes people reach for that don't
    work, and I think knowing why is the interesting part. First, switching to L1
    — the minimiser becomes the conditional median, which does at least sit
    inside a mode. But it's discontinuous in the mode balance: as the mixture
    weight crosses one half it jumps from one mode to the other, so near-balanced
    modes give you a target that sampling noise decides. I measured the sample
    median's standard deviation going from 0.007 at a sixty-forty split to 0.65
    at fifty-fifty. And the symmetric case is exactly the one that motivates the
    problem, so the fix is weakest where you need it. Second, and this one is
    subtler — discretise each action dimension and put a softmax on it. That
    feels like it solves multimodality, and every per-dimension marginal does
    come out correctly bimodal. But independent heads represent a product of
    marginals, so the correlations are gone. I ran a two-dimensional case where
    the expert only ever played left-up or right-down: both marginals were right,
    and fifty percent of the joint mass landed on the two combinations that never
    occurred. The general statement is that the KL gap is exactly the mutual
    information between action dimensions, so finer bins don't help. What that
    leaves is that the output has to stop being a point and the model has to be
    joint — a mixture, an energy, a codebook over whole action vectors, or a
    diffusion head.
  followUps:
    - q: "Couldn't you design a better loss?"
      a: >-
        No, and I think that's a clean thing to be able to say. Any loss of the
        form expectation of ell of a minus f of o has a minimiser that's some
        functional of the conditional distribution — squared error gives the
        mean, absolute error the median, quantile loss a quantile, certain
        non-convex penalties a mode. Every one collapses a distribution to a
        number. That's a consequence of asking a deterministic function to
        answer, not of which penalty you picked, so loss engineering is
        misdirected effort here. The move that changes the situation is making
        the output a distribution, and once you see that, the whole zoo of
        answers — latent-variable policies, implicit BC, behaviour transformers,
        diffusion — is one idea with different machinery.
    - q: "How would you check whether your data is multimodal in the first place?"
      a: >-
        Conditionally, which is the part people skip. The marginal action
        distribution is broad in every robot dataset because the task has phases,
        so a histogram of all actions is not evidence. I'd embed observations,
        take k nearest neighbours in observation space, and look at the spread of
        their actions — disagreement among neighbours is what predicts a
        regression head will fail. And that localises it to specific states,
        which is more useful than a dataset-level verdict: usually the
        multimodality lives at a handful of decision points and the rest of the
        trajectory is fine.
    - q: "Your policy samples look right per dimension. Is it working?"
      a: >-
        That check has no power against the failure I'd be worried about,
        because a model that factorises across dimensions matches every marginal
        by construction — it's precisely what the broken model does well. I'd
        compare something joint instead: take each sampled action and measure
        the distance to its nearest demonstrated action, and report the fraction
        with nothing nearby. Calibrate on the demonstrations against themselves
        first, so you know the radius isn't the thing generating your result.
        I'd also sample many actions at a single fixed observation and check
        they're actually diverse, because a policy that deterministically picks
        one mode looks fine on success rate whenever either mode works, and
        fails silently when the choice is context-dependent.
    - q: "If diffusion solves this, why does anyone still use discrete methods?"
      a: >-
        Mostly inference cost and the fit to existing machinery. A binned or
        codebook head is one forward pass, where a diffusion head is an
        integration — and for a policy the step budget is set by the control
        rate. Discrete tokens also drop straight into a transformer that's
        already doing next-token prediction, which is why VLA models tend to go
        that route rather than bolting on a sampler. The thing to get right is
        that the discrete units have to be joint actions — clustered action
        vectors or a learned codebook over chunks — not per-dimension bins. Get
        that wrong and you've reintroduced the independence failure while
        believing you solved the problem.
  traps:
    - "Describing the averaged action as a compromise. It has essentially no expert support — measured at 0.00% within a generous tolerance."
    - "Proposing more data or a bigger model. This is a specification problem; both compute the wrong quantity better."
    - "Offering L1 as the fix. Its minimiser is discontinuous in the mode balance and unstable exactly at the symmetric case."
    - "Treating per-dimension discretisation as solving it. Every marginal matches while half the joint mass lands on combinations that never occurred."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Policy drives into the obstacle it should go around → mode averaging">
Check whether the demonstrations disagree at that observation. If they do, the
head is the problem, not the data.
</Step>

<Step title="Fails only at specific decision points → conditional multimodality">
Rank states by neighbour action spread. Multimodality is usually local, so a few
states explain most of it.
</Step>

<Step title="Marginals match, rollouts fail → check the joint">
The signature of a factorised head. Measure the distance from sampled actions to
the nearest demonstrated action.
</Step>

<Step title="Samples at a fixed observation are all identical → picked a mode">
Passes on success rate whenever either mode works. Fails when the choice is
context-dependent.
</Step>

<Step title="Mixture head with one dominant component → unconstrained variances">
The likelihood is unbounded as $\sigma \to 0$. Floor it before suspecting the
data.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The strongest version of this answer refuses to stop at "MSE averages the modes",
which is the sentence everyone has. What separates it is the three follow-through
claims: that the average has essentially no support rather than being a
compromise, that no choice of loss escapes because every one yields a summary
statistic, and that the obvious discretisation fix passes every per-dimension
check while inventing half its joint mass. The last is the one that most reliably
surprises, because it is a mistake people make while believing they have solved
the problem.
</Warning>
