## What each loss is actually asking for

<Derivation title="The squared-error minimiser is the conditional mean">

For a fixed observation $o$, minimise over the scalar $c = f(o)$:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[(a - c)^2 \mid o\right] = \mathbb{E}[a^2 \mid o] - 2c\,\mathbb{E}[a \mid o] + c^2
$$

Differentiate and set to zero:

$$
-2\,\mathbb{E}[a \mid o] + 2c = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c^{\star} = \mathbb{E}[a \mid o]
$$

**The assumption doing the work is that $f$ returns a point**, so the objective
is minimised pointwise over a single number. Nothing here mentions the shape of
$p(a \mid o)$ — the answer is the mean whether that distribution is a tight
unimodal blob or two well-separated modes, and in the second case the mean is a
value the distribution assigns almost no density.

</Derivation>

Similarly $\arg\min_c \mathbb{E}[|a - c|] $ is the conditional median. Both are
summaries; the choice of loss selects *which* summary.

<KeyIdea>
Generalising: any loss of the form $\mathbb{E}[\ell(a - f(o))]$ has a minimiser
that is some functional of $p(a \mid o)$ — mean, median, a quantile, a mode for
certain non-convex $\ell$. So no amount of loss engineering escapes the problem.
The output has to stop being a point. That is the single sentence this whole
concept reduces to, and it is why the answers all look like generative models.
</KeyIdea>

## What the median buys, and what it costs

For a mixture $p\,\mathcal{N}(-1,\sigma^2) + (1-p)\,\mathcal{N}(+1,\sigma^2)$
with $\sigma = 0.15$, measured over two million samples:

| $p$ | mean | median | sd of sample median |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.40 | $+0.201$ | $+0.855$ | 0.007 |
| 0.45 | $+0.099$ | $+0.799$ | 0.014 |
| 0.48 | $+0.040$ | $+0.735$ | 0.026 |
| **0.50** | $+0.001$ | unstable | **0.653** |
| 0.52 | $-0.039$ | $-0.733$ | 0.030 |
| 0.60 | $-0.200$ | $-0.855$ | 0.010 |

The median does land inside a mode — a real improvement over the mean, which
never does. But it is *discontinuous* at $p = 1/2$: an arbitrarily small change
in the mode balance flips the answer from one mode to the other. Near balance the
target is decided by sampling noise, with the sample median's standard deviation
rising nearly a hundredfold.

<Warning>
A discontinuous target is not obviously better than a stable wrong one. A
network fitting it sees inconsistent supervision across nearby observations with
near-balanced modes, and what it learns in between is unconstrained. And under
either regime the minority mode is never produced, which matters when the modes
are context-dependent rather than interchangeable.
</Warning>

## Why factorising is the wrong shape

Independent per-dimension heads represent

$$
q(a \mid o) = \prod_{d=1}^{D} p(a_d \mid o)
$$

<Derivation title="The error is exactly the dependence you discarded">

Among all distributions with the given marginals, the product is the one of
maximum entropy — the least committal, which here means it spreads mass across
every combination the marginals permit. Its divergence from the truth is

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p(a \mid o)\,\big\|\,\textstyle\prod_d p(a_d \mid o)\right)
= \sum_{d} H(a_d \mid o) - H(a \mid o)
$$

the total correlation among action dimensions. So the approximation error is not
a resolution artefact that finer bins reduce — it is precisely the mutual
information between dimensions, and refining the bins improves each marginal
while leaving the divergence in place.

**Correlated modes are the case that maximises this term.** "Left and up, or
right and down" is a statement that the dimensions are dependent.

</Derivation>

Measured on exactly that two-dimensional expert, with 21 bins per dimension:

| | mass on demonstrated combinations | mass on never-demonstrated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| true joint | **100.0%** | 0.0% |
| product of marginals | 50.0% | **50.0%** |

Both marginals came out correctly bimodal, with modes recovered at $\pm 1.07$ in
each dimension. Every per-dimension check passes and half the samples are actions
the expert never took.

<KeyIdea>
This is why the working answers — a mixture over joint actions, a quantised
codebook of action vectors, an energy function on the whole action, a diffusion
or flow head — all share one property: they model the joint. Autoregressive
factorisation over dimensions also works, since $\prod_d p(a_d \mid a_{<d}, o)$
is an exact factorisation rather than an independence assumption. The distinction
is not discrete versus continuous but joint versus independent.
</KeyIdea>
