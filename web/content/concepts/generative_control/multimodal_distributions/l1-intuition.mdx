## The failure is in the loss, not the data

<Intuition>
When several different actions are all correct — go left or right around an
obstacle, grasp from either side — the data contains genuine disagreement at the
same observation. A regression head cannot express that. Trained with squared
error, its optimal output is the conditional mean of the action given the
observation, so it answers a question nobody asked: not "what would the expert
do?" but "what is the average of everything the expert did?"
</Intuition>

The distinction that matters is that the average is not a *worse* action. It is
an action with no support.

Right: a two-dimensional expert playing only left-up or right-down, where per-dimension discretisation reproduces both marginals correctly yet places half its joint probability mass on the two combinations that never occurred." caption="Two measurements. Measured on a two-mode distribution with modes at $\pm 1$ and width $0.15$:

| within 0.3 of… | fraction of expert actions |
| --- | --- |
| the MSE optimum | **0.00%** |
| a mode | 47.7% |

<KeyIdea>
This is a specification problem rather than an estimation one, and the difference
determines what to do about it. More demonstrations estimate the conditional mean
more precisely; a bigger network represents it more faithfully. Both make you
better at computing the wrong quantity. Nothing improves until the model can
represent a distribution instead of a point.
</KeyIdea>

## Two fixes that do not work

<Steps>
<Step title="Switching to L1 loss">
The optimum becomes the conditional median, which does at least sit inside a mode
when one is heavier. But it *jumps* between modes as the balance crosses 50/50,
and at the symmetric case — the one that motivates the whole problem — it is
unstable: the sample median's standard deviation goes from 0.007 at a 60/40 split
to 0.65 at 50/50.
</Step>

<Step title="Discretising each dimension independently">
Every marginal comes out correctly bimodal, which is what makes this convincing.
But independent heads represent a product of marginals, so in the two-dimensional
test 50% of the joint mass landed on action combinations the expert never played.
</Step>

<Step title="Adding output noise">
Sampling around the mean spreads mass through the valley rather than onto the
modes. It converts a confidently wrong action into an unpredictable one.
</Step>

<Step title="What actually works">
Model the distribution over the whole action vector — a mixture, a quantised
codebook over joint actions, an energy function, or a generative head. What these
share is a joint representation rather than a per-dimension one.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The per-dimension trap is worth dwelling on because it survives the obvious
check. If you plot a histogram of each action dimension and compare it against
the data, everything matches. The disagreement is only visible in the joint —
which for a seven-dimensional action space is not something you can eyeball, so
it has to be tested deliberately.
</Warning>
