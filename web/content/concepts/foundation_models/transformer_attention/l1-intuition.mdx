## Lookup by meaning

Attention is a dictionary lookup where the keys are learned and the match is
soft. Every token produces three vectors: a **query** (what am I looking for), a
**key** (what do I offer), and a **value** (what I will hand over). Compare each
query against every key, turn the similarities into weights, and return the
weighted mix of values.

<Intuition>
You are reading a paper and hit the word "it". To resolve what "it" refers to,
you do not scan back word by word — you glance over the recent text and
something lights up. That glance is query-key matching, and how strongly each
candidate lights up is the attention weight.

The mechanism does not know about grammar, position, or distance. It only knows
that this query and that key look alike.
</Intuition>

## Why this matters for a policy specifically

The language-model framing undersells what attention does for robotics. Three
things.

<Figure src="/figures/embodied-attention-tokens.svg"
        alt="Patches from two cameras, a proprioception vector, and instruction words are each projected into one embedding space and concatenated into a single token sequence, alongside learned action queries. One action query attends across all of them with weights of varying strength."
        caption="Every modality becomes tokens in one sequence, and an action query decides for itself how much of each to use. Nothing in the architecture says the wrist camera matters more during a grasp — that comes out of the content." />

<Steps>
<Step title="Fusion you did not have to design">
A conv-plus-MLP pipeline commits in advance to how the modalities combine —
usually concatenate the pooled features. Attention makes the combination
input-dependent: weight the wrist view when the gripper is near the object, the
overhead view when it is not, the word "cup" when disambiguating a target.
</Step>

<Step title="Memory that does not have to be carried">
To use something from 500 steps ago, a recurrent network must pass it through
500 sequential updates, each of which can dilute or overwrite it. Attention
reaches it in one step. Path length $O(1)$ against $O(n)$ is the whole argument
for transformers over RNNs on long-horizon tasks.
</Step>

<Step title="A variable number of things">
Three objects on the table or seven, one camera or four — the sequence just gets
longer. An MLP over a fixed concatenated vector cannot express that without
padding to a maximum and hoping.
</Step>
</Steps>

## The pattern worth stealing

In language models, the output tokens are the same kind of thing as the input
tokens. In a policy, they are not — you want actions out of an observation.

The standard solution is **learned queries**: allocate one query vector per
action you intend to predict, let each attend over the observation tokens, and
read the action off its output. ACT does exactly this, with roughly a hundred
learned queries for a hundred-step action chunk. The queries carry no
information themselves; they are slots that learn what to look for.

<KeyIdea>
This also gives you action chunking for free, and gives it a nice property: the
queries attend to *each other* as well as to the observation, so the predicted
actions are coherent as a motion rather than being independently plausible
per-step. That is a structural fix for the jitter a per-timestep policy produces.
</KeyIdea>

## What it costs

<Warning>
Every token attends to every token, so compute and memory grow with the *square*
of sequence length. In robotics that budget is spent almost entirely on vision:
a 224x224 image at patch size 14 is 256 tokens, so two camera views is 512
before proprioception, language, history, or action queries.

Doubling the token count roughly quadruples the attention cost. This is why
practical robot transformers compress vision aggressively — fewer views, smaller
images, larger patches, or a convolutional trunk that pools before the sequence
model. When a transformer policy is too slow, count your tokens before reaching
for anything else.
</Warning>

There is a second cost that gets less attention. Convolutions have translation
equivariance built in; transformers do not, and have to learn it from data. That
is fine at scale and expensive when you have 200 demonstrations — one of several
reasons hybrid designs, with a conv trunk feeding a transformer, remain common
in robot learning rather than being a legacy compromise.
