## Attention before transformers

<PaperCard refKey="bahdanau2015attention" />

Attention was invented to fix a specific bottleneck. Encoder-decoder translation
compressed an entire source sentence into one fixed-length vector, and quality
fell off as sentences got longer — the vector simply could not hold it. The fix
was to let the decoder query the encoder's whole sequence at each output step,
so nothing is compressed away.

<KeyIdea>
The original framing explains the shape of the mechanism, and it generalises
straight into robotics. Any time you are about to pool a variable-length or
heterogeneous input into a fixed vector, you are recreating that bottleneck.
Attention is the alternative to pooling.
</KeyIdea>

## Then attention became the model

<PaperCard refKey="vaswani2017transformer" />

Removing recurrence altogether had two consequences that matter here. Path
length between any two positions becomes $O(1)$ instead of $O(n)$, so
long-horizon dependencies are one hop rather than a chain of dilutions. And the
sequence can be processed in parallel during training, which is what made scale
practical.

The costs are equally structural: $O(n^2)$ compute, and no built-in notion of
position or locality — both of which have to be supplied or learned.

## Images are sequences too

<PaperCard refKey="dosovitskiy2021vit" />

ViT cut an image into 16x16 patches and treated them as tokens, discarding
translation equivariance entirely. It loses to convolutions at ImageNet scale
and wins on much larger pretraining sets.

<Warning>
That crossover is the useful part, and it transfers directly to robot learning.
A built-in inductive bias is a constraint: it helps when data is scarce and
limits you when data is plentiful. Robot datasets are firmly in the scarce
regime, which is why a convolutional trunk feeding a transformer remains a
sensible default in policy architectures rather than an outdated compromise. The
right question is never "conv or transformer" but "how much data do I have".
</Warning>

## Control as sequence modelling

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="chen2021dt" />
<PaperCard refKey="reed2022gato" />
</PaperGrid>

Decision Transformer models (return-to-go, state, action) as a token sequence
and predicts actions with a plain next-token loss. No Bellman backups, no
bootstrapping, and therefore none of the deadly-triad instability — at the cost
of losing dynamic programming's ability to *stitch* better trajectories out of
mediocre fragments, which is where Bellman methods still win on low-return
offline data.

Gato pushed the framing to its limit: one set of weights over Atari, captioning,
chat, and real robot control, with each modality serialised into the same token
stream. As a demonstration that modality is a tokenisation detail rather than an
architectural one, it is the cleanest evidence available.

## The embodied-native pattern

<PaperCard refKey="zhao2023act" />

ACT is where the architecture stops imitating language models. Its outputs are
actions, not more tokens of the same kind, so it allocates **learned action
queries** — constant parameter vectors, one per predicted timestep — that attend
over multi-view observation tokens and read out a chunk of roughly a hundred
actions in a single forward pass.

<Steps>
<Step title="One pass, not autoregressive">
The chunk is emitted together, so inference is one decoder forward rather than
$H$ sequential ones. This is the difference between a transformer policy that
runs at control rate and one that does not.
</Step>

<Step title="Queries attend to each other, bidirectionally">
Because there is no generation order to respect, later actions can inform
earlier ones — which is what makes the chunk coherent as a motion rather than a
sequence of individually plausible steps.
</Step>

<Step title="It works from 50 demonstrations">
Worth noting against the scale narrative. The transformer here is not
contributing internet knowledge; it is contributing fusion across camera views
and coherence across the chunk, both of which pay off at small data.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
The recurring pattern across this whole line is the same operation being pointed
at different questions: what in the source relates to this output word, what in
the image relates to this patch, what in the past relates to this action, what
in this observation relates to the action I am about to take. The mechanism has
barely changed since 2017. What changed is what gets tokenised — and in
robotics, that is still the live design question.
</KeyIdea>
