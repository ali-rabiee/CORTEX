## The operation

$$
\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V
$$

with $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d_k}$, $K \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times d_k}$,
$V \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times d_v}$. Read it right to left: $QK^\top$ scores every
query against every key, the softmax turns each row into a distribution over the
$m$ positions, and multiplying by $V$ returns a weighted average of value
vectors.

<KeyIdea>
Every output is a convex combination of value vectors. Attention cannot invent
content — it can only decide, per query, how much of each existing value to mix.
All the expressive power is in *what gets mixed*, which is why what the
projections $W_Q, W_K, W_V$ learn matters more than the averaging itself.
</KeyIdea>

## The scaling factor

<Derivation title="Why $1/\sqrt{d_k}$, and what breaks without it">

Take $q, k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}$ with independent entries of mean 0 and variance
1. Their dot product is

$$
q\cdot k = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i
$$

Each term has mean $\mathbb{E}[q_i k_i] = 0$ and variance
$\mathrm{Var}(q_i k_i) = \mathbb{E}[q_i^2]\mathbb{E}[k_i^2] = 1$ by
independence. Summing $d_k$ independent terms:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(q\cdot k) = d_k
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
|q\cdot k| \sim \sqrt{d_k}
$$

**What that does to the softmax.** At $d_k = 64$ typical logits are around 8,
and differences between them are of the same order. $\text{softmax}$ of logits
separated by 8 is essentially one-hot — and the Jacobian of a saturated softmax
is nearly zero, so no gradient flows to the queries or keys. Training stalls
before it starts, and the effect gets worse as $d_k$ grows.

Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ restores unit-variance logits regardless of dimension,
which keeps the softmax in its responsive region.

**The assumption doing the work** is that queries and keys have roughly
unit-variance entries — which is what the initialisation and the layer
normalisation before the projections are arranging. It is not a law; it is a
condition the rest of the architecture maintains.

</Derivation>

## Multi-head

$$
\text{MHA}(X) = \big[\text{head}_1;\ \ldots;\ \text{head}_h\big]\,W^O,
\qquad
\text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(XW^Q_i,\, XW^K_i,\, XW^V_i)
$$

with $d_k = d/h$ per head, so the total parameter count is essentially unchanged.

<Derivation title="What splitting into subspaces buys">

A single softmax produces exactly **one** distribution over positions per query.
So in a single-head layer, a query that needs to attend to two different things
for two different reasons has no choice but to blend them — and the blended
weights are a compromise that may serve neither purpose.

With $h$ heads there are $h$ independent distributions, each computed in its own
learned subspace. One head can track the object being manipulated, another the
gripper's own position, another the language token that names the target. The
output concatenates them, so downstream layers see all $h$ relations rather than
their average.

This is structurally the same argument as multi-modality in action
distributions: one softmax can only express one mode, and the fix is to have
several. Note the cost — each head has $d/h$ dimensions, so heads that are too
narrow cannot represent a useful similarity at all, which is why $d_k$ is
typically kept at 64 or more rather than $h$ being pushed arbitrarily high.

</Derivation>

## Masking is a statement about dependency

The mask $M \in \{0, -\infty\}^{n\times m}$ is added to the logits before the
softmax, so $-\infty$ removes an edge entirely:

$$
\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\right) V
$$

<Steps>
<Step title="Causal, across time">
Observation tokens from step $t$ must not see step $t+1$. This is a genuine
constraint — future observations do not exist at deployment.
</Step>

<Step title="Bidirectional, within an action chunk">
All $H$ actions are emitted in one forward pass from one observation, so there
is no generation order to respect. Masking them causally discards real structure:
where a trajectory ends up genuinely should influence how it starts. This is the
detail most often copied wrongly from language modelling.
</Step>

<Step title="Cross-attention, for conditioning">
Queries from one stream, keys and values from another — action queries attending
over observation tokens, or observation tokens attending over language. Cost is
$O(nm)$ rather than $O((n+m)^2)$, which matters when one stream is much longer.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Copying a decoder-only language architecture wholesale into a policy imports a
causal mask that was there for a reason that no longer applies. If your action
chunk is generated in one pass, causal masking within it is pure loss.
</Warning>

## The cost, in tokens

$$
\text{time, memory} \;=\; O\big(n^2 d\big) \quad\text{for self-attention over } n \text{ tokens}
$$

Count what $n$ is for a robot:

$$
n = \underbrace{2 \times \left(\tfrac{224}{14}\right)^2}_{512\ \text{visual}}
\;+\; \underbrace{1}_{\text{proprio}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\sim 10}_{\text{language}}
\;+\; \underbrace{H}_{\text{action queries}}
$$

<KeyIdea>
Vision dominates, and the dependence is quadratic — halving the number of visual
tokens cuts attention cost by roughly four. That is why practical robot
transformers use large patches, small images, few views, or a convolutional
trunk that pools before the sequence model. When a transformer policy is too
slow, the token count is the first number to look at and usually the last one
you need.
</KeyIdea>

## What you gave up

Convolutions build in translation equivariance: shift the input, shift the
output. Attention over patches has no such structure — position enters only
through positional embeddings, and equivariance must be learned from data.

At scale that is a benefit, since a learned inductive bias beats an imposed one
when there is enough data to learn it. At 200 demonstrations it is a real cost,
and it is why a convolutional trunk feeding a transformer remains a sensible
default in robot learning rather than a legacy compromise.
