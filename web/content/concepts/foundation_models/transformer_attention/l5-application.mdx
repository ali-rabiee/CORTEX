  prompt: "Give your interview answer on attention for embodied AI. What does it buy a policy, what does it cost, and what is the masking trap?"
  keyPoints:
    - "Lead with what attention is — content-based lookup returning a weighted mix of values — then say what it buys a *policy*: learned multimodal fusion, $O(1)$ path length for memory, and variable-length inputs."
    - "Be able to derive the $\\sqrt{d_k}$ scaling as a variance argument; it is the standard probe and reciting it does not distinguish you."
    - "Name the cost in tokens, not parameters: two 224px views at patch 14 is 512 tokens, and attention is quadratic in that."
    - "The masking trap: causal across time, bidirectional within an action chunk emitted in one pass. Copying a language decoder imports a constraint that no longer applies."
interview:
  answer: >-
    Attention is content-based lookup. Every token produces a query, a key, and
    a value; you score each query against every key, softmax the scores into
    weights, and return the weighted average of the values. So every output is a
    convex combination of existing value vectors — attention can't invent
    content, it decides what to mix, and all the expressiveness is in what the
    query, key, and value projections learn. For a robot policy specifically it
    buys three things a conv-plus-MLP doesn't. First, fusion you didn't have to
    design: two camera views, proprioception, and a language instruction all
    become tokens in one sequence, and how much each matters for this decision
    is decided by content rather than fixed by the architecture — you never had
    to write down that the wrist camera matters more during a grasp. Second,
    memory: path length between any two positions is constant, so something from
    500 steps ago is one hop away, where a recurrent network has to carry it
    through 500 sequential updates that can each dilute it. Third, a variable
    number of things — three objects or seven, the sequence just gets longer.
    On the mechanism, the one derivation worth having is the scaling factor. A
    dot product of two d-dimensional vectors with unit-variance entries has
    variance d, so unscaled logits grow like root-d — at d equals 64 that's
    logits around 8, which saturates the softmax and kills the gradient.
    Dividing by root-d-k restores unit-variance logits at any dimension. And
    multi-head is the same argument as multimodality elsewhere: one softmax
    gives you exactly one distribution over positions, so a single head has to
    average all the relations it needs into one compromise, while several heads
    in separate subspaces can track the object and the gripper and the target
    word simultaneously. The cost is quadratic in tokens, and in robotics the
    token budget is almost entirely vision — a 224 by 224 image at patch 14 is
    256 tokens, so two views is 512 before anything else. That's the number to
    look at when a policy is too slow, not the parameter count. The trap I'd
    flag is masking. Across time, causality is real — you can't see future
    observations. But within an action chunk that's emitted in one forward pass
    from one observation, there's no generation order to respect, so masking the
    action queries causally throws away real structure. Where the motion ends up
    genuinely should shape how it starts. It's the most common bug when someone
    copies a decoder-only language architecture into a policy, and it doesn't
    error — it just quietly makes the chunk less coherent.
  followUps:
    - q: "Would you use a pure transformer or a conv trunk feeding a transformer?"
      a: >-
        With a couple of hundred demonstrations, a conv trunk. ViT's result is
        that patches beat convolutions given enough data and lose given less,
        and robot datasets are firmly in the "less" regime — translation
        equivariance is a genuine prior that a conv net gets for free and a
        transformer has to learn. The trunk also pools before the sequence
        model, which cuts the token count, and since attention is quadratic in
        that, it's a large constant-factor win on top. I'd go patches-first when
        I'm starting from a pretrained ViT or VLM backbone, because then the
        data question is already answered by the pretraining and I want to
        inherit those weights rather than bolt a conv net onto them.
    - q: "Your transformer policy is too slow. Where do you look?"
      a: >-
        Token count first, because it's the only lever with a quadratic effect.
        I'd print the sequence length and see where it goes — it's almost always
        vision. Fewer views, larger patches, smaller images, or a conv trunk
        that pools before the sequence model; halving the visual tokens cuts the
        attention cost by about four. After that I'd make sure I'm using a fused
        attention kernel rather than a hand-written softmax, since the n-by-n
        matrix is the memory bottleneck and not materialising it is often the
        difference between batch size 8 and 64. Then the ordinary things —
        precision, KV caching if there's any autoregressive decode. Making the
        model smaller is what I'd try last, because it usually costs more
        performance per unit of speedup than the token budget does.
    - q: "How do you know your attention policy is looking at the right thing?"
      a: >-
        Reshape the attention weights from an action query back onto the patch
        grid and overlay them on the image. If the query attends to the object
        and the gripper, the model found the task; if it's pinned to a table
        corner or a fixed background feature, it found a shortcut that won't
        survive a change of scene. That's the main interpretability an attention
        policy gives you for free, and it's the same diagnostic as visualising
        spatial-softmax keypoints. The complementary check is ablation — zero
        the language tokens, then the wrist view, then proprioception, and
        measure the drop. A modality whose removal changes nothing is costing
        you tokens quadratically for no benefit, and I'd cut it.
    - q: "Is a transformer actually necessary for a manipulation policy?"
      a: >-
        Not for a single task with one camera, no. A conv encoder with a spatial
        softmax and an MLP head predicting an action chunk is a strong baseline
        and much cheaper. Where the transformer earns its place is when you have
        several heterogeneous inputs that need learned fusion, when you need
        language conditioning, when the task needs memory beyond a frame stack,
        or when you want to inherit a pretrained vision-language backbone —
        which is really the dominant reason now. I'd frame it as: the
        transformer is what lets you plug into pretrained weights and into
        multiple modalities, and if you need neither, the simpler thing is
        probably better at your data scale.

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Too slow → count tokens, not parameters">
The dependence is quadratic and vision dominates. Fewer views, larger patches,
a conv trunk that pools before the sequence model. Then check you are on a fused
attention kernel.
</Step>

<Step title="Runs out of memory at a small batch → the $n\times n$ matrix">
It is the attention matrix held for the backward pass, not the weights. Fused
implementations recompute it in tiles and typically buy an order of magnitude in
batch size.
</Step>

<Step title="Action chunks look jittery at the seams → check the mask">
If the action queries were masked causally, later actions cannot inform earlier
ones and the chunk loses coherence. Within a chunk emitted in one pass, the mask
should be open.
</Step>

<Step title="Attention pinned to background → shortcut learning">
Overlay the weights on the patch grid. Queries fixed on a table corner mean the
model found something correlated with the action in your data rather than the
task. Augmentation and more scene variation, not a bigger model.
</Step>

<Step title="Diverges early in training → post-norm without warmup">
Pre-norm (`norm_first=True`) trains stably from step one. The original post-norm
formulation needs a learning-rate warmup, and copying an old implementation is a
reliable way to lose a day.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Two things separate a good answer here from a recitation. Derive the
$\sqrt{d_k}$ scaling rather than stating it — it is the standard probe, it takes
two lines, and everybody states it. And talk about the cost in *tokens*: a robot
transformer's binding constraint is a 512-token visual budget with quadratic
scaling, not its parameter count. That single reframing is what makes it clear
you have profiled one of these rather than read about them.
</Warning>
