interview:
  answer: >-
    I'd start with why this is a data-economics question rather than a modelling
    one. A foundation model is really an economic arrangement — pay once for
    broad pretraining, adapt cheaply to many downstream tasks — and it works in
    language because the corpus already existed, produced by other people for
    other reasons, at a marginal cost of a crawl. Robotics has to generate every
    sample: a robot, an operator, and wall-clock time. If you put numbers on it,
    at thirty seconds a trajectory including reset and a six-hour operator day,
    one robot produces about 180,000 trajectories a year — so ten million is
    roughly fifty-six robot-years. That's not a matter of trying harder, it's a
    fleet operating for years, and it puts robot data about three orders of
    magnitude below CLIP's four hundred million image-text pairs. Two strategies
    follow. Borrow: start from a web-pretrained vision-language model so your
    demonstrations only have to teach motor skill, since the expensive half —
    knowing what a mug is — was already paid for. That's RT-2. Or pool:
    distribute the collection cost across institutions, which is Open
    X-Embodiment — twenty-two robots, twenty-one institutions, five hundred and
    twenty-seven skills. The thing I'd want to be precise about is what RT-2
    actually demonstrates, because it's an attribution argument rather than a
    success rate. A higher number on trained tasks is consistent with lots of
    explanations. Responding correctly to "pick up the extinct animal" isn't,
    because that concept appears nowhere in the demonstrations and no amount of
    scaling them produces it. That's why the emergent-capability rollouts carry
    the paper and the headline numbers don't. And the mechanism that makes
    borrowing stick is co-fine-tuning — keeping web data in the training mixture
    — which acts as a constraint anchoring the backbone to the pretrained
    solution rather than as ordinary regularisation. Drop it and you fit the
    demonstrations perfectly and lose the generalisation you adopted the model
    for, and that's invisible in held-out demonstration loss. What I wouldn't
    claim is a scaling law. Language has one because tokens are fungible —
    another billion tokens is another billion tokens — so data is a scalar you
    can trade against parameters, which is what makes something like Chinchilla
    possible. Robot data has at least three non-interchangeable axes:
    environments, objects, and demonstrations per pair. The evidence is that
    diversity along the first two beats depth along the third, so "we doubled
    the data" is underspecified until you say which axis.
  followUps:
    - q: "Is robot data going to reach web scale eventually?"
      a: >-
        Not by collection, on the arithmetic. The marginal cost per sample
        differs by orders of magnitude and always will, because one side is a
        crawl and the other is physical time on hardware. What could change the
        picture is a different source. Human video is the obvious candidate —
        Ego4D is three thousand hours of first-person physical interaction — and
        it's cheap and enormous and lacks exactly the thing that makes robot
        data expensive, which is action labels. Methods that extract
        representations or affordances from it work and are in use; methods that
        turn it into demonstrations don't, because you'd have to infer the
        actions from pixels across a morphology change from a human hand to a
        gripper. So I'd say the honest position is that the gap closes by
        borrowing and pooling, not by collecting, and that anyone claiming to
        have solved scarcity with human video should be asked how they recovered
        the actions.
    - q: "What would convince you a robotic foundation model is genuinely scaling?"
      a: >-
        A curve, with the axis named. Concretely: hold environments and objects
        fixed and vary demonstrations per pair, then hold demonstrations fixed
        and vary environments, and show what each buys separately — because the
        current evidence is that those are different exponents. And I'd want it
        measured with intervals, since real-robot evaluation is small-sample and
        the differences being claimed are often inside the noise. The other
        thing I'd want is an attribution control: a from-scratch model at the
        same scale, so I can tell whether an improvement came from the
        pretraining or from the capacity. Without that comparison, "the big
        model is better" is consistent with the pretraining contributing
        nothing.
    - q: "If you were starting a robot learning effort today, borrow or pool?"
      a: >-
        Both, and I'd sequence them. Borrow first, because it's free — start
        from an open VLA rather than training from scratch, adapt with LoRA on
        a few hundred demonstrations, and evaluate specifically on out-of-set
        objects and phrasings to confirm the transferred capability survived the
        fine-tuning. That gets you the semantic generalisation without paying
        for it. Then contribute to and draw from a pooled corpus, because the
        cross-embodiment transfer result means your own setup isn't necessarily
        the right unit of work and other people's data measurably helps yours.
        What I wouldn't do is start by collecting a hundred thousand
        demonstrations of my own tasks — that's the most expensive path to the
        least transferable asset, and the marginal value of depth in a covered
        cell is the lowest-value data you can collect.
    - q: "Does the foundation-model framing actually fit robotics, or is it borrowed vocabulary?"
      a: >-
        Partly borrowed, and it's worth separating which parts fit. The
        pretrain-once-adapt-cheaply structure genuinely fits — LoRA on an open
        VLA with a few hundred demonstrations is exactly that arrangement, and
        it works. What doesn't transfer is the thing that made the language
        version compelling: the predictability. In language you can decide a
        compute budget in advance and know roughly what you'll get, and in
        robotics nobody can tell you what the next order of magnitude of data
        buys. So I'd say it's a foundation model in the architectural and
        economic sense and not yet in the scaling sense, and conflating those is
        how the field ends up promising things it can't deliver. That distinction
        is also the thing I'd most want a candidate to volunteer.

## Reading the situation

<Steps>
<Step title="Someone quotes a robotics scaling result → ask which axis">
Environments, objects, and demonstrations per pair have different exponents.
A curve without a named axis is not interpretable.
</Step>

<Step title="A generalisation claim with no from-scratch control → attribution unestablished">
"The big pretrained model is better" is consistent with the pretraining
contributing nothing. The control is what makes it evidence.
</Step>

<Step title="Fine-tuned model excellent in-distribution, brittle outside → forgetting">
Expected at a few hundred demonstrations without LoRA or a web term. Not visible
in held-out demonstration loss by construction.
</Step>

<Step title="Plan to collect 100k demonstrations of your own tasks → check the marginal value">
Depth in a covered cell is the lowest-value data available and the most
expensive to produce. Borrow, pool, and spend collection on the axes that are
thin.
</Step>

<Step title="Human video proposed as the solution → ask how actions are recovered">
The tier is real and cheap and lacks action labels. Representations transfer;
demonstrations do not, yet.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The move that makes this answer land is arithmetic. Almost everyone can say
robot data is scarce; producing the number — 180k trajectories per robot-year,
so $10^7$ is 56 robot-years — turns a complaint into a constraint, and the two
strategies then follow as consequences rather than as a list. Pair it with the
attribution point about RT-2 and an explicit refusal to extrapolate a scaling
law, and you have said something a senior interviewer in this field will
recognise as their own working position.
</Warning>
