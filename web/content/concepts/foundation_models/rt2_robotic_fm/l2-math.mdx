## The cost model

The economic claim underneath everything here is quantifiable, so quantify it.
Take a demonstration to cost 30 seconds including reset — optimistic — and an
operator day to be 6 productive hours over 250 days a year:

$$
\text{trajectories per robot-year} = \frac{6 \times 250 \times 3600}{30} = 180{,}000
$$

<Derivation title="What each order of magnitude actually costs">

| target | robot-hours | operator-days | robot-years |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $10^5$ | 833 | 139 | 0.6 |
| $10^6$ | 8,333 | 1,389 | 5.6 |
| $10^7$ | 83,333 | 13,889 | 55.6 |

**Read the last row.** Ten million trajectories is roughly 56 robot-years —
a fleet of twenty running for nearly three years, with the maintenance, resets,
hardware failures, and operator turnover that implies.

**Compare against the borrowed tier.** CLIP's pretraining used 400 million
image-text pairs. Against RT-1's 130k demonstrations that is a ratio of about
3,000; against a pooled million-trajectory corpus, about 400. And the numerator
cost a crawl.

**The conclusion is structural rather than motivational.** The gap does not close
by trying harder, because the marginal cost per sample differs by orders of
magnitude and always will. That arithmetic is the entire argument for borrowing
and pooling, and it is worth being able to produce rather than assert.

</Derivation>

## What a robotics scaling law would have to be

Language scaling laws have a convenient property: the x-axis is one number.
Performance is a power law in tokens $D$ and parameters $N$, and a compute budget
can be allocated between them before training starts.

$$
L(N, D) \;\approx\; E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}}
$$

Robot data has no equivalent scalar. A demonstration is characterised by at least
three quantities:

$$
D \;\longrightarrow\; \big(\underbrace{E}_{\text{environments}},\ \underbrace{O}_{\text{objects}},\ \underbrace{M}_{\text{demos per } (E,O) \text{ pair}}\big)
$$

<KeyIdea>
And the evidence is that these are **not interchangeable**: diversity along $E$
and $O$ buys more than depth along $M$, once $M$ is above a floor. So a curve
fitted along one axis says nothing about another, and "we doubled the data" is
an underspecified claim. The right question about any robotics scaling result is
which axis it was measured along.
</KeyIdea>

This also reframes the marginal-value argument from data curation. In a
well-covered $(E, O)$ cell the estimator error falls like $M^{-1/2}$, so the
marginal demonstration is worth $O(M^{-3/2})$; in an uncovered cell, the first
demonstration converts an unbounded extrapolation error into a bounded one. That
asymmetry is why the axes are not exchangeable.

## Co-fine-tuning as a constraint

Training only on robot data moves the shared backbone away from the solution
that contains the transferred semantics. The mixture objective is what prevents
it:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(o,\ell,a)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{robot}}}
\big[-\log p_\theta\big(z(a) \mid o, \ell\big)\big]
\;+\; \lambda\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{web}}}
\big[-\log p_\theta(y \mid x)\big]
$$

<Derivation title="Why this is a constraint and not regularisation">

Write $\theta_0$ for the pretrained weights. Near $\theta_0$, the web term is
approximately quadratic in the displacement:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{web}}(\theta) \approx \mathcal{L}_{\text{web}}(\theta_0)
+ \tfrac{1}{2}(\theta - \theta_0)^\top H_{\text{web}} (\theta - \theta_0)
$$

with $H_{\text{web}}$ the web loss's Hessian. So the second term is a quadratic
penalty on displacement from $\theta_0$, **weighted by the curvature of the web
objective** — it is expensive to move in directions the web task cares about and
free to move in directions it does not.

**That is what distinguishes it from weight decay.** Weight decay penalises
$\|\theta\|^2$, an isotropic preference for small weights with no reference to
any solution. This anchors to a *specific* prior solution along the directions
that solution depends on.

**The consequence for evaluation.** Dropping the term produces a model that fits
the demonstrations perfectly and has lost generality — which does not show up in
held-out demonstration loss, because that is measured on the same narrow
distribution. It shows up only on out-of-set objects and phrasings.

</Derivation>

## Reconciling heterogeneous embodiments

Pooling across robots requires a shared action interface, and the robots do not
have one — different kinematics, different degrees of freedom, different control
modes.

<Steps>
<Step title="Map into a common space">
End-effector deltas in a canonical frame plus a gripper command is the usual
choice: it is defined for most manipulators and hides the joint-space
differences. The cost is that anything requiring joint-level control — redundancy
resolution, whole-arm contact — is not expressible.
</Step>

<Step title="Normalise per embodiment">
Each robot's action statistics differ by workspace size and control rate, so
normalisation constants are per-embodiment. Sharing them across robots silently
rescales one body's actions into another's units.
</Step>

<Step title="Let an embodiment token absorb the remainder">
What the common space cannot express becomes a conditioning variable, exactly as
in a multi-task policy. The model can then represent per-body behaviour while
sharing everything the bodies have in common.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Positive transfer across embodiments was not obvious in advance, and the reason
it is interesting is the same reason it might have failed: this is the
negative-transfer regime from multi-task learning, with the tasks being *bodies*.
That it helps says the shared structure — visual scenes, object semantics,
manipulation strategy — outweighs the kinematic differences **at this scale**.
It is a scale-dependent empirical claim, not a principle, and it is exactly the
kind of claim that should be re-checked when the scale changes.
</Warning>
