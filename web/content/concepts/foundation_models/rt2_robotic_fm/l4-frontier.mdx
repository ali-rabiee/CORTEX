## The template robotics inherited

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="kaplan2020scaling" />
<PaperCard refKey="hoffmann2022chinchilla" />
</PaperGrid>

These matter here as the *contrast* case. Language modelling has smooth power
laws relating loss to parameters, data, and compute — accurate enough that
Chinchilla could reallocate a fixed compute budget between parameters and tokens
and show that prior models had been badly under-trained on data.

<KeyIdea>
That precision rests on one property: tokens are fungible. Another billion tokens
is another billion tokens, so data is a scalar and the exponent means something.
Robotics inherited the vocabulary — "foundation model", "scaling" — without that
property, and reading Chinchilla as an aspiration is a category error. The useful
reading is that it shows exactly what robotics lacks.
</KeyIdea>

## Answer one: pay for the data

<PaperCard refKey="brohan2022rt1" />

130,000 demonstrations over 700+ instructions, collected directly. The finding —
joint training across tasks beats per-task policies once the data is large enough
— is real and it is bounded by what the dataset contains. Generalisation stays
inside the distribution that was paid for.

At the cost model from Level 2, that is roughly nine months of a single robot
operating continuously, which understates it: multiple robots, operators,
supervision, and resets over a much longer calendar period. This is what direct
investment in collection buys, and it establishes the price.

## Answer two: borrow it

<PaperCard refKey="brohan2023rt2" />

The strategic alternative. Start from a model that already knows what objects
are, and let the demonstrations teach only motor skill.

<Warning>
The evidential burden is different from RT-1's, and this is the part worth being
precise about. A higher success rate on trained tasks would be consistent with
many explanations. The claim being made is *attribution* — that a capability came
from the web pretraining — so the evidence has to be behaviour the robot dataset
cannot account for. That is why the emergent-capability rollouts carry the
argument, and why a from-scratch control is the comparison that makes it stick.
</Warning>

The mechanism that makes borrowed knowledge persist is co-fine-tuning, and it is
the least glamorous part of the paper. Robot data is narrow enough that training
on it alone drags the backbone off the pretrained solution — and the loss of
capability is invisible in held-out demonstration loss.

## Answer three: distribute the cost

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="oneill2023openx" />
<PaperCard refKey="khazatsky2024droid" />
</PaperGrid>

If no single institution can afford the corpus, share the bill. Open X-Embodiment
assembled 22 robots across 21 institutions covering 527 skills; DROID collected
76,000 trajectories across 564 scenes and 84 tasks, from 50 collectors on three
continents over twelve months.

<Steps>
<Step title="Why pooling had to be shown to work, not assumed">
Different embodiments have different kinematics and action spaces, so this is the
negative-transfer regime from multi-task learning with *bodies* as the tasks.
That it helps says shared structure outweighs the kinematic differences at this
scale — an empirical, scale-dependent claim.
</Step>

<Step title="Why DROID's protocol is the contribution">
Volume is the cheap axis and diversity is the expensive one, because diversity
requires physically going somewhere else. A standardised rig is what makes many
labs' data combine, and collecting in real kitchens and offices is what makes the
scene variation real. Read against the scaling finding — environments and objects
matter more than depth — the protocol *is* the result.
</Step>
</Steps>

## The tier nobody has cracked

<PaperCard refKey="grauman2022ego4d" />

3,000 hours of first-person human activity: an enormous, cheap corpus full of
physical interaction. It lacks exactly the thing that makes robot data expensive
— action labels — and it has a morphology gap, since a human hand is not a
gripper.

<KeyIdea>
This is the largest untapped tier of the pyramid and the reason it stays untapped
is precise. Methods that extract *representations* or *affordances* from human
video work and are in use. Methods that turn it into *demonstrations* do not, and
the obstacle is not data volume but the inference from pixels to actions across a
morphology change. Anyone claiming to have solved robot data scarcity via human
video should be asked how they recovered the actions.
</KeyIdea>

## Asking the scaling question properly

<PaperCard refKey="lin2024scalinglaws" />

The first careful attempt to give robotics something like a scaling law, and its
main contribution is refusing to treat "more data" as one dial. Separating
environments, objects, and demonstrations per pair, it finds diversity along the
first two outweighs volume along the third.

<Warning>
What this establishes is that the axes are not interchangeable, which is enough
to change collection decisions. What it does not establish is an exponent you can
plan a budget against, or that the relationship holds outside the tasks studied.

So the honest summary of the field: borrowing works for semantics, pooling works
even across bodies, and nobody can currently tell you what another order of
magnitude of robot data buys. That last uncertainty is the centre of the field,
and a candidate who states it plainly is more credible than one who extrapolates
from a language scaling curve.
</Warning>
