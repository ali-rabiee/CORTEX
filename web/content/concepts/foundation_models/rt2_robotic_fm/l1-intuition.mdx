## What "foundation model" actually claims

The phrase describes an economic arrangement more than an architecture: pay once
for broad pretraining on a large corpus, then adapt cheaply to many downstream
tasks. It works in language because the corpus already existed — produced by
other people, for other reasons, at a marginal cost of a crawl.

Robotics does not have that. Every trajectory has to be *generated*: a robot, an
operator, and wall-clock time, per sample.

<Figure src="/figures/robot-data-pyramid.svg"
        alt="Three stacked tiers. Web image-text pairs at 400 million, egocentric human video at 3,000 hours, robot demonstrations at 76 to 130 thousand. A bracket marks roughly four orders of magnitude between top and bottom."
        caption="The gap is structural, not a matter of current effort. So the productive question is not how to collect more robot data, but what can be borrowed from data someone else already paid for." />

<Intuition>
Language modelling inherited a library. Robotics has to write every book it
wants to read, by hand, in real time, and a page takes as long to produce as it
takes to happen.

You do not close that gap by hiring more scribes. You close it by finding out how
much of what you need is already in the library.
</Intuition>

## Two strategies, and they are complementary

<Steps>
<Step title="Borrow — start from a web-pretrained model">
Take a vision-language model that already knows what objects are, and let your
demonstrations teach only the motor skill. The bet is that concepts and motor
skill are separable, and that the expensive half was already paid for by someone
else. This is the RT-2 line.
</Step>

<Step title="Pool — combine robot data across labs and bodies">
No single institution can afford a corpus at the scale the recipe needs, so
distribute the cost. Open X-Embodiment assembled 22 robots across 21
institutions, 527 skills. This is the RT-X line.
</Step>

<Step title="And the third, less discussed — mine human video">
Thousands of hours of egocentric footage exist, containing enormous amounts of
physical interaction. What they lack is action labels, which is exactly the
thing that would make them usable, and extracting a usable signal from them is
still an open problem rather than a settled technique.
</Step>
</Steps>

## What RT-2 actually demonstrates

The result people quote is that RT-2 handles instructions and objects the robot
data never contained. The reason that is *evidence* rather than a good number is
an attribution argument.

<KeyIdea>
A higher success rate on trained tasks is consistent with many explanations —
more parameters, better optimisation, more data. A correct response to "pick up
the extinct animal" has one available explanation, because no amount of scaling
demonstrations of the same seven hundred instructions produces a concept that
was never in them. That is what makes the emergent-capability rollouts, rather
than the headline numbers, the part of the paper that changed the field's
direction.
</KeyIdea>

The mechanism that makes borrowing stick is less glamorous and equally
important: **co-fine-tuning**. Training on robot data alone drags the shared
backbone away from its pretrained solution, and the semantic knowledge lives in
that backbone. Keeping web vision-language data in the training mixture is what
preserves it — and it is easy to skip when reimplementing, because held-out
demonstration loss looks fine either way.

## How good is the analogy to language?

<Warning>
Looser than the shared vocabulary suggests, and the difference is worth being
precise about. Language modelling has scaling laws accurate enough to allocate a
compute budget between parameters and tokens before training starts. Robotics
does not have anything comparable — the recent work is a first careful attempt
at even asking the question properly, and it finds that the axes are not
interchangeable: environments and objects matter more than demonstrations per
environment-object pair.

So "more data" is not one dial in robotics, and a scaling curve fitted on one
axis does not tell you what to do on another. Anyone quoting a robotics scaling
law should be asked which axis it is along.
</Warning>

## What is actually established

<Steps>
<Step title="Borrowing works, for semantics">
Web pretraining contributes generalisation to objects, categories, and phrasings
that robot data cannot explain. This is solid.
</Step>

<Step title="Pooling works, even across bodies">
Heterogeneous data from different robots gives positive transfer rather than
interference. Not obvious in advance, and it is what makes contributing to a
shared corpus rational rather than charitable.
</Step>

<Step title="Whether it keeps scaling is open">
There is no established curve saying what another order of magnitude of robot
data buys, and the collection cost is what makes finding out expensive. That
uncertainty is the honest centre of the field right now, and a candidate who
states it is more credible than one who extrapolates.
</Step>
</Steps>
