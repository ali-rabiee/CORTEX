## Two families, two interfaces

"VLM" covers two quite different things, and knowing which you are holding
determines what you can ask it.

<Steps>
<Step title="Contrastive: CLIP and its descendants">
Two encoders — one for images, one for text — trained so that matching pairs
land close together in a shared embedding space and mismatched pairs land far
apart. The output is a **similarity score**. You supply the candidate texts,
which turns it into a zero-shot classifier: embed "a photo of a mug", "a photo of
a stapler", and see which is closer.
</Step>

<Step title="Generative: Flamingo, BLIP-2, LLaVA">
A vision encoder whose output is projected into a language model's token space
through an adapter, so the LLM can attend over image tokens alongside text ones.
The output is **text**. Open-ended questions, descriptions, and reasoning — much
more flexible, much slower, and capable of being wrong in confident prose.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Intuition>
The contrastive model is a very good librarian who can tell you how well a
caption matches a photograph but cannot write one. The generative model is
someone who will happily describe the photograph at length — including the parts
they did not look at carefully.
</Intuition>

## What they are actually good at

<Figure src="/figures/vlm-semantic-vs-spatial.svg"
        alt="One scene with two boxes of questions. The green box lists naming and categorisation questions marked reliable. The red box lists distance, relative position, and reachability questions marked unreliable."
        caption="The split follows the training objective. Web captions say what is in an image far more often than they say how far apart things are — so semantics is supervised and metric geometry is not." />

The reliable capability is **semantic**: naming objects, recognising categories
they were never explicitly trained on, judging affordances ("which of these
would break if dropped"), and matching an open-vocabulary description to an
image. This is what internet-scale image-text pretraining actually contains, and
it is genuinely remarkable.

<Warning>
The unreliable capability is **metric and relational spatial reasoning**. How
many centimetres apart two objects are, whether A is nearer the edge than B,
whether a gripper could reach behind something. Captions describe what is in an
image; they rarely encode precise geometry, so nothing in the objective forced
the model to represent it.

The dangerous part is that the model does not decline. It answers fluently and
confidently, which in a robot stack means a plausible-sounding wrong answer
flows straight into a motion command.
</Warning>

There is a sharper version of this failure worth knowing. Contrastive models
often behave like **bags of words**: they score "the mug to the left of the box"
and "the box to the left of the mug" almost identically, because word-matching
already satisfies the contrastive objective and word *order* was never forced to
matter. For language-conditioned manipulation, where relational instructions are
the whole point, that is a direct hazard.

## The interface that actually gets used in robotics

A caption is the wrong output for a manipulation stack. If you need to grasp the
object a user named, you need pixels — a box or a mask — not a sentence you then
have to parse back into coordinates.

<KeyIdea>
The workhorse perception interface for language-conditioned robotics is
**open-vocabulary detection and grounded segmentation**: text query in, boxes or
masks out. It gives the semantic generalisation you adopted a VLM for, in a form
a motion planner or a grasp predictor can consume. A generative VLM sits above
that, at the planning layer, where its fluency is an asset and its metric
weakness matters less.
</KeyIdea>

<Steps>
<Step title="Use a VLM for">
Deciding *what* to interact with. Open-vocabulary identification, affordance
judgements, high-level task decomposition, and recognising objects your training
set never contained.
</Step>

<Step title="Use classical perception for">
Deciding *where* it is, precisely. Depth, pose, and metric geometry come from
sensors and geometry, not from a language-supervised model — and mixing the two
correctly is most of the engineering in a modern manipulation stack.
</Step>
</Steps>
