interview:
  answer: >-
    VLM covers two fairly different things and it's worth separating them
    because they give you different interfaces. Contrastive models, CLIP and its
    descendants, have separate image and text encoders trained so matching pairs
    land close in a shared embedding space. The output is a similarity score, so
    you supply the candidate texts and it becomes a zero-shot classifier or a
    retrieval engine. Generative models — Flamingo, BLIP-2, LLaVA — feed a
    vision encoder's output into a language model through an adapter, so the
    output is text: open-ended questions, descriptions, reasoning. For robotics
    the thing I'd emphasise is what to trust them with. They're genuinely
    reliable at semantics — naming objects, recognising categories they were
    never explicitly trained on, judging affordances like which of these would
    break if dropped. They're unreliable at metric and relational spatial
    reasoning: distances, whether A is nearer the edge than B, whether the
    gripper can reach behind something. And the reason is the training
    objective, not capacity. Web captions say what's in an image far more often
    than they encode precise geometry, so nothing ever forced the model to
    represent it. There's a sharper version of that worth knowing: contrastive
    models often behave like bags of words. They score "the mug left of the box"
    and "the box left of the mug" almost identically, because random in-batch
    negatives essentially never differ from the true caption only by word order,
    so ordering was never punished. For language-conditioned manipulation, where
    relational instructions are the normal case, that's a direct hazard. What
    makes both of these dangerous rather than merely limiting is that the model
    doesn't decline — it answers fluently and confidently, and in a robot stack a
    plausible wrong number flows straight into a motion command. So the
    interface I'd actually build against is open-vocabulary detection or
    grounded segmentation: text query in, boxes or masks out. That's what a
    grasp planner can consume, and it gives me the open-vocabulary
    generalisation I adopted a VLM for without asking it to do geometry. Depth
    and camera calibration supply the metric part, where they're reliable. The
    division of labour is the whole design: the VLM decides what to interact
    with, the sensors decide where it is.
  followUps:
    - q: "How would you test whether a VLM is safe for your relational instructions?"
      a: >-
        Directly, on my own inputs. I'd take a set of scenes and query each
        relation in both orders — "the mug left of the box" and "the box left of
        the mug" — and compare the outputs. If they're nearly identical, the
        model is bag-of-words on this input distribution and any relational
        instruction is unsafe. The second test is a decoy: run the query on a
        scene where the target isn't present, because the softmax is over my
        candidate list and there's no "none of these" unless I add one. A
        confident answer on an absent object is the difference between doing
        nothing and grasping the wrong thing. If either test fails, I'd either
        use a model trained with order-permuted hard negatives, or take relations
        out of the VLM entirely and compute them from the detected boxes, where
        I can verify the geometry.
    - q: "When would you use a generative VLM rather than a detector?"
      a: >-
        At the planning layer, where fluency is the asset and metric precision
        isn't the question. Decomposing "clear the table" into a sequence of
        object-level subgoals, deciding which of several objects satisfies a
        vague description, or answering whether a precondition looks satisfied —
        those play to what the model is good at. What I wouldn't do is ask it
        for coordinates and parse them out of prose, because that reintroduces
        the grounding problem with a failure mode that reads as confident. So
        the layering I'd argue for is a generative VLM proposing what to do next,
        an open-vocabulary detector turning each object reference into pixels,
        and geometry handling everything metric.
    - q: "Why does batch size matter so much for CLIP training specifically?"
      a: >-
        Because the batch is the negative set. The softmax denominator ranges
        over the texts in the batch, so batch size sets the difficulty of the
        discrimination task rather than just the gradient noise. At batch 8 the
        model beats seven random distractors, which coarse subject-level
        features achieve; at batch 32k it has to separate this caption from
        thousands of plausible alternatives. That's why the training runs use
        very large batches gathered across devices — and it's also a real bug
        source, because if you skip the cross-device all-gather each device
        solves its own small problem and the loss curve looks completely
        healthy. SigLIP's per-pair sigmoid is the fix that decouples the
        objective from batch size entirely.
    - q: "Is the spatial reasoning gap fundamental?"
      a: >-
        The evidence says no, it's a supervision gap. SpatialVLM synthesised
        metric question-answer data by lifting internet images into 3-D,
        computing true distances and relations, and turning them into training
        pairs — and the capability appeared. If the architecture were incapable,
        manufacturing the data wouldn't have helped. That's a more useful
        diagnosis than "VLMs are bad at spatial reasoning", and it generalises:
        the right question about any self-supervised model is what its objective
        *forced* it to learn, not what it could in principle represent. Captions
        never state distances, so distances aren't there. That said, for a real
        system today I'd still get metric answers from depth rather than from a
        language-supervised model, because I can characterise the error of a
        depth sensor and I can't characterise the error of a fluent guess.

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Wrong side, wrong relation → bag-of-words">
Query both orderings of the relation. Near-identical scores mean word order was
never encoded. Fix with hard negatives, or compute relations from detected boxes
where geometry is verifiable.
</Step>

<Step title="Confidently identifies an absent object → no null option">
The softmax is over your candidate list. Add a background prompt or calibrate an
absolute similarity threshold before this reaches an actuator.
</Step>

<Step title="Fluent but wrong distances → asking the wrong component">
Metric geometry was never supervised. Get it from depth and calibration; use the
VLM to decide *which* object, not *where* it is.
</Step>

<Step title="Coarse localisation from a strong model → the adapter bottleneck">
A query-bottleneck architecture with 32 learned queries over hundreds of patches
discards fine spatial detail while remaining fluent. Check localisation
explicitly rather than inferring it from answer quality.
</Step>

<Step title="Weak features despite a huge nominal batch → missing all-gather">
Contrastive loss is not separable across devices. Without gathering embeddings,
each device solves a much easier problem and the loss curve looks fine.
</Step>
</Steps>

<InterviewAnswer />

<Warning>
The thing that separates a strong answer here is explaining the limits by the
*objective* rather than listing them. "VLMs are bad at spatial reasoning" is a
fact anyone can repeat. "Random in-batch negatives almost never differ only by
word order, so bag-of-words matching already wins the contrastive game, and
captions rarely encode distances so metric geometry was never supervised" is a
diagnosis — and it predicts both the fixes that worked, hard negatives and
synthesised spatial data. It also tells the interviewer you read objectives for
what they force rather than for what they allow.
</Warning>
