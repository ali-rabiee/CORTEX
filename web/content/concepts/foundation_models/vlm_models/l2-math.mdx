## The contrastive objective

Given a batch of $N$ image-text pairs, encode both sides and $\ell_2$-normalise:

$$
u_i = \frac{f_{\text{img}}(x_i)}{\|f_{\text{img}}(x_i)\|},
\qquad
v_j = \frac{f_{\text{txt}}(t_j)}{\|f_{\text{txt}}(t_j)\|}
$$

Form the $N\times N$ similarity matrix $S_{ij} = u_i^\top v_j / \tau$ with a
learned temperature $\tau$, and treat each row and each column as an
$N$-way classification whose correct answer is the diagonal:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big(
\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_i -\log\frac{\exp S_{ii}}{\sum_j \exp S_{ij}}}_{\text{image}\to\text{text}}
+
\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_j -\log\frac{\exp S_{jj}}{\sum_i \exp S_{ij}}}_{\text{text}\to\text{image}}
\Big)
$$

<Derivation title="Why the batch size is a capability parameter">

The denominator ranges over the batch, so the batch *supplies the negatives*.
With batch size $N$, the model must make the true caption outscore $N-1$
alternatives.

**At $N = 8$**, seven random distractors are almost always about a completely
different subject. Separating "a dog on a beach" from seven unrelated captions
needs only coarse subject-level features.

**At $N = 32{,}768$**, thousands of captions describe similar scenes, and
winning requires distinguishing this dog on this beach from many others.

So $N$ sets the *difficulty of the task*, not merely the gradient noise. This is
qualitatively unlike supervised training, and it is why CLIP-family runs use
very large batches gathered across devices.

**The assumption doing the work is that in-batch negatives are informative.**
They are drawn at random, so they are almost never a near-miss — which sets up
exactly the failure in the next derivation.

</Derivation>

## What the objective does *not* require

<Derivation title="Where compositionality is lost">

The loss is satisfied when

$$
u_i^\top v_i \;>\; u_i^\top v_j \quad \text{for all } j \ne i \text{ in the batch}
$$

Consider the true caption "the mug to the left of the box" and a random
in-batch negative, "a bicycle leaning on a fence". A representation that encodes
only the *content words* — mug, box — already separates them by a wide margin.

Now consider the hypothetical negative "the box to the left of the mug". It has
the identical content words and differs only in order. Under random batching,
such a negative essentially never appears — so the objective never applies
pressure to distinguish them, and a bag-of-words representation remains optimal.

**Consequence.** The model can score "A left of B" and "B left of A" almost
identically, and this is not a capacity failure — it is the objective getting
exactly what it asked for. For a language-conditioned policy, where relational
instructions are the point, it is a direct hazard.

**The fix follows from the derivation.** Construct hard negatives by permuting
the caption's word order and include them explicitly, so ordering becomes the
*only* discriminating signal for that pair.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
Read every self-supervised objective this way: not "what could the model learn?"
but "what is the cheapest representation that wins this game?" Contrastive
learning with random negatives makes bag-of-words optimal, so that is what you
get. The same reasoning explains why metric geometry is absent — no caption
pairing ever required it.
</KeyIdea>

## The sigmoid alternative

SigLIP replaces the row-and-column softmax with an independent binary
classification per pair:

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}
\log \sigma\!\Big(z_{ij}\big(t\, u_i^\top v_j + b\big)\Big),
\qquad z_{ij} = \begin{cases} +1 & i = j\\ -1 & i \ne j\end{cases}
$$

with learnable scale $t$ and bias $b$.

<Steps>
<Step title="What it changes">
No normalisation across the batch, so the loss decomposes pair by pair. Training
no longer needs a global all-gather of every embedding, and the objective is no
longer coupled to $N$.
</Step>

<Step title="Why the bias term is needed">
With $N^2$ pairs of which only $N$ are positive, the labels are heavily
imbalanced — at $N = 16{,}384$ that is roughly 16,000:1. Initialising $b$
strongly negative starts the model near the correct prior instead of letting the
negatives dominate the early gradient.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Attaching vision to a language model

A generative VLM must express image content in a form a language model can read.
The LLM is a fixed function over sequences of $d$-dimensional token embeddings,
so the whole problem is a change of coordinates into that space.

$$
\text{tokens} = \big[\,g_\phi(f_{\text{img}}(x))\,;\; \text{embed}(t)\,\big]
\qquad
g_\phi: \mathbb{R}^{n_p \times d_v} \to \mathbb{R}^{n_p \times d}
$$

<Steps>
<Step title="Linear or MLP projection (LLaVA)">
$g_\phi$ is a two-layer MLP applied per patch. The striking empirical result is
that this is enough: the language model's existing machinery does the reasoning
once the vision features are expressed in its coordinates. Cost is $n_p$ extra
tokens, and attention is quadratic in the total.
</Step>

<Step title="Learned query tokens (BLIP-2's Q-Former)">
A small transformer with $k \ll n_p$ learned queries cross-attends over the
patches and emits $k$ tokens. Fixed cost regardless of image resolution, at the
price of a bottleneck — anything the $k$ queries did not retrieve is gone.
</Step>
<Step title="Gated cross-attention (Flamingo)">
Insert cross-attention layers into a frozen LLM, gated by a `tanh` initialised
at zero so the model starts exactly as the original language model and learns
its way into using vision. The text stream never lengthens, so long contexts stay
affordable.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The three differ in where the information bottleneck sits, and that determines
what survives. Per-patch projection keeps the most spatial detail and costs the
most tokens; a query bottleneck is cheap and discards whatever the queries did
not ask for. If your downstream task needs fine spatial discrimination, a
$k = 32$ query bottleneck over a 576-patch image is throwing away precisely what
you need — and it will still produce fluent answers.
</Warning>

## Turning a scorer into a detector

Open-vocabulary detection is what makes any of this usable for manipulation, and
the construction is simple: keep per-patch features rather than pooling, and
score each candidate region against the text embedding.

$$
\text{score}(\text{box}_r, \text{text}) = \frac{\phi(z_r)^\top v_{\text{text}}}
{\|\phi(z_r)\|\,\|v_{\text{text}}\|}
$$

where $z_r$ is the ViT output token for region $r$ and $\phi$ a learned
projection into the text space. Each token also predicts a box offset, so the
model outputs coordinates with an open-vocabulary label.

<KeyIdea>
The move is to keep the alignment CLIP learned but *not* pool it away. Pooling
to one embedding per image discards localisation; keeping per-token embeddings
retains it, and the same contrastive alignment then works per region. That one
change is what converts a similarity score into an interface a grasp planner can
consume.
</KeyIdea>
