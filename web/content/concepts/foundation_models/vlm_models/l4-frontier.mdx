## The alignment that started it

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="radford2021clip" />
<PaperCard refKey="zhai2023siglip" />
</PaperGrid>

CLIP's result was that contrastive alignment at 400M-pair scale produces
zero-shot classification competitive with supervised baselines — and, more
durably, an embedding space in which text and image live together. Every
open-vocabulary method since is built on some version of that space.

SigLIP's change looks small and is practically significant. Replacing the
batch-wide softmax with an independent sigmoid per pair decouples the objective
from batch size, removes the cross-device all-gather, and behaves better at
small batches. It is a good example of a contribution that changes nothing
conceptual and a great deal operationally.

## Three ways to attach vision to a language model

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="alayrac2022flamingo" />
<PaperCard refKey="li2023blip2" />
<PaperCard refKey="liu2023llava" />
</PaperGrid>

The three represent the design space cleanly. Flamingo inserts gated
cross-attention into a frozen LLM, initialised so the model starts exactly as
the unmodified language model and earns its way into using vision. BLIP-2 uses a
small transformer with learned queries to compress the image into a fixed number
of tokens. LLaVA projects patch features directly with a two-layer MLP.

<KeyIdea>
LLaVA's competitiveness is the informative result, and it becomes unsurprising
once you see that no new capability is being created. The vision encoder already
separates visual concepts; the language model already reasons over token
embeddings. The adapter is a change of coordinates. What was actually hard was
the *data* — generating multimodal instruction-following conversations, which
LLaVA did by prompting a language model with image annotations.
</KeyIdea>

The three differ in where the bottleneck sits, and that determines what
survives. Per-patch projection keeps the most spatial detail and costs the most
tokens; a query bottleneck is cheap and discards whatever the queries did not
retrieve. For a task needing fine spatial discrimination, that is not a free
choice.

## The necessary corrective

<PaperCard refKey="yuksekgonul2023bagofwords" />

Contrastive VLMs frequently cannot distinguish captions that differ only in word
order. The paper's contribution is not just the observation but the explanation:
random in-batch negatives essentially never differ from the true caption only by
ordering, so nothing in the objective ever punished ignoring it — and
bag-of-words matching is the cheapest solution that wins the game.

<Warning>
For language-conditioned robotics this is a direct hazard, because relational
instructions are the normal case rather than an edge case. "Put the mug to the
left of the box" and its reverse may score almost identically. The remedy from
the paper is order-permuted hard negatives during training; the remedy at
deployment is to test both orderings on your own inputs, and if the model cannot
separate them, handle relations with geometry you can verify instead.
</Warning>

## What robotics actually consumes

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="minderer2022owlvit" />
<PaperCard refKey="kirillov2023sam" />
</PaperGrid>

OWL-ViT makes the minimal change that converts a scorer into a detector: stop
pooling. Keep per-token ViT embeddings, project each into the text space, and
attach a box head — the contrastive alignment then works per region rather than
per image. SAM supplies promptable masks at a quality that makes a box-to-mask
step essentially free.

Together they give the pipeline that language-conditioned manipulation stacks
actually run: text query to boxes, boxes to masks, masks plus depth to a grasp.
The VLM contributes open-vocabulary semantics; the geometry stays with the
sensors.

## Closing the spatial gap

<PaperCard refKey="chen2024spatialvlm" />

SpatialVLM treats weak metric reasoning as a data problem rather than an
architectural one: lift internet images into 3-D, compute true distances and
relations, and synthesise question-answer pairs from them. That the resulting
capability appears is itself the argument — if the architecture were incapable,
manufacturing the supervision would not have helped.

<KeyIdea>
The general lesson runs through this whole page. Ask what the training objective
*forced* the model to learn, not what it could in principle represent.
Contrastive learning with random negatives makes bag-of-words optimal, so
composition is absent. Captioning never states distances, so metric geometry is
absent. Both gaps closed when someone supplied the missing signal — which is a
much more useful diagnosis than "VLMs are bad at spatial reasoning".
</KeyIdea>
