## Borrowed machinery

<PaperCard refKey="oord2017vqvae" />

Every learned action tokeniser is a VQ-VAE with actions in place of images. The
imports are the straight-through estimator that lets gradients cross a
non-differentiable argmin, the commitment loss that keeps encoder outputs near
the codebook, and — inseparably — the codebook-collapse failure mode that comes
with them.

<PaperCard refKey="zeghidour2021soundstream" />

The closer analogy, because audio is a continuous time-series with the same
structure as a trajectory. Residual vector quantisation is the idea worth
carrying over: stack codebooks so each encodes what the previous stage missed,
giving a rate-distortion trade you can move along by adding or dropping stages
rather than by retraining.

<KeyIdea>
Action chunks are closer to audio than to images — smooth, band-limited, strongly
autocorrelated in time. That is why the codec literature transfers so directly,
and why "what would an audio codec do here?" is a productive question when
designing an action representation.
</KeyIdea>

## The simplest code, and why it was enough

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="brohan2022rt1" />
<PaperCard refKey="brohan2023rt2" />
</PaperGrid>

RT-1 bins each action dimension into 256 values and predicts them as tokens. No
transform, no codebook, no training — and it works for real manipulation at
scale, which is the finding that made everything downstream possible.

RT-2 adds the implementation detail that carries a surprising amount of weight:
put the action bins in the **least-used slots of the existing language
vocabulary** rather than extending it.

<Warning>
Extending the vocabulary means new rows in the embedding matrix and new columns
in the output projection, randomly initialised, sitting in exactly the layers
that pretraining spent enormous compute shaping. Overwriting rarely-used existing
slots avoids that entirely, which is why actions become indistinguishable from
language to the rest of the model and no surgery is needed. A small choice
carrying a real fraction of why borrowing a VLM is cheap.
</Warning>

## Refusing to choose

<PaperCard refKey="shafiullah2022bet" />

The cleanest decomposition of the argument. Discretisation's real benefit is that
a categorical distribution represents multi-modality — mass on separated actions
with nothing between them — which an MSE regression structurally cannot. Its real
cost is resolution.

So predict both: a categorical over cluster centres, and a continuous residual
offset *conditioned on the chosen cluster*. The categorical carries the modes,
the residual restores the precision, and because the residual is conditional the
averaging problem does not reappear inside it.

## Attacking the decode length, from both ends

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="pertsch2025fast" />
<PaperCard refKey="black2024pi0" />
</PaperGrid>

Once the backbone is fixed, inference latency is dominated by the number of
sequential decode steps, and $H \times d$ tokens per chunk is a lot. Two opposite
responses.

FAST imports transform coding: treat the chunk as a signal, move to a basis where
energy concentrates, and quantise the significant coefficients. The chunk costs a
fraction of the tokens at essentially unchanged fidelity, and everything about
the discrete route is preserved — vocabulary reuse, free multi-modality from the
softmax.

$\pi_0$ removes the code. A flow-matching action expert emits continuous chunks,
so there is no quantisation floor and no sequential decode at all — at the cost
of a separate generative head with its own training dynamics, its own
multi-modality machinery, and no reuse of the pretrained output head.

<KeyIdea>
Neither dominates, and the choice is determined by two questions with numbers
attached. Does your task's precision requirement sit near the quantisation floor —
about 0.2 mm at 256 bins over a 10 cm range? And is your latency budget tight
enough that $Hd$ sequential decode steps do not fit? If both answers are no, the
simplest code is the right one, and that is a defensible position rather than a
lazy one.
</KeyIdea>

## What is settled and what is not

<Steps>
<Step title="Settled">
That naive per-dimension binning is adequate for a wide range of manipulation,
that its resolution floor is computable and usually irrelevant, and that
discretisation buys multi-modality as a genuine benefit rather than a workaround.
</Step>

<Step title="Established recently">
That action chunks have exploitable temporal structure, and that codes ignoring
it spend most of their token budget on redundancy. This reframed tokenisation
from a formatting decision into a compression problem.
</Step>

<Step title="Open">
Whether a *learned* code beats a hand-designed transform once both are tuned —
the comparison is rarely run at equal token budgets. And whether the discrete
route survives at all, or whether continuous action experts make the question
moot. The honest position is that the field is still measuring, and a
rate-distortion comparison on held-out chunks costs almost nothing to run
yourself before committing.
</Step>
</Steps>
