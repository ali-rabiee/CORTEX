## The problem, stated as rate-distortion

Given an action chunk $a \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times d}$, choose an encoder
$Q: \mathbb{R}^{H\times d} \to \{1,\ldots,V\}^{L}$ producing $L$ tokens from a
vocabulary of size $V$, and a decoder $Q^{-1}$. Two quantities:

$$
\underbrace{R = L\log_2 V \ \text{bits}}_{\text{rate}}
\qquad\qquad
\underbrace{D = \mathbb{E}\big\|a - Q^{-1}(Q(a))\big\|^2}_{\text{distortion}}
$$

Minimise $R$ at a tolerable $D$. What makes this a real engineering problem
rather than an abstract one is that $L$, not $R$, is what you pay: autoregressive
decoding costs one backbone forward pass per token.

## Scalar quantisation

<Derivation title="The $w^2/12$ law, and the 6 dB per bit rule">

Bin a scalar uniformly with width $w$ and decode to the bin centre. If the value
is uniform within its bin, the error $e$ is uniform on $[-w/2, w/2]$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[e] = 0,
\qquad
\mathbb{E}[e^2] = \frac{1}{w}\int_{-w/2}^{w/2} e^2\,\mathrm{d}e = \frac{w^2}{12}
$$

With range $\rho$ and $B$ bins, $w = \rho/B$, so

$$
D_{\text{scalar}} = \frac{\rho^2}{12 B^2}
$$

**The exponent is the useful part.** Each additional bit halves $w$ and therefore
quarters $D$ — the standard 6 dB per bit. So the exchange rate is fixed: one more
bit per sample buys a factor of four in mean squared error and costs you rate.

**Numbers.** $\rho = 0.10$ m, $B = 256$: RMS error
$\rho/(B\sqrt{12}) \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-4}$ m, worst case $\rho/2B \approx 0.2$ mm.

**Rate for a whole chunk.** $L = Hd$ tokens, so
$R = Hd\log_2 B$. At $H=16$, $d=7$, $B=256$: 112 tokens, 896 bits.

**The assumption doing the work** is that the value is uniform within its bin,
which requires the bins to be placed where the data is. Uniform bins over a
heavy-tailed action distribution violate it badly — most mass lands in a few
central bins and the effective $B$ is far smaller than the nominal one. Quantile
binning restores it approximately, and is nearly free.

</Derivation>

## Transform coding

<Derivation title="Why an orthonormal transform lets you discard coefficients safely">

Let $T$ be orthonormal ($T^\top T = I$) applied along the time axis, giving
coefficients $C = Ta$. **Parseval's theorem** says energy is preserved:

$$
\|a\|^2 = \|C\|^2
$$

More useful, the same holds for the *error*. If $\hat C$ keeps some coefficients
and zeroes others, and $\hat a = T^\top \hat C$:

$$
\|a - \hat a\|^2 = \|T^\top(C - \hat C)\|^2 = \|C - \hat C\|^2
$$

**So the time-domain reconstruction error equals the discarded energy**, computed
without reconstructing anything. Two consequences.

First, coefficient selection becomes a solved optimisation: keeping the $k$
largest-magnitude coefficients provably minimises the error for that budget.

Second, $k$ can be set from a *target error* rather than by trial — take the
smallest $k$ whose discarded energy falls under your tolerance.

**Why the DCT specifically.** Robot motion is band-limited: actuators have finite
bandwidth and demonstrators do not command high frequencies. Measured on typical
smooth 16-step chunks, the top 4 of 16 DCT coefficients carry about 99% of the
energy, and the top 2 about 91%. So

$$
L: \ 112 \longrightarrow 28 \text{ tokens},
\qquad
R: \ 896 \longrightarrow 224 \text{ bits}
$$

at essentially unchanged distortion — a 4x reduction in decode steps.

**The assumption is smoothness.** A chunk containing a genuine discontinuity — a
contact event, a gripper snap — has energy spread across the spectrum, and
truncation blurs exactly the transition that mattered. That is the failure mode
to watch for, and it is task-dependent rather than universal.

</Derivation>

## Vector quantisation

Rather than binning each component, learn a codebook
$\mathcal{E} = \{e_1,\ldots,e_K\} \subset \mathbb{R}^{m}$ and encode by
nearest neighbour:

$$
z_q = e_{k^*},
\qquad
k^* = \arg\min_k \|z_e - e_k\|_2
$$

where $z_e$ is an encoder output over a whole chunk (or a segment of one).

<Derivation title="The two separate reasons VQ beats scalar quantisation">

**The memory advantage.** Scalar quantisation treats components independently, so
its cells are hypercubes aligned to the axes. If the components are correlated,
most of those cells contain almost no probability mass — the data lies near a
lower-dimensional manifold and the code is spending resolution on empty space.
VQ places codewords where the data is, so this advantage grows with the strength
of the dependence. For robot actions it is large: joints move together during a
coordinated motion, and successive timesteps are near-duplicates.

**The space-filling advantage.** Even with *independent* components, hypercubes
are not the most efficient way to tile $\mathbb{R}^m$ for minimising mean squared
error at a fixed cell count — better-shaped cells do better, and the gap grows
with dimension. This is purely geometric and exists regardless of the
distribution.

**Keeping them apart matters** because they respond to different things. The
memory advantage says "use VQ when your components are correlated"; the
space-filling advantage says "use VQ in high dimensions". Robot action chunks
are both, which is why the gains are substantial.

</Derivation>

## Training a codebook, and its failure mode

The encoder-quantiser-decoder is trained with three terms:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\|a - D(z_q)\|^2}_{\text{reconstruction}}
+ \underbrace{\|\operatorname{sg}[z_e] - e\|^2}_{\text{codebook}}
+ \beta \underbrace{\|z_e - \operatorname{sg}[e]\|^2}_{\text{commitment}}
$$

with $\operatorname{sg}$ the stop-gradient. The quantisation step is not
differentiable, so gradients are passed to the encoder by the **straight-through
estimator**: copy $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z_q$ directly to $z_e$.

<Warning>
**Codebook collapse** is the characteristic failure and it is self-reinforcing. A
codeword that is never the nearest neighbour receives no gradient from the
codebook term, so it never moves toward the data, so it stays unused. Runs
routinely end with a small fraction of a large codebook active — and the symptom
is a reconstruction error that plateaus well above what the nominal codebook size
should achieve, with no other sign of trouble.

Three standard remedies. **EMA updates** move codewords toward the running mean
of their assigned encodings rather than by gradient, which is more stable.
**Dead-code restarts** reinitialise unused entries to randomly chosen encoder
outputs, putting them back where data is. And the **commitment loss** $\beta$
keeps encoder outputs from drifting away from the codebook faster than it can
follow. Always log codebook utilisation; it is the diagnostic that makes this
visible.
</Warning>

## Where the schemes sit

<Steps>
<Step title="Scalar quantile binning">
Rate $Hd\log_2 B$. Exploits only the marginal distribution per dimension. No
training, no failure modes, and a perfectly reasonable default.
</Step>

<Step title="DCT truncation plus scalar binning">
Rate $kd\log_2 B$ with $k \ll H$. Exploits temporal smoothness, needs no
training, and has an error you can compute in the transform domain before
committing to $k$.
</Step>

<Step title="Vector quantisation">
Rate $L\log_2 K$ with $L$ typically small. Exploits whatever structure the data
has, including dependencies you did not anticipate — at the cost of a training
procedure whose failure mode is quiet.
</Step>

<Step title="Continuous head">
Rate is not defined; there is no code. Distortion is limited by the data rather
than the representation, and multi-modality has to be supplied by the head's own
machinery rather than falling out of a categorical distribution.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
The reason to prefer a discrete code, beyond interface compatibility, is that a
categorical distribution over bins represents multi-modality — mass on two
separated actions with nothing between them — which an MSE regression cannot,
since its minimiser is the conditional mean. Tokenising therefore buys the same
property diffusion policies buy, by an entirely different route, and that is a
substantive argument rather than a workaround for the transformer's interface.
</KeyIdea>
