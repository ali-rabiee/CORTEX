  prompt: "Give your interview answer on action tokenisation. What framing do you lead with, and how do you decide between schemes?"
  keyPoints:

    - "Name the wasted structure: chunks are smooth, so per-sample binning pays full price for information it already has — roughly 99% of a 16-step chunk's energy is in its first four DCT coefficients."

interview:
  answer: >-
    The framing I'd lead with is that this is a coding problem. A transformer
    emits discrete symbols and a robot action is a vector of reals, so you're
    choosing a code for a continuous signal — which makes it rate-distortion,
    trading bits against reconstruction error. And the currency is specific:
    autoregressive decoding costs one backbone forward pass per token, so
    sequence length is what you actually pay, and reconstruction error is what
    you buy with it. Once you see it that way the design space is navigable
    rather than a list of tricks. The naive code is to bin each dimension at
    each timestep — RT-1 and OpenVLA do this, 256 bins per dimension — and it
    works, but it's wasteful in a specific way. Action chunks are smooth,
    because actuators are band-limited and demonstrators don't command high
    frequencies, so consecutive samples are highly correlated and largely
    predictable from each other. Per-sample binning spends a full token on each
    one as though it carried independent information. If you measure it, the
    first four of sixteen DCT coefficients carry about 99 percent of a typical
    chunk's energy — so a 16-step 7-DoF chunk that costs 112 tokens costs 28 in
    a frequency basis at essentially the same fidelity. That's a four-times
    reduction in decode steps, which is the FAST idea. The other direction is
    vector quantisation: learn a codebook of chunk prototypes, which exploits
    whatever structure the data actually has rather than the structure you
    assumed. That has two separate advantages over scalar quantisation worth
    keeping apart — the memory advantage from exploiting correlation between
    components, which is large for robot actions since joints move together,
    and the space-filling advantage which is purely geometric and exists even
    for independent components. The cost is codebook collapse, which is
    self-reinforcing because an unused codeword gets no gradient, and it
    presents only as a plateaued reconstruction error with every loss curve
    looking healthy. And I'd want to say that discretising isn't purely a
    concession to the transformer's interface. A categorical distribution over
    bins can put mass on two separated actions with nothing in between, which
    an MSE regression structurally can't because its minimiser is the
    conditional mean. So tokenising buys the same multi-modality that diffusion
    policies buy, by a completely different route.
  followUps:
    - q: "How would you actually choose a scheme for a new project?"
      a: >-
        With two numbers and a curve, before training any policy. The first
        number is the quantisation floor against my precision requirement: 256
        uniform bins over a plus-or-minus five centimetre delta gives a worst
        case around 0.2 millimetres, which is irrelevant for picking up a mug
        and comparable to the clearance in a tight connector insertion. The
        second is decode steps against my latency budget — H times d sequential
        passes through the backbone, so 16 by 7 is 112, and if that doesn't fit
        the period then no amount of tuning the model will help. Then I'd plot
        reconstruction error against token count on held-out chunks for the
        candidate codes, which costs almost nothing since no policy is involved.
        If both numbers say the simple code is fine, I'd use the simple code and
        say so — that's a defensible answer, not a lazy one.
    - q: "What would you check beyond mean reconstruction error?"
      a: >-
        Error at discontinuities, because means hide localised failures.
        Truncating high-frequency coefficients is exactly the operation that
        smooths a step, so a transform code can reconstruct 99 percent of the
        energy while turning a crisp gripper close into a ramp — and the mean
        barely moves, because the discontinuity occupies a handful of samples
        out of sixteen. So I'd stratify the reconstruction error by whether the
        chunk contains a contact or gripper event; that column is where the
        codes actually differ. And the check I'd run first is a round trip:
        encode and decode a whole demonstration and physically replay the
        reconstruction. If the task fails on the decoded demonstration, that's
        an upper bound on the entire pipeline — a policy can't outperform the
        reconstruction of its own training targets.
    - q: "Why does VQ beat scalar quantisation? Be precise."
      a: >-
        Two separate reasons that respond to different things. The memory
        advantage is that scalar quantisation treats components independently,
        so its cells are axis-aligned hypercubes, and if the components are
        correlated most of those cells contain almost no probability mass —
        you're spending resolution on empty space. VQ puts codewords where the
        data is. That's large for robot actions, since joints move together
        during a coordinated motion and successive timesteps are near
        duplicates. The space-filling advantage is purely geometric: even for
        independent components, hypercubes aren't the most efficient
        tessellation for minimising mean squared error at a fixed cell count,
        and the gap grows with dimension. Keeping them apart matters because
        one says "use VQ when your components are correlated" and the other says
        "use VQ in high dimensions", and action chunks are both.
    - q: "Is the discrete route going to survive, given flow-matching heads?"
      a: >-
        I'd say it depends on the requirement rather than on which is better,
        and I'd be sceptical of anyone claiming it's settled. Staying discrete
        keeps three things: the pretrained embedding table and output head
        transfer untouched, since RT-2's trick of overwriting unused vocabulary
        slots means actions are indistinguishable from language to the model;
        multi-modality comes free from the softmax; and the tooling is simpler.
        Going continuous removes the resolution floor and the sequential decode
        entirely, at the cost of a separate head with its own training dynamics
        and its own way of representing modes. FAST is the interesting
        development because it shows the decode-length objection to the discrete
        route was addressable rather than fundamental — so the remaining
        argument is really about precision, which is a task property and not a
        general one.
  traps:
    - "Presenting tokenisation as a formatting concession to the transformer. It is a code, the trade is rate against distortion, and discretisation buys multi-modality as a genuine benefit."
    - "Asserting the quantisation error is a problem without computing it. About 0.2 mm at 256 bins over a 10 cm range settles the question for most tasks."
    - "Treating scalar and vector quantisation as differing only in sophistication. The memory and space-filling advantages are separate and respond to different properties of the data."
    - "Choosing a code by policy success rate. Reconstruction error against token count on held-out chunks answers the question far more cheaply and without confounding."

Compressing the code attacks the multiplier directly and costs only reconstruction error, which you can measure in advance. Option 1 reduces bits per token but not the token count, so it barely helps latency."

## Reading the situation

<Steps>
<Step title="Policy too slow → count decode steps before shrinking the model">
$H \times d$ sequential passes dominates. Compressing the code attacks the
multiplier; a smaller backbone attacks the per-pass cost and costs capacity.
</Step>

<Step title="Precise task failing at the last millimetre → check the floor">
Compute $\rho/2B$. If it is comparable to the task's tolerance, the code is the
ceiling and no policy improvement passes it.
</Step>

<Step title="Actions clipped or coarse → the quantile range">
Histogram the emitted tokens. Mass at the extremes means the range is too narrow;
mass in the centre means it is too wide and resolution is wasted.
</Step>

<Step title="VQ reconstruction plateaus with healthy losses → codebook collapse">
Log utilisation. Self-reinforcing, invisible in the loss curves, and fixed by EMA
updates and dead-code restarts rather than by the optimiser.
</Step>

<Step title="Smooth motion fine, gripper transitions mushy → smoothness prior">
A transform code truncating high frequencies blurs steps. Stratify reconstruction
error by contact and gripper events before blaming the policy.
</Step>
</Steps>

<InterviewAnswer />

<Warning>
The framing is what carries this answer. Most candidates describe tokenisation as
a formatting step — how you make a continuous thing fit a discrete interface.
Saying instead that it is a code, with a rate-distortion trade whose currency is
sequence length, immediately explains why the naive scheme is wasteful, why
transform coding helps, what vector quantisation is exploiting, and how you would
choose between them without training a policy. It also lets you make the
non-obvious point that discretisation buys multi-modality, which turns an
apparent compromise into a genuine design advantage.
</Warning>
