## A coding problem in disguise

A transformer emits symbols from a finite vocabulary. A robot action is a vector
of real numbers. Something has to bridge that, and the bridge is a **code**.

Once you say it that way, the whole design space becomes familiar: you are
choosing how to represent a continuous signal with a finite alphabet, trading
**bits against distortion**. That is the same problem as designing an image or
audio codec, and the same tools apply.

<KeyIdea>
The currency is sequence length. Autoregressive decoding costs one forward pass
through the backbone per token, so a code that halves the token count halves your
inference latency. Reconstruction error is what you buy with those tokens. Every
tokenisation scheme is a point on that curve.
</KeyIdea>

<Intuition>
Storing a photograph by writing down every pixel value works and nobody does it,
because photographs have structure — neighbouring pixels are similar, and most of
the visual energy is in low spatial frequencies. A codec spends its bits where
the information actually is.

An action chunk is a photograph of a motion. It has exactly the same kind of
structure, and the obvious code ignores all of it.
</Intuition>

## Where the naive code wastes its budget

<Figure src="/figures/action-tokenization-rate.svg"
        alt="A smooth action signal over 16 timesteps. On the right, two codes: sixteen tokens from binning every sample, versus four tokens from binning the first four frequency coefficients with the rest discarded."
        caption="Per-sample binning spends one token on each sample however predictable that sample was. Transforming first concentrates the energy, and four coefficients reconstruct the chunk to about 99% of its energy." />

Robot motion is smooth. Actuators cannot produce arbitrarily high frequencies and
demonstrators do not command them, so a trajectory is band-limited — and a
band-limited signal carries far less information than its sample count suggests.

Measured on typical smooth chunks, the first four of sixteen frequency-domain
coefficients carry around 99% of the energy. So a 16-step, 7-DoF chunk that costs
112 tokens under per-sample binning costs 28 in a frequency basis, at essentially
the same fidelity.

<Steps>
<Step title="Temporal redundancy — the biggest waste">
Consecutive samples are highly correlated. Any code that treats them as
independent is paying full price for information it already has.
</Step>

<Step title="Cross-dimension correlation — the second waste">
Joint angles move together during a coordinated motion. Quantising each dimension
independently cannot exploit that; a joint code over the whole action vector can.
</Step>

<Step title="Bin placement — the smallest but easiest to fix">
Uniform bins over the observed range waste resolution on the tails. Quantile
binning puts the bins where the data is, which is nearly free.
</Step>
</Steps>

## The design space

<Steps>
<Step title="Scalar quantisation">
Bin each dimension independently. Simple, no training, and the only structure it
exploits is the marginal distribution per dimension. This is what RT-2 and
OpenVLA do, and it is a perfectly reasonable default.
</Step>

<Step title="Transform coding">
Move to a basis where energy concentrates — a discrete cosine transform over the
time axis — keep the significant coefficients, and quantise those. Exploits
temporal smoothness explicitly, needs no training, and is the FAST approach.
</Step>

<Step title="Vector quantisation">
Learn a codebook of action-chunk prototypes and emit codebook indices. Exploits
whatever structure the data actually has, including correlations you did not
anticipate, at the cost of a training procedure with its own failure mode.
</Step>

<Step title="Do not tokenise at all">
Attach a continuous generative head — diffusion or flow matching — and skip the
code entirely. No quantisation floor, and you give up the property that made
tokenisation attractive: that actions are indistinguishable from language to the
rest of the model.
</Step>
</Steps>

## What discretisation buys beyond compatibility

<KeyIdea>
There is a reason to discretise that has nothing to do with fitting a
transformer's interface. A categorical distribution over bins can put probability
on two separated actions with nothing between them, which an MSE regression
structurally cannot — its minimiser is the conditional mean. So tokenising gets
you multi-modality for free, by exactly the same route diffusion policies get it,
and that is a substantive benefit rather than a workaround.
</KeyIdea>

<Warning>
The cost is a resolution floor, and it is worth computing rather than debating.
At 256 uniform bins over a $\pm 5$ cm end-effector delta, the worst-case error is
about 0.2 mm — irrelevant for picking up a mug and comparable to the clearance in
a tight connector insertion. So discretisation is not a general weakness; it is a
floor that binds only on precision tasks, and that is the question to ask before
reaching for a continuous head.
</Warning>
