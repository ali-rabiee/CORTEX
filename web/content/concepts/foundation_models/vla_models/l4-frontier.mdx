## Scale the robot data first

<PaperCard refKey="brohan2022rt1" />

RT-1 established the recipe before the web entered it: one transformer,
130k demonstrations, 700+ instructions, actions discretised into tokens. The
finding was that joint training across many tasks *beats* per-task policies once
the data is large enough — the negative-transfer regime is outgrown rather than
solved. The generalisation, though, stayed inside the robot dataset's
distribution.

## Then borrow the web

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="driess2023palme" />
<PaperCard refKey="brohan2023rt2" />
</PaperGrid>

PaLM-E makes the architectural move: inject continuous sensor embeddings
directly into a language model's token stream, so observations are first-class
inputs rather than a caption. RT-2 makes the training move: co-fine-tune a
web-pretrained VLM on robot data with actions as tokens.

<KeyIdea>
RT-2's evidence is behaviour the robot dataset cannot explain. Responding
correctly to "pick up the extinct animal" or "move the banana to the sum of two
plus one" requires concepts that appear nowhere in the demonstrations. That is
the cleanest available demonstration that web pretraining contributes something
*orthogonal* to robot data scale — and it is why the field reorganised around
VLM backbones rather than around bigger robot datasets.
</KeyIdea>

## Pool the robots

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="oneill2023openx" />
<PaperCard refKey="kim2024openvla" />
</PaperGrid>

Open X-Embodiment pools data from 22 embodiments and shows a single policy
trained across all of them beats per-embodiment training — positive transfer
extending past tasks to bodies. OpenVLA then made a competitive generalist open:
7B parameters, open weights, and an explicit LoRA recipe, which is why it is the
model most groups actually build on.

## The current argument is about the action head

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="black2024pi0" />
<PaperCard refKey="pertsch2025fast" />
<PaperCard refKey="kim2025oft" />
</PaperGrid>

With the backbone settled, the bottleneck moved to how actions leave the model.
$\pi_0$ removes token decoding entirely in favour of a flow-matching action
expert emitting continuous chunks at 50 Hz. FAST keeps discrete tokens and
attacks the same cost from the other side, compressing a chunk in a
frequency-domain representation so it needs far fewer tokens. The OFT study
sweeps the surrounding design space — parallel decoding, chunk length, head type
— and finds large gains from choices entirely orthogonal to the backbone.

<KeyIdea>
Reading these three as a set gives a more accurate account of the field than
"scale it up". The vision-language model is largely a solved import; the live
disagreement is the action interface, and it is being fought over latency and
precision rather than over semantics.
</KeyIdea>

## What to be sceptical about

<Warning>
Evaluation is the weakest part of this literature, and saying so specifically is
worth more than either enthusiasm or dismissal. Real-robot rollouts are
expensive, so success rates rest on small samples. Every group has different
hardware, scenes, objects, and task lists, so cross-paper comparison is largely
uncontrolled. And the parts that most affect the number — which objects were
chosen, how many attempts, who reset the scene, how a partial success was scored
— are rarely reported in enough detail to reproduce.

The consequence is not that the results are wrong; RT-2's semantic
generalisation is a qualitative effect too large to be an artefact. It is that
*rankings* between recent methods are much softer than the tables suggest, and
that a claimed few-point improvement on a bespoke task suite should be read as
weak evidence.
</Warning>

<Steps>
<Step title="What is well established">
Web pretraining contributes semantic generalisation that robot data alone does
not. Pooling across tasks and embodiments helps at scale. Action chunking and
the action-head design materially affect both speed and success.
</Step>

<Step title="What is not">
Whether VLAs are better at *hard* manipulation — precise, contact-rich,
long-horizon — as opposed to a wider range of easy ones. Whether the ranking
among current models survives a controlled evaluation. Whether the recipe keeps
scaling with robot data, given how much of it there is.
</Step>
</Steps>
