## A VLM that can act

A vision-language model takes an image and a question and produces text. A
vision-language-action model takes an image and an *instruction* and produces
robot actions. Structurally, that is a smaller change than it sounds.

<Figure src="/figures/vla-architecture.svg"
        alt="Image patches and an instruction feed a pretrained VLM transformer. One branch decodes text tokens; the other decodes action tokens representing binned end-effector deltas and gripper state."
        caption="The backbone, the attention, and the autoregressive decoding are unchanged. What differs is what the output symbols mean — and the bet is that everything the model learned about cups survives that substitution." />

<Intuition>
You hire someone who has read enormously and seen photographs of everything, but
has never operated machinery. You do not have to teach them what a mug is, that
handles are for holding, or that liquids spill. You have to teach them to move
the arm.

The wager is that the second job is much smaller than the first — and that the
first was already done, for free, by someone else.
</Intuition>

## Why this is the dominant approach

Robot data is small. The largest pooled robot dataset is on the order of a
million trajectories; web image-text pretraining is on the order of billions of
examples. That ratio is not going to change soon, so the question becomes where
the knowledge comes from.

<KeyIdea>
The claim VLAs make is that *concepts* can be learned from the web and *motor
skill* from robot data, separately. RT-2's headline evidence is that it responds
correctly to objects and instructions that appear nowhere in its robot data —
"pick up the extinct animal", "move the banana to the sum of two plus one". No
amount of scaling the robot dataset produces that; it comes from the backbone.
</KeyIdea>

## Three decisions define a VLA

<Steps>
<Step title="How to represent actions">
Discretise each dimension into bins and emit them as tokens in the existing
vocabulary, so nothing about the model changes. Or attach a continuous head — a
diffusion or flow-matching action expert — that keeps precision at the cost of
being a new component. This is the live disagreement in the area, and Level 2
works through the trade.
</Step>

<Step title="What to train, and on what mixture">
Fine-tuning only on robot data causes the model to forget the semantics you
adopted it for. **Co-fine-tuning** — continuing to train on web vision-language
data alongside robot data — is what preserves the transfer, and it is one of
RT-2's more important practical findings.
</Step>

<Step title="How to run it fast enough">
A 7-billion-parameter model emitting one token at a time, at a control rate a
robot can use, is a real engineering problem. Action chunking amortises it;
so does parallel decoding, quantisation, and putting the expensive part on a
separate action expert.
</Step>
</Steps>

## What it is underneath

<Warning>
A VLA is trained with a supervised next-token loss on demonstrations. That is
**behaviour cloning**, with a very large pretrained initialiser. Everything that
is true of cloning is true here: the policy creates its own state distribution
at test time, so covariate shift applies unchanged, and it cannot exceed the
behaviour it was shown. The scale and the pretraining change what the model
*knows*; they do not change what the objective is optimising.
</Warning>

One genuine improvement over naive cloning is worth naming. Autoregressive
decoding over discrete tokens can represent a multi-modal action distribution —
the model can put probability on "go left" and "go right" without inventing
"straight ahead" — which is exactly what MSE regression cannot do. Discretising
actions buys the same thing diffusion policies buy, by a different route.

## What actually generalises

<Warning>
Be precise here, because the marketing is not. VLAs generalise well over
**semantics**: unseen objects, novel phrasings, categories the robot data never
contained. They do not yet generalise notably better over **dexterity**:
precise insertion, contact-rich assembly, or anything where the difficulty is
motor rather than conceptual.

That asymmetry follows directly from the pretraining. Web image-text data is
rich in what things are and silent on how they behave under contact. So the
right summary is that VLAs widen the task distribution a single policy can
cover, rather than raising the ceiling on any individual hard task.
</Warning>
