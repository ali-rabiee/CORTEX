interview:
  answer: >-
    A vision-language-action model is a VLM pretrained on web image-text data
    with actions added as an output modality — usually by discretising each
    action dimension into bins and emitting them as tokens in the existing
    vocabulary, so the embedding table, the attention, and the softmax head are
    all untouched. The bet is specific: robot data is tiny compared to the web
    and always will be, so let the web teach concepts and let demonstrations
    teach motor skill. If the backbone already knows what a mug is, your
    demonstrations only have to teach grasping. RT-2 is the evidence, and what
    makes it convincing is that the behaviour can't be explained by the robot
    dataset — responding correctly to "pick up the extinct animal" requires a
    concept that appears nowhere in the demonstrations, so no amount of scaling
    them produces it. Two things I'd want to be precise about. First, it's still
    behaviour cloning. The objective is maximum likelihood on a fixed set of
    demonstrations, so covariate shift applies unchanged — the policy generates
    its own state distribution at test time — and it can't exceed its
    demonstrators. Pretraining changes the prior and the sample efficiency, not
    the structure of what's being optimised. One genuine improvement over naive
    cloning is that a categorical distribution over bins represents
    multimodality, which MSE regression structurally can't — so discretisation
    buys the same thing diffusion policies buy, by a different route. Second,
    what generalises is semantics, not dexterity. VLAs handle unseen objects and
    rephrased instructions well and are not obviously more precise than a good
    task-specific policy on contact-rich work. That asymmetry follows from the
    pretraining data: web images are rich in what things are and silent on how
    they behave under contact. On the engineering side, the thing that bites is
    latency. Autoregressive decoding of H times d action tokens means H times d
    sequential passes through a seven-billion-parameter backbone, so the budget
    is prefill plus H-d times the per-token decode, amortised over however many
    actions you execute per chunk. Every speedup in the area attacks that
    multiplier — longer chunks, parallel decoding, compressing the chunk into
    fewer tokens like FAST does, or replacing the decode loop entirely with a
    flow-matching action expert the way pi-zero does. That's the live
    disagreement in the field right now, and it's about the action interface
    rather than the backbone.
  followUps:
    - q: "Discrete action tokens or a continuous head — which would you pick?"
      a: >-
        It depends on precision and on how much I care about reusing the
        pretrained output head. The quantisation cost is worth computing rather
        than arguing about: 256 uniform bins over a plus-or-minus five
        centimetre end-effector delta gives a worst case of about 0.2
        millimetres, which is irrelevant for picking up a mug and on the same
        order as the clearance in a tight connector insertion. So for
        pick-and-place I'd stay discrete — nothing about the model changes, you
        get multimodality free from the softmax, and the tooling is simpler. For
        precise assembly, or if I need a control rate the decode loop can't
        support, a continuous head is the right call, and I'd expect to pay for
        it with a new component that has its own training dynamics and its own
        multimodality machinery.
    - q: "You fine-tune on 300 demos for a new task and it gets worse at everything else. What happened?"
      a: >-
        Catastrophic forgetting. The semantic knowledge lives in the shared
        backbone, and 300 narrow demonstrations can't support updating seven
        billion parameters without dragging the weights away from the pretrained
        solution — you fit the demos perfectly and lose the generalisation you
        adopted the model for. The two fixes are co-fine-tuning, keeping web
        vision-language data in the training mixture so the pretrained solution
        stays a constraint rather than a starting point, and LoRA, constraining
        the update to a low-rank subspace on the attention projections. What
        makes this easy to ship by accident is that held-out demonstration loss
        looks fine — the forgetting only shows up if you evaluate on objects and
        phrasings outside the fine-tuning set, which is the evaluation people
        skip.
    - q: "How would you evaluate a VLA properly?"
      a: >-
        I'd be honest that this is the weakest part of the field. Real rollouts
        are expensive so samples are small, every lab has different hardware and
        task lists, and the details that most move the number — which objects,
        how many attempts, who resets the scene, how partial success is scored —
        are usually underspecified. So cross-paper rankings are much softer than
        the tables suggest. What I'd insist on for my own evaluation is
        stratifying by generalisation axis rather than reporting one success
        rate: seen objects and seen instructions, unseen objects with seen
        instructions, seen objects with rephrased instructions, and genuinely
        novel combinations. Those four tell you what the model actually bought
        you, and they'd expose the case where a model looks good only because
        the test set is close to training.
    - q: "Given all that, would you use a VLA on a real project?"
      a: >-
        For a task set that's broad and semantically varied — many objects, many
        instructions, humans phrasing things differently — yes, because that's
        exactly the axis where the pretraining pays and where per-task policies
        get unmanageable. For one high-precision task in a fixed cell, I'd
        expect a task-specific diffusion or ACT-style policy to match or beat it
        at a fraction of the inference cost, and I'd want the comparison run
        before committing. The middle path I'd actually reach for is a VLA as a
        pretrained initialiser adapted with LoRA on a few hundred
        demonstrations, evaluated specifically for out-of-set generalisation to
        confirm the pretraining survived the fine-tuning.

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Ignores the instruction → conditioning is dead">
Feed an empty or scrambled instruction and measure the drop. Near zero means an
expensive unconditional policy. Usually a data problem: each task collected in
its own scene, so the image already determines the instruction.
</Step>

<Step title="Fine-tuned well, lost general ability → catastrophic forgetting">
Held-out demonstration loss looks fine; out-of-set objects and phrasings
collapse. Co-fine-tune with web data, or switch to LoRA. This is invisible
unless you evaluate outside the fine-tuning distribution.
</Step>

<Step title="Actions clipped or coarse → the tokeniser range">
Histogram emitted bins. Mass at bin 0 or 255 means the quantile range is too
narrow; mass in a few central bins means it is too wide and you are wasting
resolution.
</Step>

<Step title="Drifts over long horizons → covariate shift, unchanged">
Nothing about the VLA formulation addresses it. Chunking helps by reducing
decision points; recovery data and perturbed demonstrations help more.
</Step>

<Step title="Too slow → count decode steps, not parameters">
$H \times d$ sequential passes is the cost. Attack the multiplier before
reaching for quantisation.
</Step>
</Steps>
