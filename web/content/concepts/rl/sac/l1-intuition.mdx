## Good, and undecided

Standard RL asks for the policy that collects the most reward. SAC asks for
something slightly different:

$$
\text{maximise} \quad \underbrace{\text{expected reward}}_{\text{do well}}
\;+\;\alpha\,\underbrace{\text{policy entropy}}_{\text{stay uncommitted}}
$$

Entropy measures how spread out the action distribution is. So SAC is being
asked: get the reward, and among all the ways of getting it, be the one that
keeps the most options open.

<Intuition>
Two routes home take almost exactly the same time. A greedy commuter measures
both once, finds one is 30 seconds faster, and takes it every single day — and
never notices when it closes for roadworks.

A commuter who keeps taking both, weighted by how good they are, pays a few
seconds a day and is never stranded. That is the entropy term, and note that
it is not "explore randomly" — the second route is chosen because it is nearly
as good, not because a coin came up heads.
</Intuition>

## Why this is not just an exploration bonus

<Figure src="/figures/sac-soft-vs-greedy.svg"
        alt="Left: a Q function with two nearly equal peaks and a greedy policy placing all probability on the taller one. Right: the same Q with a maximum-entropy policy proportional to exp(Q/alpha), which keeps substantial probability on both peaks."
        caption="The optimal max-entropy policy is a Boltzmann distribution over Q. The stochasticity is shaped by value — good actions get more probability, near-equal actions get near-equal probability — and it is never annealed away." />

<KeyIdea>
Epsilon-greedy adds uniform, uninformed noise to a policy that would otherwise
be deterministic, and you turn it off over time. SAC changes what "optimal"
means, so the best policy *is* stochastic, in a way that depends on how good
each action is. That is why the resulting controllers tend to be robust to
disturbances: they never learned to depend on one narrow strategy.
</KeyIdea>

The value function inherits the same softening. Instead of
$V(s) = \max_a Q(s,a)$, the max-entropy value is a log-sum-exp — a smooth
maximum. That is where the "soft" in Soft Actor-Critic comes from, and it is a
statement about the operator, not about the policy being gentle.

## The pieces, and what each one is for

<Steps>
<Step title="A replay buffer">
Every transition is stored and reused thousands of times. This is the entire
reason SAC is roughly an order of magnitude more sample-efficient than PPO, and
the reason it is the default when environment steps are expensive.
</Step>

<Step title="Twin critics, and take the minimum">
Two independently initialised $Q$ networks; targets use $\min(Q_1, Q_2)$. Taking
a max over noisy estimates is upward-biased, and the min is a blunt but
effective counterweight. Bootstrapping would otherwise propagate the inflation
backwards through the whole chain.
</Step>

<Step title="A stochastic actor with reparameterised sampling">
The action is $a = \tanh(\mu_\theta(s) + \sigma_\theta(s)\odot\epsilon)$, which
is differentiable with respect to the policy parameters. That lets gradients
flow from $Q$ through the action into the actor — a much lower-variance
estimator than the score-function form policy gradients use.
</Step>

<Step title="An automatically tuned temperature">
$\alpha$ trades reward against entropy, and the right value changes over
training and across reward scales. The second SAC paper made it self-tuning by
targeting a fixed entropy level, which removed the algorithm's most annoying
hyperparameter.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
SAC bootstraps, uses function approximation, and trains on off-policy data — all
three legs of the deadly triad at once. Every stabiliser above is there for a
named reason, and none is optional. If you are debugging SAC, match the symptom
to the mechanism: exploding $Q$ values are a triad problem, a critic that is
stably wrong in the optimistic direction is an overestimation problem, and a
policy that goes deterministic early is a temperature problem.
</Warning>
