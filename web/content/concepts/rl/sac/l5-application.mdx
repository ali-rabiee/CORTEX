interview:
  answer: >-
    SAC is an off-policy actor-critic that optimises reward plus the entropy of
    the policy. That's the defining choice and it's worth being precise about
    what it changes. It isn't an exploration bonus added to a policy that would
    otherwise be deterministic — it changes the objective, so the optimal
    policy is genuinely stochastic. If you solve the max-entropy problem for a
    fixed Q, you get a Boltzmann distribution: pi proportional to exp of Q over
    the temperature. It's a two-line Lagrangian argument, and substituting it
    back gives a value function that's a log-sum-exp instead of a max, which is
    where the word soft comes from. The practical consequence is that the
    policy keeps probability on every action that's nearly as good, weighted by
    how good, and it never anneals that away — which is why SAC controllers
    tend to be robust to perturbations rather than depending on one narrow
    strategy. In practice the actor loss is a KL projection of the policy onto
    exp of Q over alpha, and the intractable partition function drops out of
    the gradient because it doesn't depend on the policy. The actor uses the
    reparameterisation trick — action equals tanh of mu plus sigma times noise
    — so gradients flow through the action into Q. That matters a lot: the
    pathwise gradient sees grad-a Q, the shape of the value landscape, instead
    of just a scalar weight on grad log pi, so it's far lower variance than the
    score-function estimator. Where SAC actually wins is sample efficiency, and
    that comes from being off-policy — every transition goes in a replay buffer
    and gets reused thousands of times, where PPO gets a handful of epochs per
    batch. The price is that it has all three legs of the deadly triad at once.
    So the machinery isn't optional: twin critics with a min in the target
    against overestimation bias, target networks to slow the feedback loop, and
    an auto-tuned temperature so the entropy weight tracks the reward scale.
  followUps:
    - q: "How do you decide between SAC and PPO for a given project?"
      a: >-
        Almost entirely on what's scarce. If I have a fast parallel simulator
        and compute is cheap, PPO is simpler, more forgiving, and scales
        beautifully with parallel environments — it's on-policy, so it's
        missing a leg of the triad and just doesn't fail in the ways SAC does.
        If environment steps are the binding constraint, real hardware or a slow
        simulator, SAC's replay buffer is worth an order of magnitude and I'll
        pay for it in tuning. The secondary consideration is action space: SAC
        needs a differentiable sampling path for the reparameterisation, so
        discrete actions need a relaxation like Gumbel-softmax or a different
        formulation, whereas PPO handles both natively.
    - q: "Your SAC run has Q-values climbing steadily and returns flat. Diagnosis?"
      a: >-
        Climbing Q with flat returns means the critic is inflating and the
        policy is exploiting it, so I'd separate the two causes. Overestimation
        first: check the twin-critic min is actually being applied in the
        target, not just that two critics exist — using the mean instead of the
        min is a real and common bug. Then the triad: check the target network
        is being used and tau isn't so large it's effectively absent. Then I'd
        look at the entropy term in the critic target specifically. If the
        actor has the entropy bonus and the target doesn't, the critic is
        estimating the hard Q while the policy optimises the soft one, and
        they'll pull against each other. And I'd sanity check the reward scale,
        since values live on a scale of roughly R max over one minus gamma.
    - q: "Why the tanh correction, and what happens without it?"
      a: >-
        Actions are bounded so you squash a Gaussian sample through a tanh, and
        that's a change of variables, so the log-probability needs the
        log-Jacobian: minus the sum of log one minus tanh squared. Without it
        you're reporting the entropy of the pre-squash Gaussian, which is wrong
        in a specific direction — the correction blows up as the action
        approaches the bounds, which is exactly where a converged policy lives.
        So you overestimate entropy where it matters, the auto-tuner responds by
        driving alpha down, the policy saturates further, and you get a slow
        drift to determinism. It trains fine and quietly stops being a
        max-entropy method. That's my favourite example of a bug that never
        crashes.
    - q: "Where does SAC sit in robot learning right now?"
      a: >-
        Mostly locomotion and online fine-tuning rather than manipulation from
        scratch. For manipulation, imitation-based methods — action chunking,
        diffusion policies, VLAs — dominate pretraining, because demonstrations
        are easier to get than a reward function you'd trust and safe online
        exploration on a real arm is genuinely hard. Where SAC-family methods
        earn their place is when there's a well-specified reward and either a
        good simulator or a safe way to collect real interaction: legged
        locomotion, and increasingly RL fine-tuning on top of an
        imitation-pretrained policy, where you start from something competent
        and the exploration problem is much smaller.

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Q-values climbing, returns flat → overestimation">
Check the min over twin critics is actually in the *target* (using the mean is a
common and quiet bug), and that the critics were initialised independently.
Then check target-network $\tau$.
</Step>

<Step title="Policy goes deterministic, $\alpha$ collapses → the tanh correction">
The log-Jacobian term is missing or misapplied. Print the reported entropy
against a hand-computed value for a saturated action; the discrepancy grows near
the bounds.
</Step>

<Step title="Critic and actor seem to fight each other → inconsistent objectives">
Verify the entropy term appears in *both* the critic target and the actor loss.
If it is in only one, they are optimising different reward functions.
</Step>

<Step title="Unstable early, fine later → reward scale">
Values live on a scale of $R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$, and $\alpha$ has to trade
against that scale. Auto-tuning handles most of it, but an unnormalised reward
in the hundreds will make the early phase violent regardless.
</Step>

<Step title="Sample-efficient but never reaches good performance → exploration, not stability">
Max-entropy exploration is local: it keeps mass on nearby good actions, it does
not solve a hard exploration problem. If the reward is sparse and the good
behaviour is far away, no temperature setting finds it — that is a
demonstrations or shaping problem.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Two sentences carry this answer. The first is that maximum entropy changes the
objective rather than adding noise, with the Boltzmann policy as evidence you
can derive it. The second is that sample efficiency comes from the replay
buffer, not from entropy — because that is the sentence that lets you answer
"SAC or PPO?" as an engineering decision about what is scarce, rather than as a
preference.
</Warning>
