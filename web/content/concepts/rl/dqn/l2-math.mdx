## The loss

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s')\sim\mathcal{D}}
\Big[\big(\underbrace{r + \gamma(1-d)\max_{a'}Q_{\theta^-}(s',a')}_{\text{target } y,\ \text{no grad}} - Q_\theta(s,a)\big)^2\Big]
$$

with $\theta^-$ a periodically-copied snapshot and $d$ the terminal flag. Two
details in that expression carry most of the algorithm.

<Warning>
The max belongs to the **next** state, inside the target. The current state's
$Q$ is indexed by the action actually logged in the transition — `gather`, not
`max`. Swapping them means regressing $Q(s, \cdot)$ toward targets for actions
you never took, which is not Q-learning and is a surprisingly common bug that
still trains and still improves, just to the wrong thing.
</Warning>

## Why the target is held constant

<Derivation title="Semi-gradient, and what taking the full gradient would give you">

The squared Bellman error contains $\theta$ in both terms. Its true gradient is

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = -2\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(y - Q_\theta(s,a)\big)
\big(\nabla_\theta Q_\theta(s,a) - \gamma\nabla_\theta \max_{a'}Q_\theta(s',a')\big)\Big]
$$

DQN drops the second term inside the brackets. That is a choice, not an
oversight, and it has a name.

**What you get by keeping it.** The residual-gradient method is honest gradient
descent on a well-defined objective, and it converges more reliably. But its
minimiser is not what you want: with stochastic transitions, an unbiased
estimate of the squared expected residual needs *two independent* samples of
$s'$ from the same $(s,a)$ — the double-sampling problem — and with one sample
you minimise the expected squared residual instead, which penalises the
environment's inherent stochasticity as though it were model error. The result
is a systematically conservative value function.

**What the semi-gradient form is.** Treating $y$ as a constant makes each update
a regression step toward the Bellman backup, so the algorithm is *approximate
value iteration* rather than gradient descent on anything. That is why it has no
convergence guarantee under function approximation, and why the target network
and replay buffer are load-bearing rather than decorative.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
The frozen target makes this framing exact. With $\theta^-$ fixed, $y$ really is
a constant and each phase between target copies is an ordinary supervised
regression problem with a fixed dataset. The instability has not been removed —
it has been pushed onto the slow timescale of the target updates, where it is
manageable.
</KeyIdea>

## Overestimation, and the one-line fix

<Derivation title="Why $\max$ inflates, and why decoupling repairs it">

Write $\hat Q(s,a) = Q(s,a) + \varepsilon(s,a)$ with $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0$
per action. Since $\max$ is convex, Jensen gives

$$
\mathbb{E}\Big[\max_a \hat Q(s,a)\Big] \;\ge\; \max_a \mathbb{E}\big[\hat Q(s,a)\big] = \max_a Q(s,a)
$$

with strict inequality whenever the errors are non-degenerate. The mechanism is
selection: $\arg\max_a \hat Q$ preferentially picks actions whose $\varepsilon$
happened to be positive, and then reports the inflated value. Bootstrapping
carries it backwards, so the bias accumulates over the horizon rather than
averaging out.

**The fix.** Split the roles. Standard DQN computes

$$
y = r + \gamma\, \hat Q\Big(s',\, \arg\max_{a'} \hat Q(s',a')\Big)
$$

with the *same* $\hat Q$ in both places, so the selection noise and the
evaluation noise are the same random variable — selecting a positive error
guarantees evaluating one. Double DQN uses two:

$$
y = r + \gamma\, Q_{\theta^-}\Big(s',\, \arg\max_{a'} Q_{\theta}(s',a')\Big)
$$

Now the action is chosen by $\theta$'s noise and scored by $\theta^-$'s. Given
the selected action, the second network's error is an independent draw with
expectation zero, so there is no systematic inflation.

**The assumption doing the work is independence of the two error terms**, and it
holds only approximately here — $\theta^-$ is a delayed copy of $\theta$, so
their errors are correlated. That is why Double DQN reduces the bias
substantially rather than eliminating it, and why TD3 and SAC went further with
two independently initialised critics and a $\min$.

</Derivation>

## Two practical terms

<Steps>
<Step title="Huber loss on the residual">
TD residuals have heavy tails — a bootstrapped target can be badly wrong early,
and rewards are often spiky. Squared error turns a residual of 100 into a
gradient proportional to 100; the Huber loss is linear beyond a threshold, so
one bad target cannot dominate a batch. Reward clipping in the original paper is
the same defence applied one step earlier.
</Step>

<Step title="Exploration is bolted on, not derived">
The greedy policy explores nothing, so DQN adds $\epsilon$-greedy with an
annealed schedule. This is uninformed, uniform noise on top of an otherwise
deterministic policy — worth contrasting with maximum-entropy methods, where
stochasticity is part of the objective and is shaped by value rather than by a
coin flip.
</Step>
</Steps>
