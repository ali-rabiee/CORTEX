## Making exploration last longer than a step

<PaperCard refKey="osband2016bootstrapped" />

The insight is that the problem with $\epsilon$-greedy is not the amount of
randomness but its *incoherence*. Train an ensemble of value heads on
bootstrapped samples of the data, and each head is a plausible hypothesis about
the world. Sample one at the start of an episode and act greedily on it
throughout, and you get behaviour that is internally consistent — a plan, tested
long enough to find out whether it was right.

<KeyIdea>
"Deep exploration" means depth in *time*, not network depth. Everything in this
level is a different mechanism for making exploration persist across steps:
bonuses that propagate through value backups, sampled value functions held fixed
for an episode, or an explicit archive you return to.
</KeyIdea>

## Counting the uncountable

<PaperCard refKey="bellemare2016pseudocounts" />

The tabular theory says the bonus should scale as $1/\sqrt{N(s)}$, and $N(s)$ is
meaningless for pixels. Pseudo-counts recover it by asking a density model how
much its probability for $s$ increases after observing $s$ once more — a
quantity that behaves like a count and reduces to the true count in the tabular
case. That reduction is what earns the word "unifying" in the title.

## Curiosity, and the noisy TV

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="pathak2017icm" />
<PaperCard refKey="burda2018rnd" />
</PaperGrid>

ICM rewards forward-model prediction error, with one important refinement: the
error is measured in a feature space trained by an *inverse* model that predicts
the action from consecutive states. Features that support that prediction are
about things the agent can control, so background randomness is partially
filtered out. Partially — controllable stochasticity still produces irreducible
error.

RND attacks the other end of the problem. Make the prediction target a fixed
randomly-initialised network applied to the observation. That is a deterministic
function, so there is no aleatoric component at all, and the error is pure
epistemic uncertainty that genuinely goes to zero on states you have seen.

<Warning>
This is the pattern worth carrying away. Prediction error was measuring
"unlearnable" together with "not yet learned". ICM tries to clean the *input*;
RND changes the *target* to something guaranteed learnable. The second is a
stronger move, and it generalises — when a diagnostic conflates two quantities,
constraining the setup so only one can be present usually beats filtering after
the fact.
</Warning>

## The reframing

<PaperCard refKey="ecoffet2019goexplore" />

Go-Explore argues the field had been solving the wrong problem. The difficulty
is not deciding where to explore; it is **getting back**. *Detachment*: the
agent stops attending to a promising frontier because the bonus there is already
consumed. *Derailment*: even knowing where to go, the exploratory noise that
finds new states also destroys the long deterministic sequence needed to return
to a known one.

So separate the two. Archive the states you have reached, choose a promising one,
**return** to it deterministically, and only then explore. The decomposition
reads as obvious in retrospect and was not.

<PaperCard refKey="badia2020agent57" />

Agent57 takes the meta-level view: rather than picking one exploration setting,
run a family of policies spanning exploratory to exploitative — separate
intrinsic and extrinsic value heads with different discounts — and use a bandit
to allocate across them. The exploration-exploitation trade-off becomes a
decision the agent makes rather than a hyperparameter you set.

## Why almost none of this is on a robot

<Steps>
<Step title="The sample budget is off by orders of magnitude">
These methods are evaluated where millions of episodes are affordable. A real
arm gives you a few hundred a day.
</Step>

<Step title="Exploration on hardware is physically dangerous">
An exploring policy is a policy doing unpredictable things with a heavy machine.
The safety envelope needed to allow it usually also prevents the exploration
that mattered.
</Step>

<Step title="Demonstrations dissolve the problem">
A handful of demonstrations puts the policy directly in the region an
exploration method would have spent millions of steps finding. Given that
choice, the field took it — which is why manipulation is dominated by imitation
with RL used for local fine-tuning.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
The honest summary for a robotics interview: this literature is deep, largely
solved in simulation, and mostly bypassed in practice. Where it does resurface is
sim-to-real, where exploration is cheap and safe in the simulator, and in
residual RL on top of an imitation-pretrained policy, where the search is local
enough that an entropy bonus suffices.
</KeyIdea>
