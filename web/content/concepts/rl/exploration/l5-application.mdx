interview:
  answer: >-
    The exploration problem is that you only get a reward signal for the action
    you took, and the action you took came from the policy you currently believe
    in — so a policy that's settled on something mediocre generates data that
    confirms it's fine, because it never generates data about anything else. The
    distinction I'd want to draw is between undirected and directed exploration,
    because it isn't about how much randomness you add. Epsilon-greedy, action
    noise, and entropy bonuses all re-draw the randomness independently at every
    step, so they produce a random walk. If the reward is N correct choices away
    and every wrong choice resets you, the probability of stumbling onto it is
    one over K to the N — and crucially, raising epsilon makes that worse, not
    better, because each step becomes more random and you need N in a row. So
    it's not a badly tuned estimator, it's the wrong shape. What breaks the
    exponential is exploration that persists across time. Two mechanisms. One is
    an uncertainty bonus put into the reward, so value backups carry it
    backwards — states that lead toward unexplored regions become valuable and
    the agent walks there deliberately. The other is posterior sampling: sample
    one plausible value function and act greedily on it for a whole episode, the
    way Bootstrapped DQN does with an ensemble. A sampled value function is a
    coherent hypothesis, so following it produces a plan rather than a wobble.
    On intrinsic motivation specifically, the thing worth knowing is the noisy
    TV. If you reward forward-model prediction error, a genuinely stochastic
    transition is unpredictable forever, so it pays out forever and the agent
    parks in front of it. That's conflating epistemic uncertainty with aleatoric
    noise. Random Network Distillation fixes it by making the prediction target
    a fixed randomly-initialised network — deterministic by construction, so
    there's no irreducible error and the bonus really does go to zero on states
    you've seen. And the practical part: on a real robot, almost none of this is
    what you'd do. The sample budgets are off by orders of magnitude, an
    exploring policy is a heavy machine doing unpredictable things, and a
    handful of demonstrations puts you directly in the region an exploration
    method would spend millions of steps finding. So in manipulation the field
    bypasses the problem rather than solving it.
  followUps:
    - q: "Where does exploration research still matter for robotics, then?"
      a: >-
        Two places. Sim-to-real, because in the simulator exploration is free
        and safe — you can afford millions of episodes and there's nothing to
        break — so the full machinery is available there and only the transfer
        problem is left. And residual or fine-tuning RL on top of an
        imitation-pretrained policy, where you're starting from something
        competent and the search is genuinely local, so an entropy bonus is
        adequate and the deep-exploration machinery would be overkill. The
        third honest answer is that a lot of what looks like an exploration
        problem in robotics is really a reward-specification problem — if the
        reward is so sparse that you need Go-Explore, it's usually worth asking
        whether a shaped reward or a goal-conditioned formulation with hindsight
        relabelling would dissolve it.
    - q: "How would you know an exploration bonus is helping rather than just adding noise?"
      a: >-
        Not from the return curve, because on a hard-exploration task that's
        flat at zero both while you're failing and while you're nearly
        succeeding — it carries no information for me as the engineer. I'd track
        coverage instead: a discretised count of distinct states touched, or
        better, task-specific milestones, how far into the task the agent has
        ever got. Those move continuously and tell me whether to keep going. And
        I'd specifically not use the magnitude of the intrinsic reward as the
        metric, because it can be large simply because the predictor is
        undertrained, which says nothing about whether the agent is reaching new
        places.
    - q: "Doesn't SAC's entropy term solve exploration?"
      a: >-
        It solves a real part of it and not the hard part. The entropy term
        makes the optimal policy genuinely stochastic in a value-dependent way,
        so it keeps mass on every action that's nearly as good — that's better
        than epsilon-greedy, because the randomness is shaped rather than
        uniform, and it's never annealed away so the policy stays robust. But
        it's still local. It explores the neighbourhood of the current
        behaviour, and if the reward requires a long specific sequence that the
        current policy has essentially zero probability of producing, no
        temperature setting finds it. I'd describe max-entropy exploration as
        the right default and as categorically insufficient for a
        combination-lock problem.
    - q: "What's the argument for optimism in the face of uncertainty?"
      a: >-
        It's a case analysis, not wishful thinking. If you act greedily on an
        upper confidence bound, then either the optimistic estimate was right
        and you collect the reward, or it was wrong and you've just learned
        that, which shrinks the bonus. Every optimistic action either pays or
        informs, and that's exactly what bounds the regret. The bound itself
        comes straight out of Hoeffding — the confidence width is order square
        root of log one over delta over N, and the log-t in UCB1 appears because
        you need the bound to hold at every round simultaneously. The nice
        contrast is with offline RL, where the same optimism is a bug: there
        the inflated values come from noise in the max over actions you can't
        try, so they can't be corrected, and the whole field is about
        suppressing them.

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Return flat at zero, never moves → is it exploration or reward?">
Before adding machinery, check whether the reward is ever achievable by the
current policy at all. If a scripted or demonstrated trajectory gets reward and
the policy never does, it is exploration. If nothing gets reward, it is a reward
bug.
</Step>

<Step title="Agent fixates on one part of the scene → the noisy TV">
Prediction-error curiosity has found irreducible stochasticity. Move to a
deterministic target (RND) or to controllable features (ICM-style inverse
model). Annealing the coefficient hides it rather than fixing it.
</Step>

<Step title="Intrinsic reward large but coverage flat → the bonus is undertrained, not informative">
A big bonus can simply mean the predictor has not caught up. Track state
coverage or milestones; the bonus magnitude is not a progress metric.
</Step>

<Step title="Explores early, then collapses → the bonus decayed and nothing replaced it">
Expected, and worth planning for. Either anneal $\beta$ deliberately so the
final policy optimises the true reward, or keep separate value heads so the
deployed objective is unambiguous.
</Step>

<Step title="Behaves erratically near hazards on hardware → stop">
This is where the simulator results stop transferring. Exploration on a real
robot needs a safety envelope, and the envelope usually removes the exploration
that mattered. Demonstrations are the answer, and it is not a compromise.
</Step>
</Steps>
