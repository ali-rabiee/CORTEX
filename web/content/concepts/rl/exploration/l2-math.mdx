## The bandit case, where it can be solved

Strip out the state entirely: $K$ actions, each with an unknown mean reward
$Q_a$. Play $T$ rounds, minimise **regret** against always playing the best arm:

$$
\mathcal{R}_T = T\max_a Q_a - \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t\right]
$$

This turns "how much should I explore?" from a hyperparameter into a
quantitative question with an answer.

<Derivation title="UCB from Hoeffding's inequality">

**Step 1 — bound the estimation error.** Let $\hat Q_a$ be the sample mean of
$N_a$ pulls of arm $a$, with rewards in $[0,1]$. Hoeffding's inequality gives

$$
\Pr\big(|\hat Q_a - Q_a| \ge u\big) \;\le\; 2\exp(-2N_a u^2)
$$

**Step 2 — invert it at a chosen confidence.** Set the right-hand side to
$\delta$ and solve:

$$
u = \sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2N_a}}
$$

So with probability $1-\delta$, the true mean is below $\hat Q_a + u$. The
bonus shrinks like $1/\sqrt{N_a}$ — uncertainty falls as you sample.

**Step 3 — make it hold at every round.** You need the bound simultaneously
across all rounds, so $\delta$ must shrink with $t$ fast enough for a union
bound to converge. Taking $\delta \sim t^{-4}$ gives $\log(1/\delta) \propto \log t$
and the familiar

$$
a_t = \arg\max_a \left[\hat Q_a + \sqrt{\frac{2\log t}{N_a}}\right]
$$

**Why this is not wishful thinking.** Act greedily on the upper confidence
bound. Either the arm really is that good — you collect the reward — or it is
not, and you learn that, shrinking its bonus. Every optimistic pull either pays
or informs, which is exactly the case analysis that bounds the regret at
$O(\log T)$.

**The assumptions doing the work** are bounded rewards (Hoeffding needs them)
and independence across pulls. Neither survives contact with an MDP.

</Derivation>

## Why MDPs are different

In a bandit, exploring an action is a single decision you can make right now. In
an MDP, the thing you are uncertain about may be twenty steps away, behind a
sequence of specific actions.

<Derivation title="Undirected exploration is exponentially slow on a chain">

Consider $N$ states in a chain. At each, exactly one of $K$ actions advances;
the other $K-1$ return you to $s_0$. The only reward is at $s_N$.

Under a uniform random policy, the probability of choosing correctly at any
state is $1/K$, and reaching $s_N$ requires $N$ correct choices with no mistake:

$$
\Pr(\text{reach } s_N \text{ in one attempt}) = \left(\frac{1}{K}\right)^{N}
$$

so the expected number of attempts is $K^N$. With $K = 4$ and $N = 20$ that is
about $10^{12}$ episodes.

**The critical observation is the direction of the $\epsilon$ dependence.** With
$\epsilon$-greedy over a policy that has learned nothing, raising $\epsilon$
raises the randomness per step and makes an $N$-step correct sequence *less*
likely. There is no schedule that fixes this — the estimator is the wrong shape,
not badly tuned.

**What breaks the exponential.** Give unvisited states a bonus and put it in the
reward. Value backups then assign $s_{N-1}$ part of $s_N$'s bonus, $s_{N-2}$
part of $s_{N-1}$'s, and so on. The agent now has a gradient pointing down the
chain and walks it deliberately. This is "deep exploration": the requirement is
not that exploration be random, but that it *propagate*.

</Derivation>

## Making counts work in continuous spaces

$N(s)$ is well defined for a gridworld and meaningless for camera images, where
no state is ever visited twice.

<Steps>
<Step title="Pseudo-counts from a density model">
Fit a density model $\rho(s)$ over observations and define a pseudo-count from
how much $\rho$ increases after one more observation of $s$. This recovers the
$1/\sqrt{N}$ bonus shape and reduces to true counts in the tabular case, which
is the sense in which it 'unifies' counts with intrinsic motivation.
</Step>

<Step title="Prediction error, and its trap">
Reward the agent where a learned forward model $\hat f(s,a) \approx s'$ predicts
badly. Simple, and it conflates two things:
$\text{error} = \underbrace{\text{epistemic}}_{\text{shrinks with data}} + \underbrace{\text{aleatoric}}_{\text{never shrinks}}$.
A genuinely stochastic transition pays out forever — the noisy TV.
</Step>

<Step title="Random Network Distillation">
Fix a randomly initialised network $f$ and train a predictor $\hat f_\theta$ to
match it on visited observations, with intrinsic reward
$r^{\text{int}}(s) = \|\hat f_\theta(s) - f(s)\|^2$. Since $f$ is a
*deterministic* function of the observation, there is no aleatoric term at all —
the error is pure epistemic uncertainty and genuinely goes to zero on states you
have seen. Novelty detection reduced to a regression problem.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
RND's trick is worth understanding as a general move. The problem was that
prediction error measured "unlearnable" as well as "not yet learned". The fix
was to choose a prediction target that is *guaranteed learnable* — a fixed
deterministic function — so the only remaining source of error is the one you
wanted to measure.
</KeyIdea>

## Posterior sampling

The alternative to a bonus is to be uncertain about the value function itself.
Maintain an approximate posterior, sample one $Q$ from it, and act greedily on
that sample for an entire episode.

$$
Q^{(k)} \sim p(Q \mid \mathcal{D}),
\qquad
a_t = \arg\max_a Q^{(k)}(s_t, a) \quad \text{for the whole episode}
$$

Bootstrapped DQN approximates the posterior with an ensemble of heads trained on
bootstrapped data, picking one head per episode.

<Warning>
Note what holding the sample fixed accomplishes. A sampled $Q$ is a coherent
hypothesis about the world, so acting greedily on it produces a *plan* — a
sequence of actions that make sense together. Re-sampling every step would
average the heads and collapse back to something like $\epsilon$-greedy. Both
directed approaches — bonus-plus-backups and posterior sampling — are answers to
the same requirement, that exploration extend across time rather than being
redrawn at each step.
</Warning>

## Intrinsic reward is not free

Adding $r^{\text{int}}$ changes the MDP you are solving, which has two
consequences. The bonus is **non-stationary** — it decays as states become
familiar — so the value function is chasing a target that moves for reasons
unrelated to policy improvement. And the optimal policy for $r + \beta r^{\text{int}}$
is not the optimal policy for $r$, so $\beta$ must eventually be annealed or the
two streams kept separate with different discounts, which is what the
Agent57-style architectures do.
