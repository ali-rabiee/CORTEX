## Learning without touching anything

Offline RL is reinforcement learning from a fixed dataset. No rollouts, no
exploration, no ability to test a hypothesis. You are handed logged transitions
$(s, a, r, s')$ — from human teleoperation, from an old controller, from
failures — and asked for the best policy those transitions support.

The appeal for robotics is obvious. Online RL on real hardware means an
untrained policy exploring with a real arm, which is slow, dangerous, and
usually not allowed. Offline RL promises to use the data you already have.

<Intuition>
Learning to drive from a hard drive of dashcam footage. You can watch anything
in it as many times as you like. You cannot try turning left at a junction where
nobody turned left, and nothing in the archive can tell you what would have
happened.

The dangerous part is not that you do not know. It is that a value function
asked to guess will guess *something*, and the algorithm's next move is to pick
whichever guess was highest.
</Intuition>

## The one thing that goes wrong

It is tempting to say offline RL is hard "because of distribution shift". That
is too vague to act on. The problem is specific and it lives in one operator.

The Bellman backup contains $\max_{a'} Q(s', a')$. That maximisation ranges over
*all* actions, including ones the dataset never contains. On those the critic is
extrapolating, its error is unbounded, and the max deliberately selects
whichever extrapolation came out highest.

<Figure src="/figures/offline-rl-ood-optimism.svg"
        alt="A Q function over the action axis. Inside the shaded region covered by dataset actions, the learned Q tracks the true Q. Outside it, the learned Q has a large spurious peak, and the max in the backup selects that peak."
        caption="The max is not a neutral operator here — it is a search for the network's most optimistic extrapolation. Online, taking that action produces a real reward and the error corrects itself. Offline, there is nothing to correct it." />

<KeyIdea>
The difference between offline and online off-policy RL is **refutation**.
Online, an overestimated action gets chosen, tried, and disproved; the error is
self-limiting. Offline, the feedback loop is cut, and bootstrapping carries the
inflated value backwards into every state that can reach it.
</KeyIdea>

That is why running SAC unmodified on a frozen buffer does not work. It is not
a tuning failure — it is the algorithm doing exactly what it was designed to do
in a setting where one of its assumptions has been removed.

## Then why not just clone?

Behaviour cloning has none of these problems. It never queries an unsupported
action, so there is nothing to extrapolate and nothing to inflate. It is the
baseline, and on near-expert data it is a strong one that frequently wins.

Offline RL earns its place in three situations.

<Steps>
<Step title="The data is mixed quality">
Cloning imitates the average of what it is shown, including the bad parts.
Offline RL has a reward signal and can prefer the good parts.
</Step>

<Step title="The data contains failures">
A collision, a dropped object, a failed grasp is *informative* — it tells you
what not to do. Cloning either discards it or imitates it. A value function can
use it.
</Step>

<Step title="No single trajectory is good, but a path through them is">
This is the important one, and it is called **stitching**. If trajectory A gets
efficiently to a midpoint then wanders, and trajectory B starts badly but
finishes well from near that midpoint, dynamic programming can combine them into
a policy better than anything in the dataset. Value backups propagate across
trajectory boundaries; cloning and return-conditioned sequence models cannot
cross them.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The three method families all attack the same $\max$, in different places.
**Policy constraint** keeps the learned policy close to the behaviour policy so
it cannot ask about unsupported actions. **Value pessimism** (CQL) explicitly
pushes down $Q$ on actions the data does not support. **In-sample** methods
(IQL) never evaluate an out-of-distribution action at all, learning an upper
expectile over dataset actions instead. Knowing which knob a method turns is
more useful than memorising its acronym — and every one of them has the same
tuning hazard: too much conservatism and you have reinvented behaviour cloning,
too little and the optimism leaks back in.
</Warning>
