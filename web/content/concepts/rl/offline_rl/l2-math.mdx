## The setting

You have $\mathcal{D} = \{(s_i, a_i, r_i, s_i')\}$ generated by some behaviour
policy $\pi_\beta$ (often a mixture, often unknown). No further interaction is
permitted. You want $\pi$ maximising expected return.

The trouble is a mismatch between two things:

$$
\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{TD}} = \mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s')\sim\mathcal{D}}
\big[(Q(s,a) - y)^2\big]}_{\text{measured only on }\operatorname{supp}(\pi_\beta)}
\qquad
\underbrace{y = r + \gamma\max_{a'} Q(s',a')}_{\text{evaluated over all }a'}
$$

The loss constrains $Q$ where the data is. The target queries $Q$ everywhere.

<Derivation title="Why the max is systematically optimistic, and where">

Write the learned critic as truth plus error, $\hat Q(s,a) = Q(s,a) + \varepsilon(s,a)$.

**Direction.** Taking a max over noisy estimates is upward biased. If
$\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0$ per action, convexity of $\max$ gives

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[\max_a \hat Q(s,a)\big] \;\ge\; \max_a Q(s,a)
$$

This much also happens in tabular Q-learning, and double estimators address it.

**Location.** The TD loss is an expectation over $\mathcal{D}$, so it penalises
$\varepsilon$ only where $\pi_\beta$ has mass. Off the support, nothing in the
objective constrains $\varepsilon$ at all — its magnitude is set by the
network's inductive bias. So $\varepsilon$ is small in-distribution and
arbitrarily large out of it, and $\arg\max_a \hat Q$ preferentially lands in the
unconstrained region.

**Compounding.** This is the offline-specific part. That inflated
$\max_{a'}\hat Q(s',a')$ becomes the target for $Q(s,a)$ at every predecessor
state, so the error propagates backwards and grows. Online, the policy would
select the offending action, observe a real reward, and the value would be
corrected. **The assumption that fails offline is not Markov, not function
approximation — it is that the agent can test its own beliefs.**

</Derivation>

## Family 1: constrain the policy

Do not let $\pi$ ask about actions $\pi_\beta$ would not take:

$$
\max_\pi\; \mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{D},\,a\sim\pi}\big[Q(s,a)\big]
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
D\big(\pi(\cdot\mid s),\, \pi_\beta(\cdot\mid s)\big) \le \epsilon
$$

TD3+BC is the minimal version: add a behaviour-cloning term to the actor loss,
$\mathcal{L} = -\lambda\mathbb{E}[Q(s,\pi(s))] + \mathbb{E}[(\pi(s) - a)^2]$,
with $\lambda$ normalised by the mean $|Q|$ so the two terms stay comparable
across reward scales.

**The assumption doing the work:** that $\pi_\beta$ is good enough to be worth
staying near. With a poor or highly multi-modal behaviour policy, the constraint
holds you close to something you did not want.

## Family 2: make the value pessimistic

CQL adds a term that pushes $Q$ down on actions sampled from a proposal $\mu$
and holds it up on actions from the data:

$$
\min_Q\;\; \alpha\Big(\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{D},\,a\sim\mu}\big[Q(s,a)\big]
- \mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[Q(s,a)\big]\Big)
\;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\big[(Q - \hat{\mathcal{B}}Q^k)^2\big]
$$

<Derivation title="CQL's fixed point: penalty proportional to the density ratio">

Work pointwise. Let $d(s)$ be the state distribution of $\mathcal{D}$; then the
first term contributes $\alpha\, d(s)\mu(a\mid s)$ to the coefficient of
$Q(s,a)$, the second contributes $-\alpha\, d(s)\pi_\beta(a\mid s)$, and the TD
term contributes $d(s)\pi_\beta(a\mid s)\,(Q(s,a) - \hat{\mathcal{B}}Q^k(s,a))$.

Set the derivative with respect to $Q(s,a)$ to zero:

$$
\alpha\,d(s)\mu(a\mid s) - \alpha\,d(s)\pi_\beta(a\mid s)
+ d(s)\pi_\beta(a\mid s)\big(Q(s,a) - \hat{\mathcal{B}}Q^k(s,a)\big) = 0
$$

Divide through by $d(s)\pi_\beta(a\mid s)$ and rearrange:

$$
\boxed{\;Q^{k+1}(s,a) = \hat{\mathcal{B}}Q^k(s,a)
\;-\; \alpha\left(\frac{\mu(a\mid s)}{\pi_\beta(a\mid s)} - 1\right)\;}
$$

**Read what this says.** The correction is the density ratio minus one. Where
$\mu$ proposes an action about as often as the data contains it, the ratio is
near 1 and the penalty vanishes. Where $\mu$ proposes an action the data rarely
or never contains, the ratio blows up and the value is driven far below its
Bellman target. The conservatism is automatically targeted at exactly the
actions that would otherwise be exploited.

**The assumption doing the work is that $\alpha$ is right.** Too large and every
value is suppressed until the greedy policy is just $\pi_\beta$ — behaviour
cloning with extra steps. Too small and the optimism leaks back through.

</Derivation>

In practice $\mu$ is taken to be the soft-max over actions, which makes the
first expectation a $\log\sum_a \exp Q(s,a)$ — a differentiable soft maximum,
estimated by sampling in continuous action spaces.

## Family 3: never leave the data

IQL removes the offending operator instead of penalising its consequences.
Learn $V$ by **expectile regression** on $Q$, using only dataset actions:

$$
\mathcal{L}_V = \mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[
L_2^\tau\big(Q_{\bar\theta}(s,a) - V_\psi(s)\big)\Big],
\qquad
L_2^\tau(u) = \big|\tau - \mathbb{1}(u < 0)\big|\,u^2
$$

With $\tau = 0.5$ this is ordinary least squares and $V$ learns the mean. As
$\tau \to 1$, under-predictions are penalised far more than over-predictions, so
$V$ is pulled toward the **upper** end of the $Q$ values observed at that state
— an approximation to $\max_a Q(s,a)$ taken *over the support of the data only*.

The critic then bootstraps through $V$ rather than through a max:
$\mathcal{L}_Q = \mathbb{E}[(r + \gamma V_\psi(s') - Q_\theta(s,a))^2]$, and no
unsupported action is ever evaluated anywhere in the algorithm.

Policy extraction is advantage-weighted regression:

$$
\mathcal{L}_\pi = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\Big[\exp\!\big(\beta\,(Q(s,a) - V(s))\big)\,\log\pi_\phi(a\mid s)\Big]
$$

which is behaviour cloning weighted toward the better-than-average actions
actually present in the data.

<KeyIdea>
Three answers to one question. **Constrain the policy** so it cannot ask.
**Penalise the value** so asking is unrewarding. **Change the operator** so the
question is never posed. The first two have a conservatism coefficient to tune
and can in principle exceed the data's support; the third has no such
coefficient and structurally cannot. That is the trade, and it is the right way
to place any new offline method you meet.
</KeyIdea>

<Warning>
All three inherit a problem none of them solves: **offline evaluation**. You
cannot measure a policy's return without running it, and off-policy evaluation
is itself an open problem with the same support issues. So hyperparameters —
including the conservatism coefficient that determines whether the method works
at all — are usually chosen with online rollouts, which quietly reintroduces
the interaction the setting was defined to exclude. Being able to say this out
loud is a genuine signal in an interview.
</Warning>
