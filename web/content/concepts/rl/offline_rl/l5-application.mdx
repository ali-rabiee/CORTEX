interview:
  answer: >-
    Offline RL is learning a policy from a fixed dataset with no environment
    interaction. The reason it's a distinct problem rather than just off-policy
    RL with a frozen buffer is worth being precise about, because "distribution
    shift" is too vague to act on. The Bellman backup contains a max over
    actions, and that max ranges over everything, including actions the dataset
    never contains. On those the critic is extrapolating, its error is
    unconstrained because the TD loss is only computed on dataset actions, and
    the max deliberately selects whichever extrapolation came out highest.
    Online that's self-limiting: the policy picks the overestimated action,
    tries it, gets a real reward, and the value is corrected. Offline that
    feedback loop is cut, so the inflated value gets bootstrapped backwards into
    every state that can reach it and compounds. That's why running SAC
    unmodified on a fixed dataset gives you a critic with absurd values and a
    policy that's fled the data. Everything in the field is an answer to that
    one operator, and there are three families. Policy constraint — keep the
    learned policy close to the behaviour policy so it can't ask about
    unsupported actions; TD3-plus-BC is the minimal version. Value pessimism —
    CQL adds a term that pushes Q down on sampled actions and holds it up on
    dataset actions, and if you work out the fixed point the penalty is exactly
    the density ratio of the proposal to the behaviour policy, minus one, so
    it's automatically targeted: near zero in-distribution and unbounded out of
    it. And in-sample methods — IQL learns V by expectile regression over
    dataset actions only, which approximates a max over the support of the
    data, so no unsupported action is ever evaluated anywhere in the algorithm.
    The first two have a conservatism coefficient to tune and can in principle
    reach outside the data; the third has no coefficient and structurally
    can't. Practically, I'd want to be honest that behaviour cloning is the
    baseline and it often wins on near-expert data. Offline RL earns its place
    when the data is mixed quality, when there are informative failures a
    reward can use and cloning would just imitate, and when stitching matters —
    combining the good halves of two mediocre trajectories into a path better
    than anything observed.
  followUps:
    - q: "How do you pick the conservatism coefficient without online rollouts?"
      a: >-
        That's the honest weak point of the whole field. Off-policy evaluation
        has the same support problem the algorithm does — you can't evaluate a
        policy on actions the data doesn't cover — so there's no clean answer.
        In practice most published results tune alpha with online rollouts,
        which quietly reintroduces the interaction the setting was supposed to
        exclude, and I'd want to say that rather than pretend otherwise. What I
        can do without interaction is watch diagnostics that indicate the
        failure rather than measure the outcome: Q values drifting far above
        anything achievable given the reward scale and gamma, and the learned
        policy's actions drifting away from the dataset's action distribution.
        Those catch under-conservatism. The over-conservative side is harder,
        because a policy that's collapsed onto the behaviour policy looks
        perfectly healthy. That asymmetry is part of why in-sample methods with
        no coefficient became popular.
    - q: "Give me a concrete case where stitching is the whole argument."
      a: >-
        Navigation datasets are the canonical one — the AntMaze benchmarks are
        built specifically to isolate it. You have trajectories that each go
        from some start to some goal, none of which goes from your start to your
        goal, but collectively they cover the space. Dynamic programming
        propagates value backwards across trajectory boundaries, so it finds
        the composite path. Behaviour cloning can't, because it only ever
        reproduces behaviours it saw, and return-conditioned sequence modelling
        can't either — conditioning on a high return it's never observed just
        gives you extrapolation. That split is the sharpest empirical evidence
        that Bellman backups buy something real over sequence modelling, and
        it's the example I'd give if someone claims RL is just supervised
        learning in disguise.
    - q: "Where does offline RL sit in robot learning right now?"
      a: >-
        Less as a standalone recipe than as components. The dominant
        manipulation pipeline is imitation on demonstration corpora — action
        chunking, diffusion policies, VLAs — because demonstrations are easier
        to get than a reward function anyone trusts. Offline RL ideas come back
        in two places. One is using failure data, which a cloning pipeline
        throws away and a value function can learn from; Q-Transformer is the
        clearest version of that, doing autoregressive Q-learning over
        discretised action tokens so it scales like the imitation models do.
        The other is offline-to-online: pretrain a policy and critic offline,
        then fine-tune with a modest amount of real interaction. The known
        hazard there is the initial crash — a conservative critic is
        miscalibrated the moment real data arrives — which is what Cal-QL is
        specifically about.
    - q: "Isn't the OOD problem just the deadly triad again?"
      a: >-
        They're related but I'd keep them distinct. The triad is about the
        projected Bellman operator failing to contract, and it can hit you
        online too. The offline problem is narrower and sharper: it's
        specifically that the max queries actions where the loss provides no
        constraint, and that the agent has no way to test and correct the
        resulting belief. You could remove function approximation entirely — go
        tabular with a dataset that doesn't cover every state-action pair — and
        you'd still have the offline problem, because the max over
        never-observed actions is still unconstrained. The lack of refutation
        is the offline-specific ingredient, and that's the thing to name.

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Q-values above what the reward scale allows → under-conservatism">
Compare against $R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$. Exceeding it means the critic is
extrapolating, not estimating. Confirm by checking whether the policy's actions
have drifted from the dataset's action distribution — the two travel together.
</Step>

<Step title="Policy indistinguishable from the behaviour policy → over-conservatism">
The harder failure, because nothing looks wrong. Values are sane, training is
stable, and you have reinvented behaviour cloning at ten times the cost. Compare
against an actual BC baseline; if they match, your conservatism coefficient is
doing all the work.
</Step>

<Step title="Good on expert data, no better than BC → wrong tool for this dataset">
Expected, not a bug. Offline RL's advantage lives in mixed data, informative
failures, and stitching. If your data is uniformly near-expert, the honest
recommendation is BC.
</Step>

<Step title="Offline metrics look fine, real performance is poor → offline evaluation">
There is no reliable way to rank policies without running them. Treat every
offline number as a diagnostic, never as an evaluation, and say so.
</Step>

<Step title="Performance crashes at the start of online fine-tuning → calibration">
A conservative critic is systematically pessimistic, and the moment real
on-policy data arrives its values are wrong in a way that destabilises the
actor. This is the known offline-to-online failure, and it is what Cal-QL
addresses.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The sentence that carries this answer is the one about refutation. "Distribution
shift" is what most candidates say and it does not distinguish offline RL from
anything else. The precise claim — the max queries actions where the TD loss
provides no constraint, and the agent cannot try them and be corrected — is what
makes the three method families obviously necessary rather than a list of
acronyms. Follow it by volunteering that behaviour cloning is a strong baseline
and that hyperparameter selection usually cheats, and you sound like someone who
has run these rather than read them.
</Warning>
