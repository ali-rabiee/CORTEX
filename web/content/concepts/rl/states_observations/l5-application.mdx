  prompt: "Give your interview answer on observation design. What is the counterintuitive claim, and what test would you run?"
  keyPoints:
    - "Lead with the design framing: the state is given, the observation is chosen, and the choice determines which failures are possible."
    - "The counterintuitive claim: adding a channel can make the policy worse, with a better training loss."

interview:
  answer: >-
    The distinction people usually give is that the state is the true
    configuration of the world and the observation is what your sensors report,
    and that's right but it's not the useful part. The useful part is that you
    don't choose the state — it's a property of the physics — and you always
    choose the observation. So the observation is a design decision, and it
    determines which failure modes are even available to you. The one I'd lead
    with is the counterintuitive one: adding a channel can make the policy
    worse. Not slower to train — worse, and with a lower training loss. The
    canonical case is putting the previous action into the observation. If
    you're cloning a teleoperator at a hundred hertz, their commands are smooth,
    so the previous action explains something like ninety-four percent of the
    variance in the current one. The learner has a choice between extracting the
    goal from a noisy image and copying the last action, and it takes the
    second, because least squares minimises residual variance and doesn't care
    which channel is causal. Then you deploy and that channel changes source.
    During training it was the expert's action, a real signal about the task.
    During rollout it's whatever the policy just emitted, so the policy is
    regressing its own output onto itself with only a weak connection to the
    world. What makes this expensive rather than merely annoying is that it's
    invisible offline. In a linear version I worked through, sweeping the vision
    noise across three orders of magnitude moves training R-squared from 0.999
    to 0.941 while closed-loop error goes from half a millimetre to thirty-seven
    centimetres. The metric is essentially constant across the entire range from
    working to useless. And the failure isn't instability — the effective
    feedback gain collapses to about a sixteenth of the expert's, so the robot
    still converges to the goal, just far too slowly to finish the episode. It
    looks sluggish, which is why people spend weeks on the controller instead.
    So the test I'd insist on is a channel ablation scored on rollout success
    rather than on loss, because loss cannot answer this question in principle.
  followUps:
    - q: "Suppose the ablation shows removing the camera doesn't change success. What now?"
      a: >-
        Then the policy isn't using vision to do the task, and for anything
        that's actually manipulation that's a red flag rather than a saving. It
        means something else in the observation predicts the expert — usually
        proprioception correlating with the phase of the demonstration, since if
        every demo starts from the same reset, joint configuration alone tells
        you how far through the episode you are and roughly what comes next.
        That policy works perfectly on the demonstrated setup and fails the
        moment an object moves. The fix is on the data side: randomise initial
        object placement so that phase stops being predictive, which breaks the
        correlation at the source rather than trying to train around it.
    - q: "How do you decide what should go into the observation?"
      a: >-
        I'd ask of each channel whether it's causally connected to why the expert
        acted, not whether it could conceivably help — the causal confusion
        result is that "could help" isn't a sufficient bar. Then I'd start
        minimal and add channels one at a time with an ablation each time, which
        is slow but it's the only way to know what the policy is actually using.
        And I'd deliberately exclude anything that's a consequence of the action
        rather than a cause of it. Previous actions, and any status signal that
        lights up because the operator did something, are the two that catch
        people.
    - q: "Is there ever a case for putting the previous action in?"
      a: >-
        Yes, and it's worth being precise about it, because the blanket rule is
        wrong. If the true dynamics depend on it — actuator lag, a filtered
        command, anything where the plant's response depends on its recent input
        history — then the previous action is genuinely part of the state and
        omitting it makes the problem partially observed. The distinction is
        whether it's there because the physics needs it or because it correlates
        with the expert. Practically I'd only include it when I can name the
        physical mechanism, and I'd still run the ablation, since the failure
        looks identical either way from the loss curve.
    - q: "You mentioned control rate. Does running faster help?"
      a: >-
        It makes two things worse, which surprises people. The shortcut gets
        stronger, because consecutive actions become more similar as the period
        shrinks — the previous action explains about half the variance at ten
        hertz and ninety-seven percent at two hundred. And finite-difference
        velocity noise scales as one over delta-t, so the same one-millimetre
        encoder gives four centimetres per second of velocity noise at thirty
        hertz and twenty-eight at two hundred. The fix for both is a longer
        temporal window rather than a shorter one — a least-squares slope over
        several frames for velocity, action chunking on the output side. Which
        is a nice consistency: the answer to both problems is to stop treating
        adjacent timesteps as independent.
  traps:

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Robot is sluggish but eventually gets there → effective gain collapse">
Characteristic of a shortcut absorbing the feedback path. Check whether any input
channel is a function of a previous action.
</Step>

<Step title="Excellent loss, poor rollouts, honest split → the loss cannot see it">
Distribution shift or a shortcut. An ablation separates them; no offline metric
will.
</Step>

<Step title="Works on the demonstrated layout, fails when anything moves → phase shortcut">
Proprioception is encoding progress through the episode. Fix at collection by
randomising resets, not in training.
</Step>

<Step title="Behaviour changes on a different machine → time skew">
Channel latencies shifted. Timestamp at capture and interpolate to one clock.
</Step>

<Step title="One channel appears ignored → check its scale before its content">
An unnormalised small-magnitude channel is invisible at initialisation, which is
indistinguishable from being uninformative until you look.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The move that separates a strong answer here is refusing the textbook framing.
Everyone can define state versus observation. What demonstrates experience is
stating that adding a channel can make the policy worse, naming the mechanism —
the channel changes source between training and rollout — and then saying that
this is why you score ablations on rollouts rather than on loss. If you can add
that the failure presents as sluggishness rather than as instability, you have
described something people only learn by losing time to it.
</Warning>
