## The counterintuitive result

<PaperCard refKey="dehaan2019causal" />

The finding worth remembering is directional: **more information, worse policy**.
Not slower, not marginally worse — worse, with a better fit. The mechanism is
that a learner minimising prediction error has no preference for causes over
correlates, and in demonstration data the correlates are often the easier fit.

Their sharpest instance is the one this concept opened with: showing the policy
the expert's previous action, or a dashboard indicator that lights up *because*
the expert braked. The indicator predicts braking beautifully and is a
consequence of it, so a policy relying on it never brakes.

<PaperCard refKey="song2020obsoverfit" />

The same phenomenon in RL, with a useful addition: which irrelevant dimensions an
agent latches onto is determined by the architecture's implicit bias, not only by
the data. That makes it partly an architectural question and explains why two
networks on identical observations can generalise differently.

## The other half: information you have but cannot deploy

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="pinto2018asymmetric" />
<PaperCard refKey="lee2020quadruped" />
</PaperGrid>

In simulation you have the full state, and the robot never will. Two ways to
spend that gap:

<Steps>
<Step title="Give it to the critic only">
The critic exists during training and is thrown away. Feeding it privileged state
makes value estimation strictly easier and lowers advantage variance, while the
actor's input set stays whatever the hardware provides. The asymmetry is free
because the two networks have different lifetimes.
</Step>

<Step title="Train a teacher, then distil">
Train a policy on privileged state, then supervise a student that sees only what
the robot sees. The teacher's problem is easy; the student's is a supervised
mapping rather than an RL problem, which is a much better-conditioned thing to
optimise.
</Step>

<Step title="Check the information is recoverable first">
Distillation moves a mapping, not information. The quadruped student can recover
terrain properties because contact response encodes them in proprioceptive
history. Where the privileged variable leaves no trace in the observation
history, the student's best achievable output is a posterior mean — and for a
multi-modal posterior that is an action nobody would take.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
Put together, the four papers say the two directions are not symmetric. Adding a
channel is dangerous, because the learner may prefer it for reasons unrelated to
the task. Withholding one is safe but bounded, because you cannot distil what the
sensors never recorded. The design question is therefore not "how much can I
provide" but "which channels are causally connected to the action, and is the
rest inferable from what remains".
</KeyIdea>
