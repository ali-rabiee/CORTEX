## The one you choose

<Intuition>
The state is whatever determines the future — the physics. You do not choose it;
it is a property of the world. The observation is what your sensors report and
what you decide to pass to the policy, and that is entirely a design decision.
Most discussion of this concept is about the gap between the two. The more
useful framing for building systems is that the observation is a *choice with
consequences*, and the consequences are strange enough to be worth knowing in
advance.
</Intuition>

The strangest one: **adding a channel can make the policy worse**. Not slower to
train, not marginally worse — categorically worse, with a lower training loss.
That fact is the reason this concept earns its own page.

## Why more information can hurt

Suppose you are cloning a teleoperator at 100 Hz. Their commands are smooth, so
the action at one step is close to the action at the previous step. Concretely,
for a proportional controller at that rate, the previous action alone explains
about 94% of the variance in the current one.

Now put that previous action into the observation. The learner has two ways to
predict: extract the goal from a noisy image, or copy the last action. The second
is easier and almost as accurate, so that is what it learns.

<Figure src="/figures/observation-shortcut.svg" alt="Left: during training the previous-action channel comes from the expert; during rollout it comes from the policy, forming a self-loop. Right: as the vision channel gets noisier, training fit stays flat while closed-loop error rises three orders of magnitude." caption="The two panels are the whole concept. The channel changes source between training and rollout, and no offline metric can see it happen." />

Then you deploy, and the channel changes meaning. During training `a(t-1)` was
the expert's action — a real signal about the task. During rollout it is the
policy's own last output. The policy is now regressing its own behaviour onto
itself, with only a weakly-used vision channel connecting it to the world.

<KeyIdea>
In a linear version of exactly this setup, sweeping the vision noise from 0.5 cm
to 1 m moves the training $R^2$ from 0.999 to 0.941 — a rounding error — while
closed-loop final error goes from 0.05 cm to 37 cm. Roughly a 700-fold
degradation, under a training metric that barely moved. The offline number is not
merely a weak proxy here; it is nearly constant across the entire range from
working to useless.
</KeyIdea>

## The design decisions, and what each one costs

<Steps>
<Step title="What goes in">
Every channel is a hypothesis about what the task needs. A channel that predicts
the action without being causally connected to it is worse than no channel. The
test is not "could this be useful" but "is this the reason the expert acted".
</Step>

<Step title="How it is scaled">
Joint angles near $0.05$ rad and contact forces near $50$ N differ by a factor of
a thousand. At initialisation the large-magnitude channel dominates the first
layer's output entirely, and the small one is effectively invisible. Per-channel
normalisation is not cosmetic.
</Step>

<Step title="How velocity is obtained">
Differencing two consecutive positions amplifies sensor noise by $1/\Delta t$. At
30 Hz with 1 mm of position noise that is 4 cm/s of velocity noise — and at
200 Hz, 28 cm/s. Sampling faster makes it *worse*.
</Step>

<Step title="Whether the channels agree about time">
A camera 60 ms behind the joint encoders, on an arm moving 0.5 m/s, describes a
gripper 3 cm from where the encoders say it is. That is a systematic bias, not
noise, and the policy will learn to compensate for the value it saw in training.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Notice that two of these get worse at higher control rates: the shortcut becomes
more predictive as consecutive actions become more similar, and finite-difference
velocity noise grows as $\Delta t$ shrinks. The instinct to run the loop faster
because it feels more responsive makes the observation harder to design, not
easier.
</Warning>
