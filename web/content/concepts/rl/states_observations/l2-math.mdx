## Setting up the comparison

Take an expert that is a proportional controller, $a_t = K(g - x_t)$, sampled at
rate $1/\Delta t$, and give the policy two channels:

$$
o_t = \big(\underbrace{(g - x_t) + \varepsilon_t}_{\text{causal, } \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_v^2)},\ \underbrace{a_{t-1}}_{\text{shortcut}}\big)
$$

Both are legitimate observations. Neither is a bug. The question is which one a
least-squares fit will use.

<Derivation title="Which channel the objective prefers">

Fitting $a_t$ from the causal channel alone leaves the measurement noise:

$$
\min_w \mathbb{E}\big[(w\,((g-x_t)+\varepsilon_t) - K(g-x_t))^2\big]
\;\longrightarrow\; \text{residual} \;\propto\; \frac{\sigma_v^2}{\mathrm{Var}(g-x) + \sigma_v^2}
$$

Fitting from the shortcut alone leaves the expert's *innovation* — the part of
$a_t$ not predictable from $a_{t-1}$. Writing $\rho$ for the lag-one
autocorrelation of the expert's action stream,

$$
\text{residual} = (1 - \rho^2)\,\mathrm{Var}(a)
$$

For a proportional controller, $a_t = (1 - K\Delta t)\,a_{t-1}$ exactly in the
noiseless case, so $\rho = 1 - K\Delta t$ and the shortcut's residual is

$$
1 - \rho^2 = 1 - (1 - K\Delta t)^2 \approx 2K\Delta t
$$

**The assumption doing the work is that the expert is smooth relative to the
control period.** That is what makes $\rho \to 1$ as $\Delta t \to 0$. With
$K = 3$: at 10 Hz the shortcut explains 49% of the action, at 30 Hz 81%, at
100 Hz 94%, at 200 Hz 97%.

So the fit uses the shortcut whenever

$$
(1 - \rho^2)\,\mathrm{Var}(a) \;<\; \frac{\sigma_v^2}{\mathrm{Var}(g-x)+\sigma_v^2}\,\mathrm{Var}(a)
$$

which is a comparison between *your sensor noise* and *your control rate*.
Neither quantity has anything to do with the task.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
The objective is not being fooled. It is solving the problem it was given —
predict $a_t$ from $o_t$ on this dataset — and solving it correctly. What is
missing is any term representing the fact that one channel will change source at
deployment. This is why the failure survives more data, more capacity, and better
optimisation: none of them change the objective.
</KeyIdea>

## What the choice costs

Suppose the fit lands at $\alpha = 0.006$ on vision and $\beta = 0.968$ on the
shortcut — the values a least-squares fit actually produces at 100 Hz with
$\sigma_v = 1$ m. The closed-loop policy is then

$$
a_t = \alpha(g - x_t) + \beta a_{t-1}
$$

<Derivation title="The effective gain, and why the failure is quiet">

Unroll the recursion. Over timescales on which $x$ changes slowly compared with
the action filter,

$$
a_t = \alpha \sum_{j \ge 0} \beta^{\,j} (g - x_{t-j}) \;\approx\; \frac{\alpha}{1-\beta}\,(g - x_t)
$$

So the policy is *still* a proportional controller — with gain

$$
K_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\alpha}{1-\beta} = \frac{0.006}{0.032} = 0.19
$$

against the expert's $K = 3$. Since $\beta < 1$ the loop is stable and the fixed
point is still $x = g$. Nothing diverges. The time constant is simply 16 times
longer, so over a 3-second episode the remaining error is

$$
e^{-K_{\text{eff}} T} = e^{-0.19 \times 3} = 0.57
$$

With initial errors averaging 0.67 m that predicts a final error of **38 cm**.
The simulation measures **37 cm**. The training $R^2$ over this whole regime
moves from 0.999 to 0.941.

</Derivation>

<Warning>
The failure mode is sluggishness, not instability — which is what makes it
expensive. A diverging policy is reported on the first rollout. A policy that
reaches the goal 16 times too slowly gets attributed to the controller, the
gripper, the reset procedure, or "needing more data", and people tune those for
weeks.
</Warning>

## Two smaller effects with exact answers

<Steps>
<Step title="Feature scale enters the first layer quadratically">
For a linear layer, $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial W_{ij} = \delta_i x_j$, so one
SGD step changes the *contribution* $W_{ij}x_j$ by $\eta\,\delta_i x_j^2$.
Channels at $0.05$ rad and $50$ N therefore differ by $(50/0.05)^2 = 10^6$ in how
fast their contributions move. Adam's per-parameter normalisation removes most of
this from the update, but not from the initialisation: at init the contribution
variance still scales as $\mathbb{E}[x_j^2]$, so the network begins life as a
function of the large channel alone.
</Step>

<Step title="Finite differencing amplifies noise by $1/\Delta t$">
$\mathrm{Var}\!\left[(x_t - x_{t-1})/\Delta t\right] = 2\sigma^2/\Delta t^2$.
With $\sigma = 1$ mm: 4.2 cm/s at 30 Hz, 14.1 at 100 Hz, 28.3 at 200 Hz.
</Step>

<Step title="A $k$-frame slope trades noise against lag">
Least-squares slope variance is $\sigma^2 / \sum_i (t_i - \bar t)^2$, and for $k$
evenly spaced samples $\sum_i (t_i - \bar t)^2 = \Delta t^2 k(k^2-1)/12$, giving
noise $\propto k^{-3/2}$ against a lag of $(k-1)\Delta t/2$. At 30 Hz with 1 mm
noise: $k=3$ gives 2.1 cm/s at 33 ms lag, $k=10$ gives 0.33 cm/s at 150 ms. The
$3/2$ power is why a modest window helps so much.
</Step>

<Step title="Time skew is a bias, not noise">
If the camera lags the encoders by $\Delta\tau$, the two channels describe states
$\Delta\tau$ apart, an inconsistency of $\|v\|\,\Delta\tau$ — 3 cm at 0.5 m/s and
60 ms. The policy learns to compensate for the skew present during collection, so
changing the machine, the USB load, or the camera driver changes the task.
</Step>
</Steps>
