## Where the surrogate comes from

The true objective is an expectation over trajectories from $\pi_\theta$, which
you cannot sample without running $\pi_\theta$. The surrogate replaces it with
something you can compute from a batch collected under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$:

$$
L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a\mid s)}\; \hat A^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(s,a)\right]
$$

<Derivation title="The surrogate's gradient is the policy gradient">

Differentiate under the expectation. Only the ratio depends on $\theta$:

$$
\nabla_\theta L(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a\mid s)}\,\hat A\right]
$$

Evaluate at $\theta = \theta_{\text{old}}$, where the denominator equals
$\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$:

$$
\nabla_\theta L\big|_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
= \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}\,\hat A\right]
= \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\, \hat A\big]
$$

which is exactly the policy gradient with an advantage weight.

**Why this is more useful than the gradient itself.** $\nabla J$ is a vector at
one point. $L$ is a *function* you can keep climbing as $\theta$ moves — which
is what lets you run ten epochs of SGD on one batch instead of taking one step
and throwing it away.

</Derivation>

## The error the surrogate makes

<Warning>
The importance ratio corrects the **action** distribution. It does not correct
the **state** distribution. The true objective averages over states visited by
$\pi_\theta$; the surrogate averages over states visited by
$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. Nothing in $L$ knows about that mismatch, and it
grows as the policies diverge. This one omission is the entire reason a trust
region is needed.
</Warning>

TRPO turned that into a bound. Writing $\alpha = \max_s D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid s)\,\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)\big)$:

$$
J(\theta) \;\ge\; L(\theta) - C\,\alpha,
\qquad C = \frac{4\epsilon_{\max}\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^2}
$$

where $\epsilon_{\max} = \max_{s,a}|A^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(s,a)|$.
Maximising the right-hand side guarantees improvement in the left — so "improve
the surrogate while staying close in KL" is a theorem, not a hope. Note the
$1/(1-\gamma)^2$: the bound degrades quadratically as the horizon grows, which
is why the theoretically-correct step sizes are far too small to use and
practical TRPO uses the KL as a constraint with a tuned radius instead.

## The clipped objective, case by case

$$
L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\Big[\min\big(r_t \hat A_t,\;
\operatorname{clip}(r_t,\, 1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon)\,\hat A_t\big)\Big]
$$

<Derivation title="All four cases, and what the min is for">

**Case 1 — $\hat A > 0$, $r \le 1+\epsilon$.** Both terms equal $r\hat A$ (or
the clip is inactive). Gradient active: keep increasing this action's
probability.

**Case 2 — $\hat A > 0$, $r > 1+\epsilon$.** Clipped term is $(1+\epsilon)\hat A$,
unclipped is $r\hat A$ which is *larger*. The min picks the clipped, constant
term. **Gradient zero** — you have already moved this action up as far as the
evidence justifies.

**Case 3 — $\hat A < 0$, $r \ge 1-\epsilon$.** Clip inactive or both equal.
Gradient active: keep decreasing this action's probability.

**Case 4 — $\hat A < 0$, $r < 1-\epsilon$.** Clipped term is
$(1-\epsilon)\hat A$; unclipped is $r\hat A$ which is *more negative*, hence
smaller. The min picks the **clipped** term. Gradient zero — you have already
suppressed this action enough.

**Now the case that explains the min.** Take $\hat A < 0$ with $r > 1+\epsilon$:
the policy has made a *bad* action substantially more likely. Clipping alone
would return $(1+\epsilon)\hat A$, a constant — no gradient, no recovery. But
$r\hat A$ is more negative than $(1+\epsilon)\hat A$, so the min selects the
**unclipped** term and the gradient survives, pushing the probability back down.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
The rule underneath all four cases: the gradient vanishes only when the ratio
has moved past the threshold **in the direction the advantage favours**. Move
too far the wrong way and the objective still pulls you back. That asymmetry is
why $L^{\text{CLIP}}$ is a pessimistic lower bound on the unclipped surrogate,
and why the min cannot be simplified away.
</KeyIdea>

## What the objective does not give you

<Steps>
<Step title="No constraint on the ratio">
Nothing projects $\theta$ anywhere. A large step can carry $r$ far outside
$[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$ in one update, and epochs 2 through 10 begin from a
policy that has already moved. Monitor the empirical KL; early-stopping the
epoch loop on it is standard practice and is effectively a trust region
smuggled back in.
</Step>

<Step title="No guarantee of monotonic improvement">
TRPO's bound came from the KL penalty with a specific constant. PPO replaces
that with a heuristic that behaves similarly and proves nothing.
</Step>

<Step title="No specification of the algorithm">
Advantage normalisation, value-function clipping, reward scaling, orthogonal
initialisation, and learning-rate annealing all materially affect results and
none are in the objective. "We used PPO" names a family, not a method.
</Step>
</Steps>
