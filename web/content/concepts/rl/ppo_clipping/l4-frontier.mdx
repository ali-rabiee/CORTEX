## How this line of work evolved

The arc is: *trust regions → clipping → "it was the details all along" → PPO
as infrastructure*.

TRPO established **why** constraining policy updates works (monotonic
improvement bounds). PPO kept the constraint in spirit and deleted the
second-order machinery; the 2020-era empirical papers then showed that much
of what we call "PPO" is really a bundle of normalization and annealing
tricks. None of that dented its adoption — it dominated because it is simple,
parallelizes trivially, and fails gracefully.

## Where PPO is load-bearing now

Two places you will actually meet it:

<PaperGrid>
  <PaperCard refKey="ouyang2022instructgpt" />
  <PaperCard refKey="rudin2022massively" />
</PaperGrid>

In robotics, PPO + massive sim parallelism (Isaac Gym/Isaac Lab) is the
standard recipe for locomotion and increasingly for whole-body control;
sample inefficiency stops mattering when one GPU simulates 4096 robots. In
LLM post-training, PPO variants compete with simpler preference methods
(DPO-family), but when teams need online RL against a reward model, clipped
policy gradients are still the default.

<KeyIdea>

Interview-ready synthesis: "PPO won not by being the best optimizer but by
being the most *forgiving* one — first-order, a one-screen diff from vanilla
PG, robust across two orders of magnitude in env count. That's why it
outlived TRPO and became the substrate for both RLHF and sim-to-real
locomotion."
