## Failure modes

Action space design mistakes: (1) Unnormalized actions — if joint 1 has range $[-1, 1]$ and joint 7 has range $[-100, 100]$, the policy struggles to learn appropriate scales. Fix: normalize all actions to $[-1, 1]$. (2) Action dimension too high — learning a policy for $a \in \mathbb{R}^{30}$ (e.g., full dexterous hand) is extremely sample-inefficient. Fix: use action primitives, hierarchical actions, or pre-trained low-level controllers. (3) Missing $\tanh$ squashing — Gaussian policies can output $|a| \gg 1$, causing simulator instabilities or NaN gradients. (4) Incorrect log-probability computation when using $\tanh$ — forgetting the Jacobian correction term leads to biased policy gradient estimates.

## Interview-ready answer

Action space design is one of the most impactful choices in robot RL. For manipulation, I typically use end-effector delta actions $\Delta(x,y,z,\text{rot}) \in \mathbb{R}^{6-7}$ with a low-level controller, rather than raw joint torques — this dramatically improves sample efficiency. For locomotion, joint position targets with PD controllers are standard. Key implementation details: always normalize to $[-1, 1]$, use $\tanh$ squashing with log-prob Jacobian correction ($-\sum \log(1 - \tanh^2)$), and consider action smoothing (temporal filtering) to avoid jerky motions. For high-DOF systems like dexterous hands ($d \geq 20$), action space reduction via PCA, synergies, or hierarchical policies is critical for tractable learning.
