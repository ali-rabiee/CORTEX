## The TD residual

Everything is built from one quantity. For any value function $V$ — correct or
not:

$$
\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)
$$

If $V = V^\pi$ then $\mathbb{E}[\delta_t \mid s_t, a_t] = A^\pi(s_t,a_t)$, so
$\delta_t$ is an unbiased one-step advantage estimate. If $V$ is wrong, it is
still a well-defined number and everything below still holds algebraically.

## From residuals to n-step estimators

<Derivation title="The $n$-step advantage telescopes">

Define the $n$-step estimator by running $n$ real rewards and then bootstrapping:

$$
\hat A^{(n)}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\gamma^l r_{t+l} + \gamma^n V(s_{t+n}) - V(s_t)
$$

Now expand the discounted sum of residuals:

$$
\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\gamma^l\delta_{t+l}
= \sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\gamma^l\Big(r_{t+l} + \gamma V(s_{t+l+1}) - V(s_{t+l})\Big)
$$

Split it into three sums. The middle one contributes
$\gamma^{l+1}V(s_{t+l+1})$ and the third contributes $-\gamma^{l}V(s_{t+l})$, so
each interior $\gamma^{k}V(s_{t+k})$ for $1 \le k \le n-1$ appears once with each
sign and cancels. What survives are the two endpoints:

$$
\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\gamma^l\delta_{t+l}
= \sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\gamma^l r_{t+l} + \gamma^n V(s_{t+n}) - V(s_t)
= \hat A^{(n)}_t
$$

**This is an identity, not an approximation.** It uses no property of $V$
whatsoever — not correctness, not the Bellman equation, not convergence.

</Derivation>

The two ends of the family:

$$
\hat A^{(1)}_t = \delta_t
\qquad\qquad
\hat A^{(\infty)}_t = \sum_{l\ge0}\gamma^l r_{t+l} - V(s_t) = G_t - V(s_t)
$$

## The exponential average, and why it is cheap

<Derivation title="GAE collapses to a single geometric sum">

Take the exponentially weighted average over $n$, with weights summing to one:

$$
\hat A^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)}_t
= (1-\lambda)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n-1}\hat A^{(n)}_t
= (1-\lambda)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n-1}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\gamma^l\delta_{t+l}
$$

**Swap the order of summation.** The term $\gamma^l\delta_{t+l}$ appears in
$\hat A^{(n)}$ for every $n \ge l+1$, so collect its total weight:

$$
= (1-\lambda)\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\gamma^l\delta_{t+l}\sum_{n=l+1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n-1}
= (1-\lambda)\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\gamma^l\delta_{t+l}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{l}}{1-\lambda}
$$

The $(1-\lambda)$ cancels:

$$
\boxed{\;\hat A^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(\gamma\lambda)^l\,\delta_{t+l}\;}
$$

and reading it backwards gives the recursion that implementations use:

$$
\hat A_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\,\hat A_{t+1}
$$

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
An average over infinitely many estimators costs one backward pass over the
rollout and one multiply-add per step — cheaper than computing any single
$n$-step estimator by its definition. That collapse is the reason GAE became
universal rather than remaining a nice idea about interpolation.
</KeyIdea>

## Where the bias is

Both limits are worth stating carefully, because the unbiasedness properties of
the *estimator* and of the *gradient* differ.

<Steps>
<Step title="$\lambda = 1$: unbiased gradient, for any $V$">
The sum telescopes to $G_t - V(s_t)$. As an estimate of $A^\pi$ this is biased
whenever $V \ne V^\pi$. But the policy gradient is not, because $V(s_t)$ is a
function of the state alone — a valid baseline — and
$\mathbb{E}_a[\nabla\log\pi(a\mid s)\,b(s)] = 0$. A wrong critic costs variance
here, never bias.
</Step>

<Step title="$\lambda = 0$: minimum variance, maximum bias">
$\hat A_t = \delta_t$. Only one reward and one transition are random, so the
variance is $O(1)$ regardless of horizon. The estimate is off by exactly the
critic's Bellman error at that state.
</Step>

<Step title="$0 < \lambda < 1$: the bias lives in the bootstrap term">
The estimator contains $\gamma^n V(s_{t+n})$, and $s_{t+n}$ was influenced by
$a_t$. So it is *not* a function of $s_t$ alone, the baseline argument does not
apply, and a wrong $V$ biases the gradient. This is the precise location of
GAE's bias, and it vanishes exactly when $V = V^\pi$.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The original paper makes a point that is easy to miss: $\gamma$ is *also* a
variance-reduction parameter, not only a statement about the objective.
Discounting shortens the effective credit-assignment path, so lowering it
reduces variance while biasing you toward a shorter-horizon problem. In
principle $\gamma$ defines the problem and $\lambda$ estimates it; in practice
both get tuned for variance, which is why the clean separation blurs when you
read other people's hyperparameters.
</Warning>

## Two things that break the identity

<Steps>
<Step title="Episode boundaries">
Both the bootstrap and the recursion must be severed at a true terminal:
$\delta_T = r_T - V(s_T)$ and $\hat A_T = \delta_T$. Forgetting either lets
credit leak across episodes, and it inflates advantages at exactly the steps
that matter most.
</Step>

<Step title="Time limits are not terminals">
A truncation at a step limit is not the end of the world — the value of the
final state is genuinely non-zero, so you must bootstrap $V(s_T)$ there.
Treating a timeout as a terminal tells the critic the world stops at 1000 steps
and systematically depresses values near the limit.
</Step>
</Steps>
