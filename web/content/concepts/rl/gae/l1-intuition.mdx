## The quantity you actually want

Policy gradients weight each action by how good it was. "Good" should mean
*relative to what was typical from that state* — otherwise a mediocre action in
a great state looks better than a brilliant action in a terrible one. That
relative quantity is the advantage:

$$
A^\pi(s,a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s)
$$

You do not have $Q^\pi$ or $V^\pi$. You have a noisy critic and some sampled
rewards, and everything about GAE follows from that.

## Two bad options

<Steps>
<Step title="Use the real rewards to the end of the episode">
$\hat A_t = G_t - V(s_t)$. Honest — this is what actually happened. But $G_t$
accumulates every random transition and every random action for the rest of the
episode, so its variance grows with the horizon. One extremely noisy number now
has to explain one action.
</Step>

<Step title="Use the critic after one step">
$\hat A_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$. Low variance: only one reward and
one transition are random. But it is wrong by however wrong $V$ is, and early in
training $V$ is very wrong.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Intuition>
You want to know whether a chess move was good. You could play the game to the
end and see who won — accurate, but one game's outcome is mostly noise about
everything except the last few moves. Or you could make the move and ask a
positional evaluator how the resulting position looks — stable, but only as good
as the evaluator.

Or you could play three moves ahead and *then* evaluate. That is the n-step
estimator, and the whole family lies on a line between the two extremes.
</Intuition>

## The dial

GAE does not pick a point on that line. It averages over all of them, with
weights that decay geometrically:

$$
\hat A^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)}_t = (1-\lambda)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \lambda^{n-1}\, \hat A^{(n)}_t
$$

<Figure src="/figures/gae-lambda-dial.svg"
        alt="Left: bar charts of the weight on each n-step estimator, sharply decaying for lambda 0.5 and nearly flat for lambda 0.95. Right: curves showing bias falling and variance rising as lambda goes from 0 to 1, with a highlighted band around 0.9 to 0.97."
        caption="Small lambda concentrates the weight on short lookaheads and leans on the critic. Large lambda spreads it over long ones and leans on observed rewards. The useful range is near the top, because critics are usually the weaker link." />

The reason this is not just a nice idea is that the infinite sum collapses into
a single backward pass — a one-line recursion, cheaper than computing any single
n-step estimator naively. Level 2 does that derivation.

<KeyIdea>
$\lambda$ is a statement about **how much you trust your critic**. $\gamma$ is a
statement about **how far ahead you care**. They multiply together in the
recursion and get conflated constantly, but changing $\gamma$ changes the
problem you are solving, while changing $\lambda$ only changes how you estimate
it.
</KeyIdea>

## Setting it

Defaults cluster around $\lambda = 0.95$, and large-scale sweeps support roughly
$0.9$–$0.97$ as robust. That is high — closer to Monte Carlo than to TD — which
tells you something: in practice the critic is usually the less reliable of the
two sources, so you lean on real rewards and use the critic mainly to cut the
tail.

<Warning>
Tune $\lambda$ against your critic's quality, not in isolation. If explained
variance is near zero, a low $\lambda$ is feeding critic noise into every
advantage — the exact opposite of the variance reduction you wanted. And check
your advantage normalisation before blaming $\lambda$ for anything: per-batch
normalisation interacts strongly with it, and an unnormalised advantage makes
the effective learning rate drift as the reward scale changes over training.
</Warning>
