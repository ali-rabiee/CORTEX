interview:
  answer: >-
    GAE is how you estimate the advantage without having to choose between two
    bad options. You want to know how much better than typical an action was.
    If you use the actual return to the end of the episode you get something
    honest but with variance that grows with the horizon — every random
    transition and every random action after this one is noise in a single
    scalar. If you use the critic after one step you get a low-variance number
    that's wrong by however wrong the critic is. In between are the n-step
    estimators, and GAE takes an exponentially weighted average over all of
    them with weights one minus lambda times lambda to the n minus one. The
    reason that's practical rather than just elegant is that it collapses. The
    n-step advantage telescopes into a discounted sum of TD residuals — that's
    an algebraic identity, true for any value function, correct or not — and
    then swapping the order of summation in the exponential average gives you
    the sum of gamma-lambda to the l times delta t plus l. Read backwards
    that's a one-line recursion: A_t equals delta_t plus gamma lambda A_{t+1}.
    So an average over infinitely many estimators costs one backward pass over
    the rollout. On what lambda means: it's a trust setting for the critic, and
    it's a genuinely separate knob from gamma. Gamma defines the problem, how
    far ahead rewards count; lambda only changes how you estimate it. At lambda
    equals one it telescopes to the return minus V of s_t, and the policy
    gradient there is unbiased for any critic, because V of s_t is a pure
    state-dependent baseline. Below one, the estimator contains gamma to the n
    times V of s_{t+n}, and that state was influenced by the action you're
    scoring — so it isn't a valid baseline and that's exactly where the bias
    enters. Practically, 0.95 is the default and 0.9 to 0.97 holds up in
    large-scale sweeps. That's high, which tells you the critic is usually the
    weaker link.
  followUps:
    - q: "How would you tune lambda in practice?"
      a: >-
        Against the critic's explained variance rather than in isolation. If
        explained variance is near zero the critic is noise and a low lambda
        feeds that noise into every advantage — the opposite of the variance
        reduction I wanted. So I'd start at 0.95, check explained variance,
        and only consider going lower once the critic is genuinely tracking
        returns. The other thing I'd check first is advantage normalisation,
        because per-batch normalisation interacts strongly with lambda and an
        unnormalised advantage means the effective step size drifts with the
        reward scale over training. In my experience more GAE tuning is
        actually a missing normalisation than a wrong lambda.
    - q: "What are the classic GAE implementation bugs?"
      a: >-
        Two, and they're mirror images. The first is not severing at true
        terminals — at an episode end there's no future, so delta is just the
        reward minus V of s, with no bootstrap, and the recursion has to
        restart. Miss either zeroing and credit leaks across episode
        boundaries. The second is the opposite mistake: treating a time-limit
        truncation as a terminal. A timeout isn't the end of the world, the
        final state genuinely has non-zero value, and you must bootstrap V of
        s_T there. If you don't, you're telling the critic that everything
        stops at step 1000, which systematically depresses values near the
        limit and can teach the policy some strange behaviour approaching it.
    - q: "Does GAE apply off-policy?"
      a: >-
        Not directly, and that's the honest limitation. The derivation assumes
        the residuals come from trajectories generated by the current policy —
        the TD residual is only an advantage estimate for the policy that
        produced the transition. In the distributed setting where actors lag
        the learner, or genuinely off-policy, you need importance corrections,
        which is what V-trace in IMPALA does: it's structurally the same
        backward recursion with clipped importance weights folded in, and the
        clipping bounds both the bias and the variance of that correction.
        Retrace is the same idea for off-policy n-step returns. I'd describe
        V-trace as GAE's sibling for when you can't guarantee on-policy data.
    - q: "Is lambda really separate from gamma, or is that a textbook distinction?"
      a: >-
        Cleanly separate in theory, blurred in practice, and the original paper
        is explicit about it. Gamma changes the objective — the discounted
        return you're optimising — while lambda only changes the estimator for
        the same objective. But gamma also shortens the effective
        credit-assignment path, so lowering it reduces variance while biasing
        you toward a shorter-horizon problem. That means people do tune gamma
        partly for variance reasons, which muddies the distinction when you're
        reading someone else's hyperparameters. I'd still keep them separate
        when reasoning: if the task genuinely needs a long horizon, fix gamma
        from the horizon and use lambda for the variance.

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Problems confined to the end of episodes → boundary handling">
Values depressed and behaviour erratic near a time limit means truncation is
being treated as termination. Distinguish the two and bootstrap at timeouts.
Credit leaking across episodes is the mirror bug: check the recursion restarts.
</Step>

<Step title="Advantages look enormous or tiny → normalisation, not $\lambda$">
Per-batch advantage normalisation is not cosmetic; without it the effective step
size drifts with the reward scale as the policy improves. Check this before
tuning anything.
</Step>

<Step title="Training noisy and directionless → the critic, then $\lambda$">
Explained variance near zero means the advantages are mostly critic noise. Fix
the critic — capacity, learning rate, target normalisation — or raise $\lambda$
so you lean on it less. Lowering $\lambda$ here makes it worse.
</Step>

<Step title="Learns slowly on long-horizon tasks → variance, and $\gamma$ is implicated too">
Monte Carlo variance grows with the horizon, so $\lambda$ near 1 gets expensive
as episodes lengthen. This is one of the places where $\gamma$ and $\lambda$
genuinely interact, and it is worth naming that rather than pretending they are
independent.
</Step>

<Step title="Actors lagging the learner in a distributed setup → GAE is the wrong tool">
The derivation assumes on-policy trajectories. Use V-trace, which is the same
backward recursion with clipped importance weights.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The detail that reads as having implemented this rather than read it is the
bias location. Most candidates say "$\lambda$ trades bias for variance". Few can
say that at $\lambda = 1$ the gradient is unbiased *for any critic*, because
$V(s_t)$ is a valid baseline — and that below 1 the bias enters through
$\gamma^n V(s_{t+n})$, which is not a valid baseline because $a_t$ helped
determine $s_{t+n}$. That is one sentence, and it demonstrates the whole
derivation.
</Warning>
