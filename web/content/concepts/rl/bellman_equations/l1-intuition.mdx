## Value splits in two

You want to know how good a state is: the total discounted reward you expect
from here on. Written directly, that is an infinite sum over an unknown future.

Bellman's observation is that the sum has a seam in it. Peel off the first term:

$$
V(s) = \mathbb{E}\big[\, \underbrace{r}_{\text{one step}} \;+\;
\gamma \underbrace{V(s')}_{\text{everything after}} \,\big]
$$

The entire remaining future — every branch, every timestep — has collapsed into
one number: the value of wherever you land next. And that number is something
you already keep an estimate of.

<Intuition>
Evaluating a chess position works this way. You do not enumerate every line to
checkmate. You look one move ahead and ask how good the *resulting* positions
are, using judgement you have already built up about positions in general.

The Bellman equation says that habit is not a shortcut — for the true value
function, it is exactly correct.
</Intuition>

## Why that matters more than it looks

<Figure src="/figures/bellman-bootstrap-vs-mc.svg"
        alt="A trajectory of states with rewards. An orange arrow spans the whole episode, labelled Monte Carlo return. A green arrow spans one step and then points to a box holding the agent's own value estimate of the next state."
        caption="Two ways to build a learning target for the same state. Monte Carlo waits and sums; bootstrapping takes one real reward and trusts its own estimate for the rest." />

Without the recursion you must wait until the episode ends to know what
happened, and your target's variance accumulates over every random transition
and every random action along the way. With it, you learn from a single
transition.

<KeyIdea>
The trade is exact and worth stating in these words: **Monte Carlo targets are
unbiased with high variance; bootstrapped targets are biased with low
variance.** The bias is your own current error, borrowed and reused. Everything
in between — n-step returns, TD($\lambda$), GAE — is a dial between the two.
</KeyIdea>

## The two equations

<Steps>
<Step title="Bellman expectation — how good is this policy?">
Fix a policy $\pi$ and ask what it is worth:
$V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi,\, s'\sim P}\big[r + \gamma V^\pi(s')\big]$.
No choosing happens; $\pi$ already decided. This equation is *linear* in $V$,
which for a small known MDP means you can solve it exactly with linear algebra.
</Step>

<Step title="Bellman optimality — how good could any policy be?">
Replace "whatever $\pi$ does" with "the best action":
$V^*(s) = \max_a \mathbb{E}\big[r + \gamma V^*(s')\big]$.
The max makes it nonlinear, so there is no direct solve — but iterating it still
converges, which is value iteration.
</Step>

<Step title="The action-value versions">
$Q^\pi(s,a)$ and $Q^*(s,a)$ say the same things one step later, after the action
is fixed. They matter because acting greedily on $Q$ needs no model at all —
just $\arg\max_a Q(s,a)$ — whereas acting greedily on $V$ requires knowing where
each action leads.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Bootstrapping is safe in the tabular case for a specific reason: the backup
shrinks errors by a factor of $\gamma$ every sweep, so whatever error you
borrowed gets discounted away. That guarantee does not survive function
approximation, where updating the value at one state moves the value at others.
Combine bootstrapping, function approximation, and off-policy data and you have
the deadly triad — the reason target networks exist, and the reason deep value
learning is more fragile than the tabular theory suggests.
</Warning>
