## Deriving the expectation equation

Start from the definition of the return and notice it is self-similar:

$$
G_t = r_t + \gamma r_{t+1} + \gamma^2 r_{t+2} + \cdots
    = r_t + \gamma\big(r_{t+1} + \gamma r_{t+2} + \cdots\big)
    = r_t + \gamma G_{t+1}
$$

<Derivation title="From $G_t = r_t + \gamma G_{t+1}$ to the Bellman expectation equation">

**Step 1 — definition.** $V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t \mid s_t = s]$.

**Step 2 — substitute the recursion and use linearity of expectation.**

$$
V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\big[r_t + \gamma G_{t+1} \mid s_t = s\big]
= \mathbb{E}_\pi[r_t \mid s_t = s] + \gamma\, \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_{t+1}\mid s_t = s]
$$

**Step 3 — condition on where you land (the tower property).**

$$
\mathbb{E}_\pi[G_{t+1}\mid s_t = s]
= \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi,\, s'\sim P}\Big[\; \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_{t+1}\mid s_{t+1}=s',\, s_t = s]\;\Big]
$$

**Step 4 — this is where Markov is spent.** The inner expectation conditions on
both $s_{t+1}$ and $s_t$. The Markov property says the past adds nothing once
you know $s_{t+1}$, so it equals $V^\pi(s')$. Drop the $s_t$ and assemble:

$$
\boxed{\;V^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)\big[R(s,a,s') + \gamma V^\pi(s')\big]\;}
$$

</Derivation>

Note that this is **linear** in $V^\pi$. In matrix form
$V^\pi = R^\pi + \gamma P^\pi V^\pi$, so $V^\pi = (I - \gamma P^\pi)^{-1}R^\pi$
whenever the inverse exists — which it does for $\gamma < 1$, since
$\gamma P^\pi$ has spectral radius below one.

## The optimality operator

Define $\mathcal{T}: \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|} \to \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}$ by

$$
(\mathcal{T}V)(s) = \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)\big[R(s,a,s') + \gamma V(s')\big]
$$

$V^*$ is by definition its fixed point, $\mathcal{T}V^* = V^*$. The max makes
this nonlinear, so there is no inverse to take — and yet iterating it converges,
for the following reason.

<Derivation title="$\mathcal{T}$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in the sup norm">

**Lemma.** $\big|\max_a f(a) - \max_a g(a)\big| \le \max_a |f(a) - g(a)|$.

*Proof.* Let $a^\star = \arg\max_a f(a)$. Then
$\max_a f - \max_a g \le f(a^\star) - g(a^\star) \le \max_a|f-g|$, since
$\max_a g \ge g(a^\star)$. Swap the roles of $f$ and $g$ for the other
direction.

**Main argument.** For any two value functions $V_1, V_2$ and any state $s$:

$$
\big|(\mathcal{T}V_1)(s) - (\mathcal{T}V_2)(s)\big|
\;\le\; \max_a\, \gamma \left|\sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)\big[V_1(s') - V_2(s')\big]\right|
$$

by the lemma — the reward terms are identical and cancel. Move the absolute
value inside:

$$
\le\; \gamma \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)\,\big|V_1(s') - V_2(s')\big|
\;\le\; \gamma \max_{s'}\big|V_1(s') - V_2(s')\big|
\;=\; \gamma\|V_1 - V_2\|_\infty
$$

**The assumption doing the work is in that last inequality**: $P(\cdot\mid s,a)$
is a probability distribution, so a weighted average of $|V_1 - V_2|$ cannot
exceed its maximum. Taking the max over $s$ on the left gives
$\|\mathcal{T}V_1 - \mathcal{T}V_2\|_\infty \le \gamma\|V_1 - V_2\|_\infty$.

**Consequence.** $(\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}, \|\cdot\|_\infty)$ is complete, so
Banach's fixed-point theorem gives a *unique* $V^*$ and

$$
\|V_k - V^*\|_\infty \le \gamma^k \|V_0 - V^*\|_\infty
$$

Geometric convergence with modulus $\gamma$ — which also says that as
$\gamma \to 1$, convergence slows like $1/(1-\gamma)$.

</Derivation>

## What breaks with function approximation

Tabular value iteration applies $\mathcal{T}$ exactly. With a parametric $V_\theta$
you cannot represent $\mathcal{T}V_\theta$, so you fit it — and the update is

$$
V \;\leftarrow\; \Pi\,\mathcal{T}\,V
$$

where $\Pi$ projects onto the representable functions, in the $L_2$ norm
weighted by whatever distribution your data came from.

<Warning>
Each piece is individually harmless. $\mathcal{T}$ contracts in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$;
$\Pi$ is a non-expansion in its own weighted $L_2$ norm. **The composition need
not contract in either**, because the norms are different — and off-policy data
makes the projection's weighting diverge from the distribution the operator
respects. Baird's counterexample is a small linear MDP where the parameters
provably diverge. This is the deadly triad stated precisely: bootstrapping,
function approximation, and off-policy data. Any two are fine; all three have no
guarantee.
</Warning>

## The max, and the bias it creates

<Derivation title="Why $\max$ over noisy estimates overestimates">

Suppose $\hat Q(s,a)$ is unbiased for every action: $\mathbb{E}[\hat Q(s,a)] = Q(s,a)$.
The max function is convex, so Jensen's inequality gives

$$
\mathbb{E}\Big[\max_a \hat Q(s,a)\Big] \;\ge\; \max_a \mathbb{E}\big[\hat Q(s,a)\big]
= \max_a Q(s,a)
$$

with strict inequality whenever the estimates carry independent noise. The max
is a *selection* rule, and it selects whichever action's noise happened to be
positive.

Bootstrapping then makes it worse: the inflated value becomes a target for the
previous state, and the bias propagates backwards through the whole chain.

**Double Q-learning's fix follows directly.** Use one estimator to choose the
action and a second, independent one to evaluate it:
$r + \gamma\, Q_2\big(s', \arg\max_{a'} Q_1(s',a')\big)$. The selection noise and
the evaluation noise are now independent, so the correlation Jensen exploited is
broken.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
Three separate results, often blurred together. **Contraction** is why tabular
methods converge. **The triad** is why deep versions can diverge. **Jensen** is
why they overestimate even when they converge. Different problems, different
fixes — target networks and ensembles for the second, double estimators for the
third.
</KeyIdea>
