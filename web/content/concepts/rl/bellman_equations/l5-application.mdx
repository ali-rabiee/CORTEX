interview:
  answer: >-
    The Bellman equation says the value of a state splits into the immediate
    reward plus the discounted value of where you land: V of s equals the
    expectation of r plus gamma V of s prime. It follows in two lines from the
    return being self-similar, and the step where you actually spend the Markov
    property is dropping the history when you condition on the next state.
    There are two versions and the difference matters. The expectation equation
    evaluates a fixed policy and is linear in V, so for a small known MDP you
    can literally solve it by inverting I minus gamma P. The optimality
    equation takes a max over actions, which makes it nonlinear — no direct
    solve — but the operator is still a gamma-contraction in the sup norm, so
    iterating it converges geometrically. That contraction proof is worth
    knowing line by line, because the step that does the work is bounding a
    weighted average of value differences by its maximum, which holds only
    because the transition probabilities sum to one. What the equation buys in
    practice is bootstrapping: you can build a learning target from one
    transition instead of waiting for an episode to end. The price is bias —
    your target contains your own current error. Monte Carlo is unbiased and
    high variance; TD is biased and low variance, and n-step returns and GAE
    are the dial between them. And then the part I'd want to get precise. In
    the tabular case bootstrapping is safe because the backup shrinks errors by
    gamma every sweep. With function approximation the update isn't the Bellman
    operator, it's the operator followed by a projection onto what your network
    can represent — and that projection is a non-expansion in a data-weighted
    L2 norm while the operator contracts in L infinity. Different norms, so the
    composition contracts in neither. That's the deadly triad, and Baird's
    counterexample shows it provably diverging on a tiny linear problem. It's
    the reason target networks exist.
  followUps:
    - q: "Your Q-values grow without bound during training. What's your diagnosis and fix order?"
      a: >-
        That's the triad presenting, so I'd work on the feedback loop between
        the target and the estimate. First check the target network is
        actually being used and its update period isn't so short it's
        effectively absent — a Polyak tau of 0.005 or a hard copy every few
        thousand steps. Then check whether the behaviour distribution has
        drifted far from what the critic is being evaluated on, because
        off-policy-ness is the third leg; if I'm training offline, that's when
        I'd add a conservatism penalty rather than fight it with
        optimisation tricks. Reward scale is worth a look too, since values
        live on a scale of R max over one minus gamma and an unnormalised
        reward at gamma 0.99 gives targets a hundred times the per-step
        magnitude. Lowering gamma is a legitimate last resort — it strengthens
        the contraction — but it changes the problem you're solving.
    - q: "How is overestimation bias different from divergence?"
      a: >-
        They're independent, and conflating them is the common mistake.
        Overestimation comes from the max operator, not from function
        approximation: even with perfectly unbiased per-action estimates,
        Jensen's inequality says the expectation of the max is at least the max
        of the expectations, because taking a max over noisy numbers
        preferentially picks whichever noise was positive. That happens in
        tabular Q-learning too. It shows up as a critic that's stably
        converged but systematically too high, and a policy that exploits the
        inflated actions. The fix is decorrelating selection from evaluation —
        double Q-learning, or the twin critics with a min in TD3 and SAC.
        Divergence is a different failure with a different fix, and if I saw
        both I'd treat them separately.
    - q: "When would you use Monte Carlo returns instead of bootstrapping?"
      a: >-
        When the bias is more expensive than the variance. That's typically
        short episodes, where the variance of the full return is manageable
        anyway, and settings where the value function is badly wrong early on
        so bootstrapping is mostly propagating noise. It's also the safer
        choice when the deadly triad conditions are all present and I want to
        remove one leg — Monte Carlo targets don't bootstrap, so that
        combination can't diverge. In practice I'd rarely go fully to either
        end; GAE with lambda around 0.95 is the standard compromise and the
        lambda is the knob.
    - q: "Where does the Bellman equation stop being the right tool?"
      a: >-
        Two places worth naming. When the return distribution matters and not
        just its mean — distributional RL backs up the whole distribution
        through the same operator and gets large empirical gains, apparently
        as a representation-learning effect. And when the data is offline and
        narrow, where the max in the optimality equation queries actions the
        dataset never covered and the errors there are unbounded. That's
        exactly what offline RL methods constrain. The interesting
        counterpoint is return-conditioned sequence modelling, which drops
        Bellman backups entirely — but it loses the ability to stitch, to
        combine the good halves of two mediocre trajectories, which is
        dynamic programming's signature move.

## Reading the symptoms

The Bellman equation shows up in interviews as a broken value function, and
three distinct failures get reported with the same words.

<Steps>
<Step title="Q-values growing without bound → the deadly triad">
Loss climbing alongside the values, often accelerating. Check the target network
first (present? update period sane?), then how far off-policy your data is, then
the reward scale — values live on a scale of $R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$, so an
unnormalised reward at $\gamma = 0.99$ produces targets a hundred times the
per-step magnitude.
</Step>

<Step title="Stable but inflated critic the policy exploits → overestimation">
Training looks healthy; evaluation does not match the predicted returns. This is
Jensen on the max, and it happens in tabular Q-learning too. Fix by
decorrelating selection from evaluation, not by damping the optimisation.
</Step>

<Step title="Value loss flat and high, returns plateaued → not a Bellman problem">
If the state is not Markov, the same state carries different returns and the
residual is irreducible. No target network or double estimator helps. Go back
and check the state definition.
</Step>

<Step title="Learns slowly on long-horizon tasks → the contraction is weak">
Error shrinks by $\gamma$ per backup, so information needs about $1/(1-\gamma)$
backups to propagate. At $\gamma = 0.999$ that is thousands. This is what n-step
returns, TD($\lambda$), and GAE exist to shortcut — not a bug, a rate.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The discriminating detail in this answer is the norm mismatch. Most candidates
can say "deadly triad: bootstrapping, function approximation, off-policy". Very
few can say *why* those three interact — that the Bellman operator contracts in
the sup norm while the projection is a non-expansion in a data-weighted $L_2$
norm, so the composition contracts in neither. That one sentence, plus knowing
Baird's counterexample is linear, is worth more than the whole rest of the
answer.
</Warning>
