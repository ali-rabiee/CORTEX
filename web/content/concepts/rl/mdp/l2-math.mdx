## The tuple

$$
\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S},\ \mathcal{A},\ P,\ R,\ \gamma),
\qquad
P: \mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S} \to [0,1],
\qquad
\gamma \in [0,1)
$$

with the defining conditional independence

$$
P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t, s_{t-1}, a_{t-1}, \ldots, s_0) = P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t, a_t)
$$

A policy $\pi(a\mid s)$ induces a distribution over trajectories, and the object
being optimised is the discounted return from time $t$:

$$
G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k\, r_{t+k}
$$

## What $\gamma$ is actually doing

<Derivation title="Boundedness and the effective horizon">

**Step 1 — the return is finite.** Assume rewards are bounded,
$|r_t| \le R_{\max}$. Then

$$
|G_t| \;\le\; \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^k R_{\max}
\;=\; R_{\max}\,\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
$$

using the geometric series, which converges precisely because $\gamma < 1$.
Note both assumptions are load-bearing: unbounded rewards break the bound even
with discounting.

**Step 2 — read the same sum as a horizon.** The total discount weight is
$1/(1-\gamma)$, and it is distributed over time as $\gamma^k$. The number of
steps that carry most of that weight is therefore $\approx 1/(1-\gamma)$: about
20 steps at $\gamma = 0.95$, 100 at $0.99$, 1000 at $0.999$.

**Step 3 — the half-life, if you want it sharper.** Rewards are discounted to
half their value after $k_{1/2} = \ln(0.5)/\ln \gamma$ steps — about 69 steps at
$\gamma = 0.99$.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
$\gamma$ appears in the problem definition but behaves like a hyperparameter,
and it moves three things at once: the planning horizon, the scale of the value
function ($\sim R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$), and the variance of return estimates.
Raising it to fix myopia buys you a harder estimation problem, which is why
GAE exists.
</KeyIdea>

## Making a process Markov

The Markov property is a property of $\mathcal{S}$, so the constructive question
is what to put in it.

<Derivation title="Finite-history augmentation, and its limit">

Suppose the true dependence has finite extent $k$:

$$
P(o_{t+1}\mid o_{1:t}, a_{1:t}) = P(o_{t+1} \mid o_{t-k+1:t},\, a_{t-k+1:t})
$$

Define the augmented state $\tilde s_t = (o_{t-k+1}, \ldots, o_t)$. Then
$P(\tilde s_{t+1}\mid \tilde s_t, a_t)$ is well defined — the new state contains
exactly what the old dependence needed — and the process is first-order Markov
over $\tilde{\mathcal{S}}$ by construction.

That is all frame stacking is. Two frames for velocity, three for acceleration.

**The assumption doing the work is that $k$ is finite and known.** It fails for
any dependence on an event at unbounded distance: a door you unlocked, an object
you saw inside a container, a mode you switched the machine into. No fixed $k$
covers those.

</Derivation>

## POMDPs, and what memory is approximating

When observations are not states, add an observation space and model:
$(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \Omega, O, \gamma)$ with
$O(o \mid s', a)$. Define the **belief**

$$
b_t(s) = P\big(s_t = s \;\big|\; o_{1:t},\, a_{1:t-1}\big)
$$

updated by Bayes' rule:

$$
b_{t+1}(s') \;\propto\; O(o_{t+1}\mid s', a_t) \sum_{s} P(s'\mid s, a_t)\, b_t(s)
$$

<Derivation title="Why the belief MDP is Markov, and why that is not a solution">

$b_{t+1}$ is computed from $b_t$, $a_t$, and $o_{t+1}$ alone — the whole history
enters only through $b_t$. So $b_t$ is a **sufficient statistic** for the
history, and the process over belief states satisfies the Markov property. An
optimal policy can be written as $\pi(a \mid b)$.

Two problems, both fatal for a learning agent:

1. The update requires $P$ and $O$. If you had those you would not be doing RL.
2. $b$ lives on the probability simplex over $\mathcal{S}$ — continuous and
   high-dimensional even for a small $\mathcal{S}$.

</Derivation>

<Warning>
The construction is still worth knowing, because it names what a recurrent
policy's hidden state or a transformer's context window is *trying* to be: a
sufficient statistic of the history. The difference is that it is learned from
the task loss rather than computed from a model, so nothing guarantees
sufficiency. That gap is the honest reason memory-based policies are finicky,
and saying it precisely is worth more in an interview than "we used an LSTM".
</Warning>

## Two variations worth having straight

<Steps>
<Step title="Episodic vs. continuing">
Episodic tasks terminate, so $\gamma = 1$ is legitimate and the return is a
finite sum. Continuing tasks do not, and $\gamma < 1$ (or an average-reward
formulation) is what keeps the objective well defined. Most robotics work is
episodic with $\gamma < 1$ anyway, using discounting as a variance-reduction
and horizon-shaping device rather than out of necessity.
</Step>

<Step title="Reward on $(s,a)$ vs. $(s,a,s')$">
Both appear; they are equivalent up to taking an expectation,
$R(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_{s'\sim P}[R(s,a,s')]$. Worth recognising instantly,
because papers switch between them without comment and the algebra looks
different depending on which you assumed.
</Step>
</Steps>
