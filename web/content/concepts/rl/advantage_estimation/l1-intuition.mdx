## Four sources, not one

"The policy gradient has high variance" is true and useless. The variance has
identifiable components, each with its own fix, and knowing which is which turns
a list of tricks into a diagnosis.

<Figure src="/figures/advantage-variance-sources.svg"
        alt="Four stacked bars showing the variance of the gradient weight shrinking as each technique is applied: full return, return-to-go, baseline-subtracted, and GAE. Each bar removes a differently-coloured segment."
        caption="Each technique targets a different term, which is why stacking them is not redundant. Only the last one costs bias — and per-batch normalisation is not on this chart at all, because it rescales rather than shrinks." />

<Steps>
<Step title="Rewards collected before the action">
An action at time $t$ cannot have influenced reward at time $t-5$. Including it
in the weight contributes nothing in expectation and everything to the noise.
</Step>

<Step title="The state's own value">
In a good state, every action produces a large return. That component is about
*where you were*, not *what you did*, and it swamps the part you care about.
</Step>

<Step title="Everything random after the action">
Transitions and rewards from $t$ onward are stochastic, and their noise
accumulates over the horizon. This is the term that grows with episode length.
</Step>

<Step title="The action sampling itself">
A stochastic policy samples the very actions it is trying to score. Irreducible
— you can only average it down with more samples.
</Step>
</Steps>

## The fixes, matched to their targets

<KeyIdea>
**Causality** drops the first: weight by the return *from* $t$ onward.
**A baseline** removes the second: subtract $V(s_t)$ so the weight is centred.
**Bootstrapping** truncates the third: replace the long random tail with a value
estimate. Nothing touches the fourth.

The first two are free — they change variance and leave the expectation exactly
alone. Only the third buys its reduction with bias, which is why it is the one
with a dial on it.
</KeyIdea>

<Intuition>
You are judging chess moves by the eventual result of the game. Including moves
played before this one is obviously noise. The strength of the position you
inherited is a large effect that has nothing to do with your move. The randomness
of everything your opponent does afterwards is real but distant. And your own
tendency to play differently on different days is irreducible.

An engine's positional evaluation is the baseline: it tells you how good the
position already was, so what remains is your contribution.
</Intuition>

## The one that is not a variance reduction

Almost every implementation normalises advantages per batch — subtract the batch
mean, divide by the batch standard deviation. It is usually described alongside
the others, and it is a different kind of thing.

<Warning>
Normalisation does not shrink any of the four terms. It rescales the whole
weight distribution to zero mean and unit variance, which stabilises the
*effective step size* as the reward scale drifts over training. That is a real
and valuable effect, and it is not variance reduction.

It also has a cost that is rarely stated. Centring each batch on its own mean
means a batch where everything went well and a batch where everything went badly
produce **identical** weight distributions — the information that the whole batch
was good is destroyed. Within a batch the relative ordering survives, which is
why it works; but the estimator is no longer an unbiased estimate of the policy
gradient. It is optimising relative advantage within batches, which is usually a
good trade and always worth knowing you made.
</Warning>

## Why this framing pays off

When a run is noisy and directionless, "reduce the variance" is not actionable.
The decomposition is.

<Steps>
<Step title="Is the weight the full return?">
Then most of your variance is the first two terms, and both fixes are free. This
is the largest available win and it costs nothing.
</Step>

<Step title="Is there a baseline, and is it any good?">
A critic with near-zero explained variance is not removing the second term — it
is adding noise of its own. Fix the critic before touching $\lambda$.
</Step>

<Step title="Are episodes long?">
Then the third term dominates and $\lambda$ is your dial. But lowering it leans
harder on the critic, so it is only the right move if the critic has earned it.
</Step>

<Step title="Is the run scale-drifting rather than noisy?">
That is normalisation, not variance. Different symptom, different fix.
</Step>
</Steps>
