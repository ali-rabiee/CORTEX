## The accounting, done properly, before deep RL

<PaperCard refKey="greensmith2004variance" />

The formal treatment of baselines as **additive control variates** — a standard
Monte Carlo variance-reduction technique — with actual bounds on the resulting
variance. It predates deep RL entirely, which is why the accounting is cleaner
than most of what came after: there was no implementation noise to hide in.

<KeyIdea>
The control-variate framing is worth internalising because it generalises.
Subtract from your estimator anything with known expectation and correlated with
it, and you reduce variance without changing the expectation. The baseline is
that construction with $\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi\cdot b(s)] = 0$ supplying the
"known expectation" — and once you see it that way, the constraint that $b$ must
not depend on the action stops being a technicality and becomes the entire
reason it works.
</KeyIdea>

## One dial for the whole family

<PaperCard refKey="schulman2015gae" />

The $n$-step advantage estimators form a family from high-bias to high-variance,
and GAE's contribution is that the exponentially weighted average over all of
them collapses into a single backward recursion — cheaper than computing any
individual member naively.

Its place in this page's accounting is specific: it is the only one of the
standard techniques that trades **bias** for its variance reduction, which is why
it is the only one with a knob. Causality and baselines are free substitutions
you should always make; $\lambda$ is a decision.

## Off-policy: bounding the correction's own variance

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="munos2016retrace" />
<PaperCard refKey="espeholt2018impala" />
</PaperGrid>

Once data comes from a different policy, unbiasedness requires importance
weights, and a product of unbounded ratios over a trajectory has variance
exponential in its length. Both papers are, structurally, GAE with corrections
folded in and clipped.

<Steps>
<Step title="Retrace: safe for arbitrary behaviour policies">
Truncated per-step ratios give a return estimator that is valid however far the
behaviour policy is from the target, and efficient when they are close — the
"safe and efficient" of the title is exactly that pair of properties.
</Step>

<Step title="V-trace: two thresholds for two different things">
$\bar c$ bounds the variance of the product along the trace; $\bar\rho$ bounds
which policy's value function the estimator converges to. Separate knobs because
they trade separate things, and collapsing them would force a variance bound and
a fixed-point shift to move together.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
Recognising the shared shape — backward recursion over discounted TD residuals,
with something clipped — means you can place a new off-policy estimator on
sight. The two questions are what it clips and what that clipping costs, and
they have always been the same two questions.
</KeyIdea>

## The cautionary tale

<PaperCard refKey="tucker2018mirage" />

Action-dependent baselines are a natural idea: if a state-dependent baseline
helps, surely a state-*and*-action-dependent one helps more. It is unbiased
provided you add a correction term restoring what the dependence broke, several
methods reported gains, and a careful reanalysis attributed those gains to
implementation differences and bugs rather than to the mechanism.

<Warning>
The specific failure is worth carrying. The correction term has to be
**estimated**, and estimating it introduces variance of its own — potentially
more than the baseline removed. So a variance-reduction scheme requiring an extra
estimated quantity can be net negative, and only a controlled comparison at
matched implementations reveals which.

The generalisable lesson is about attribution rather than about baselines. When a
method reports an improvement, the question is whether the improvement came from
the claimed mechanism — and the way to find out is to hold everything else
constant, which is exactly what the original papers had not done.
</Warning>

## Settling it empirically

<PaperCard refKey="andrychowicz2021matters" />

Thousands of controlled runs over the choices that actually move on-policy RL,
including advantage normalisation and $\lambda$. Necessary because these effects
are comparable in size to seed-to-seed variation, so a handful of runs cannot
distinguish them — and because the choices *interact*, so studying one at a time
in an uncontrolled codebase produces the contradictory folklore that preceded it.

<Steps>
<Step title="What the theory gives you">
Which techniques are free and which cost bias; what each one is targeting; why
the constraint on baselines is what it is. That is enough to diagnose a problem
and to reject a proposal that cannot work.
</Step>

<Step title="What only measurement gives you">
Where $\lambda$ should sit for your task, whether normalisation helps in your
reward regime, and how the choices interact. The analysis narrows the search; it
does not replace it.
</Step>
</Steps>
