## The estimator and its one free choice

Every policy-gradient method computes

$$
\hat g = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t^i \mid s_t^i)\; \Psi_t^i
$$

and differs only in $\Psi_t$. The menu, all unbiased for the same gradient except
where noted:

| $\Psi_t$ | what it is | bias |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $G(\tau)$ | full trajectory return | none |
| $G_t$ | return-to-go | none |
| $G_t - b(s_t)$ | baselined return | none, for any $b$ not depending on $a_t$ |
| $\hat A^{(n)}_t$ | $n$-step advantage | yes, via the bootstrap |
| $\hat A^{\text{GAE}}_t$ | exponentially averaged | yes, for $\lambda < 1$ |

<KeyIdea>
Three of the five are free. Only the bootstrapped rows trade bias for variance,
which is why $\lambda$ is the only one of these choices that has a dial — the
others are strictly-better substitutions you should always make.
</KeyIdea>

## Causality, and why it costs nothing

<Derivation title="Past rewards contribute zero in expectation">

Expand $G(\tau) = \sum_{t'} \gamma^{t'} r_{t'}$ and take a cross term with
$t' < t$:

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\; r_{t'}\big]
$$

Condition on the history up to time $t$. Then $r_{t'}$ is already determined and
comes out of the inner expectation as a constant:

$$
= \mathbb{E}\Big[r_{t'}\;\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{a_t\sim\pi_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\big]}_{=\;\nabla_\theta\sum_a \pi_\theta(a\mid s_t)\;=\;\nabla_\theta 1\;=\;0}\Big] = 0
$$

So each past reward contributes nothing to the gradient and a full share to the
variance. Dropping them is free.

</Derivation>

## The baseline as a control variate

<Derivation title="The variance-minimising baseline, and why we use $V^\pi$ instead">

Write $u = \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ and consider the single-term
estimator $u(G - b)$. Its expectation does not depend on $b$ (previous
derivation), so minimising variance is minimising the second moment:

$$
\mathcal{V}(b) = \mathbb{E}\big[\|u\|^2 (G-b)^2\big]
$$

Differentiate and set to zero:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{V}}{\mathrm{d}b} = -2\,\mathbb{E}\big[\|u\|^2 (G - b)\big] = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{\;b^* = \frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\|u\|^2\,G\big]}{\mathbb{E}\big[\|u\|^2\big]}\;}
$$

**Read it.** The optimal baseline is the return weighted by *how strongly each
sample moves the gradient* — states where the score function is large count more.

**Why $V^\pi(s)$ instead.** Dropping the $\|u\|^2$ weighting gives
$b = \mathbb{E}[G\mid s] = V^\pi(s)$: not optimal, and estimable with a network
from data you already have. The optimal one requires estimating a score-weighted
expectation, which is a harder problem for a small gain.

**The variance reduction achieved.** Writing $A = G - V$, the second moment falls
from $\mathbb{E}[\|u\|^2 G^2]$ to $\mathbb{E}[\|u\|^2 A^2]$. When the state value
dominates the advantage — a good state where every action returns a lot — that
ratio is enormous, which is why the baseline is the single largest free win.

</Derivation>

<Warning>
The proof requires $b$ to be **independent of the action being scored**. That is
what lets it factor out of $\sum_a \pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ and multiply
$\nabla_\theta 1 = 0$. An action-dependent baseline breaks this and leaves a
residual term that must be corrected explicitly — and when the correction is
included and the comparison is run carefully, the reported gains from
action-dependent baselines have been traced to implementation differences rather
than to the baseline itself.
</Warning>

## Per-batch normalisation: a different animal

The standard implementation computes, over a batch of size $n$,

$$
\tilde A_i = \frac{\hat A_i - \hat\mu}{\hat\sigma + \epsilon},
\qquad
\hat\mu = \frac1n\sum_j \hat A_j,
\qquad
\hat\sigma^2 = \frac1n\sum_j (\hat A_j - \hat\mu)^2
$$

<Derivation title="Why this is biased, and what it actually does">

**The bias.** The baseline proof needs the subtracted quantity to be independent
of the sample it is subtracted from. Here $\hat\mu$ is computed *from* the batch
and includes $\hat A_i$ itself, so

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[u_i(\hat A_i - \hat\mu)\big] \ne \mathbb{E}\big[u_i \hat A_i\big]
$$

because $u_i$ and $\hat\mu$ are correlated through $\hat A_i$. The contribution of
sample $i$ to $\hat\mu$ is $\hat A_i/n$, so the bias is $O(1/n)$ — small at
realistic batch sizes, and not zero. Dividing by $\hat\sigma$ adds a second,
separate source of the same kind.

**What it accomplishes.** The advantage scale drifts over training as the policy
improves and returns grow. With a fixed learning rate, the effective step size
drifts with it. Normalisation pins the weight distribution to zero mean and unit
variance, so the effective step size is stable — which is a *step-size* fix, not
a variance reduction.

**What it destroys.** Consider two batches, one where every advantage is about
$+2$ and one where every advantage is about $-2$. After centring, both have mean
zero; after scaling by their own standard deviations, both have the same spread.
The two batches produce **identical** weight distributions.

The information that one batch was uniformly good and the other uniformly bad is
gone. Within a batch, relative ordering survives — which is why it works at all —
but you are now optimising *relative advantage within batches* rather than the
policy gradient.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
Usually a good trade, and the reason to know you made it is diagnostic: if your
environment has episodes that are wholly good or wholly bad — sparse success,
early termination — then per-batch normalisation is discarding your main signal.
That failure looks like a policy that will not improve, and no amount of tuning
$\lambda$ addresses it.
</KeyIdea>

## Off-policy: the correction's own variance

When the data comes from a behaviour policy $\mu \neq \pi$, unbiasedness needs
importance weights, and the naive product over a trajectory has variance
exponential in the horizon.

V-trace repairs this with **two separate clipping thresholds**:

$$
\rho_t = \min\!\Big(\bar\rho,\ \tfrac{\pi(a_t\mid s_t)}{\mu(a_t\mid s_t)}\Big),
\qquad
c_t = \min\!\Big(\bar c,\ \tfrac{\pi(a_t\mid s_t)}{\mu(a_t\mid s_t)}\Big)
$$

$$
v_t = V(s_t) + \sum_{k \ge t}\gamma^{k-t}
\Big(\textstyle\prod_{i=t}^{k-1} c_i\Big)\,\rho_k\,\delta_k
$$

<Steps>
<Step title="$\bar c$ bounds the variance">
The $c_i$ appear in a *product* along the trace, and unbounded products explode.
Clipping them caps how much a single unlikely action can dominate the estimate.
</Step>

<Step title="$\bar\rho$ bounds the fixed point">
The leading $\rho_k$ determines which policy's value function the estimator
converges to. With $\bar\rho = \infty$ it is the target policy's; clipping moves
the fixed point toward the behaviour policy's.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Two thresholds because they trade different things. Collapsing them into one
forces you to accept a variance bound and a fixed-point shift together, when you
may want a tight variance bound and a fixed point close to the target policy. It
is the same shape of design as GAE's $\lambda$ — a knob whose two ends are
different failure modes — and recognising that shape is more useful than
memorising either formula.
</Warning>
