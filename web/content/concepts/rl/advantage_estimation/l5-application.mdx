interview:
  answer: >-
    I'd start by refusing the usual framing, because "policy gradients have high
    variance" is true and not actionable. The variance has four separable
    sources and each standard technique removes a different one. First, rewards
    collected before the action: an action at time t can't have influenced a
    reward at t minus five, so in expectation that term contributes exactly zero
    and it contributes a full share to the noise. Weighting by return-to-go
    instead of the full episode return drops it, and costs nothing. Second, the
    state's own value. In a good state every action produces a large return, and
    that component is about where you were rather than what you did — it swamps
    the part you care about. Subtracting a baseline removes it, and that's also
    free, because the expected score function times any state-dependent quantity
    is zero. Third, the randomness of everything after the action — transitions
    and rewards accumulating over the horizon. That's what bootstrapping
    truncates, and it's the only one of the three that buys its reduction with
    bias, which is exactly why lambda is the only one of these choices that has
    a dial. Fourth, the action sampling itself, which is irreducible for a
    stochastic policy. The thing I'd add, because it usually gets lumped in with
    the others, is that per-batch advantage normalisation isn't a variance
    reduction at all. It doesn't shrink any of those four terms — it rescales
    the whole distribution so the effective step size stays stable as the reward
    scale drifts. That's valuable and it's a different problem. It's also mildly
    biased, because you're subtracting a batch mean computed from the same
    samples, so it's correlated with them and the baseline proof doesn't apply.
    And it has a cost people rarely state: an all-good batch and an all-bad
    batch produce identical weights after centring and rescaling, so the
    information that the whole batch went well is destroyed. In sparse-reward or
    early-termination environments whole batches are routinely one-signed, and
    that's your main signal. So practically, the first thing I'd measure isn't
    lambda — it's a gradient signal-to-noise ratio, the norm of the mean
    gradient over minibatches against the mean of the norms, because that says
    whether variance is the bottleneck at all. Then explained variance, because
    a critic near zero isn't removing the state-value term, it's adding noise,
    and lowering lambda then leans harder on exactly the thing that's failing.
  followUps:
    - q: "How would you actually attribute the variance rather than guess?"
      a: >-
        Recompute the same stored batch's gradient under each weighting — full
        return, return-to-go, baselined, GAE — and compare the signal-to-noise
        across minibatches. Two training runs differ in every subsequent step,
        so that comparison confounds the estimator with the whole divergent
        trajectory and needs many seeds to say anything; recomputing on a fixed
        batch changes exactly one thing and costs a forward-backward pass. It's
        the same pairing logic as evaluating two policies on identical scenes.
        The pattern I'd expect is a big jump from full return to return-to-go,
        a bigger one to baselined, and a smaller one to GAE — and if the
        baseline row doesn't improve things, that's diagnostic on its own: the
        critic isn't subtracting the state value, it's subtracting noise.
    - q: "Why is the optimal baseline not exactly the value function?"
      a: >-
        If you differentiate the variance of the score function times the
        centred return with respect to the baseline and set it to zero, you get
        the return weighted by the squared norm of the score function, over the
        expected squared norm — so states where the gradient is more sensitive
        count more. The value function drops that weighting. It's a small loss
        in optimality and an enormous simplification, because V is a quantity
        you can fit with a network from data you already have, and the optimal
        baseline requires estimating a score-weighted expectation. It's worth
        knowing because it explains why the baseline is a choice rather than a
        derivation — and why alternatives keep getting proposed, including
        action-dependent ones, which turned out not to survive controlled
        comparison.
    - q: "What happened with action-dependent baselines?"
      a: >-
        It's a natural idea — if a state-dependent baseline helps, a state-and-
        action-dependent one should help more — and it's unbiased provided you
        add a correction term restoring what the action dependence broke.
        Several methods reported gains and a careful reanalysis attributed them
        to implementation differences and bugs rather than to the mechanism. The
        specific failure worth carrying is that the correction has to be
        *estimated*, and estimating it introduces variance of its own,
        potentially more than the baseline removed. So a variance-reduction
        scheme that needs an extra estimated quantity can be net negative. The
        general lesson is about attribution: when something reports an
        improvement, the question is whether it came from the claimed mechanism,
        and the only way to find out is to hold everything else constant.
    - q: "When would you not normalise advantages?"
      a: >-
        When whole batches are routinely one-signed, which happens with sparse
        rewards or aggressive early termination — there, per-batch centring is
        discarding the main signal, and the symptom is a policy that simply
        won't improve while every other diagnostic looks fine. I'd catch it by
        logging the raw advantage mean and the fraction positive before
        normalisation, since after normalisation that information is gone by
        construction. The fix isn't to drop normalisation entirely, because the
        step-size drift it fixes is real — it's to use a running normaliser with
        statistics accumulated across batches, which stabilises the scale
        without destroying the across-batch level. Slower to adapt, and it keeps
        the signal.

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Noisy and directionless → measure gradient SNR first">
The norm of the mean minibatch gradient over the mean of the norms. Near
$1/\sqrt{k}$ means the minibatch gradients are cancelling and variance really is
your problem; near 1 means look elsewhere.
</Step>

<Step title="SNR low and explained variance low → fix the critic, do not touch $\lambda$">
The baseline is not removing the state-value term. Lowering $\lambda$ leans
harder on the failing component.
</Step>

<Step title="Learning stalled, batches one-signed → normalisation is eating the signal">
Log the raw advantage mean and fraction positive before normalisation. Switch to
a running normaliser.
</Step>

<Step title="Effective step size drifting over training → that is normalisation's job">
Not variance. Different symptom, different fix, and it is why normalisation is
worth having despite its costs.
</Step>

<Step title="Weight includes rewards before the action → free win available">
Return-to-go instead of the full return. No bias, immediate variance reduction,
and it still appears in reimplementations.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The move that distinguishes this answer is refusing the aggregate. Everyone says
policy gradients are high-variance; almost nobody separates the sources, and the
separation is what makes the answer actionable — it tells you which fixes are
free, which one costs bias, and which "fix" is not addressing variance at all.
Adding the point that per-batch normalisation is mildly biased and destroys
across-batch level information is the detail that reads as having debugged one of
these rather than configured one.
</Warning>
