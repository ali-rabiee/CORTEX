## Three objects, one definition

All three answer "how much reward do I expect from here?", differing only in
what is held fixed.

$$
V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\big[G_t \mid s_t = s\big]
\qquad
Q^\pi(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\big[G_t \mid s_t = s,\, a_t = a\big]
$$

$V$ assumes you behave as $\pi$ from now on. $Q$ lets you take one specified
action first, then behave as $\pi$. The advantage is their difference:

$$
A^\pi(s,a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s)
$$

<Intuition>
$V$ is "how good is this chess position for me". $Q$ is "how good is this
position if I play knight-to-f3". The advantage is "is knight-to-f3 better or
worse than my usual play here?"

Only the third is directly actionable. The first two are dominated by how good
the position is, which is not something this move can take credit for.
</Intuition>

## The identity that makes the advantage useful

$V^\pi$ is not the best $Q$ — it is the **average** $Q$, weighted by how often
$\pi$ actually takes each action:

$$
V^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a\mid s)\, Q^\pi(s,a)
$$

<KeyIdea>
Because $V^\pi$ is the mean, $\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[A^\pi(s,a)] = 0$ exactly.
That zero-centring is the whole reason the advantage is the right weight for a
policy gradient: it removes an arbitrary per-state offset, so a positive weight
genuinely means "more of this than usual" instead of "this state was good".
</KeyIdea>

## When you need which

<Steps>
<Step title="$Q$, when you must act without a model">
$\arg\max_a Q(s,a)$ requires nothing but the current state. That is why $Q$
is the object of choice for model-free value methods. The cost: its input space
is $|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{A}|$, so it is a harder function to fit than
$V$, and the argmax must be tractable.
</Step>

<Step title="$V$, when a policy already exists to be criticised">
In actor-critic the actor chooses; the critic only has to score states. $V$ is
a smaller, easier function than $Q$, and it is all a baseline needs. This is why
PPO, A2C, and GAE are built on $V$ and never learn $Q$ at all.
</Step>

<Step title="$A$, whenever you are updating a policy">
Both alternatives are worse. Raw returns make every action in a good state look
good; $Q$ has the same problem with a smaller constant. The advantage is the
only one of the three whose sign means what you want it to mean.
</Step>
</Steps>

## The one that gets people

<Warning>
Every value function is defined **relative to a policy**, and $V^\pi$ and $V^*$
are different objects obeying different equations. $V^\pi$ satisfies a linear
Bellman equation you can solve by matrix inversion; $V^*$ satisfies one with a
max in it and cannot be solved directly. Policy evaluation computes the first,
value iteration computes the second. When a paper writes a bare $V(s)$, work out
which one it means before you read the next line — almost all confusion in this
area starts there.
</Warning>

There is a second habit worth forming early: distrust a critic before you
distrust a policy. A value function trained concurrently on a moving target is
the least reliable component of most RL systems, and "explained variance" — how
much of the return's variance the critic actually accounts for — is the one
number that tells you whether anything downstream of it can be trusted. Level 3
covers measuring it.
