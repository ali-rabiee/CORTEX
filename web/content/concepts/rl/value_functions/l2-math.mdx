## Four objects and the identities between them

$$
V^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a\mid s)\,Q^\pi(s,a)
\qquad
Q^\pi(s,a) = \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)\big[R(s,a,s') + \gamma V^\pi(s')\big]
$$

The pair alternates between two kinds of averaging: over actions using $\pi$,
and over next states using $P$. Substituting each into the other gives the two
Bellman expectation equations.

For the optimal policy the first identity collapses:

$$
V^*(s) = \max_a Q^*(s,a),
\qquad
Q^*(s,a) = \sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\Big[R + \gamma\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a')\Big]
$$

<KeyIdea>
The collapse is not a new definition. It is the same $\pi$-weighted mean applied
to a policy that assigns zero probability to any non-maximising action — and a
finite MDP always admits such a deterministic optimal policy. Maximum-entropy RL
is precisely the choice to give this up: add an entropy term and the optimal
policy becomes stochastic, and the max becomes a log-sum-exp.
</KeyIdea>

## The advantage is zero-mean by construction

$$
\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}\big[A^\pi(s,a)\big]
= \sum_a \pi(a\mid s)\big[Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s)\big]
= V^\pi(s) - V^\pi(s) = 0
$$

using $\sum_a \pi(a\mid s) = 1$. This is an identity for the *true* $A^\pi$; an
estimated $\hat A$ from a fitted critic will not be exactly zero-mean, which is
one reason per-batch advantage normalisation is standard rather than optional.

## Why an advantage is actionable

Knowing an action is above average is only useful if acting on it improves the
policy. That is a theorem.

<Derivation title="The policy improvement theorem">

**Hypothesis.** Let $\pi$ and $\pi'$ be policies with

$$
Q^\pi\big(s, \pi'(s)\big) \;\ge\; V^\pi(s) \qquad \text{for all } s
$$

That is: at every state, taking $\pi'$'s action and then reverting to $\pi$ is
at least as good as following $\pi$ throughout. Equivalently
$A^\pi(s,\pi'(s)) \ge 0$.

**Claim.** $V^{\pi'}(s) \ge V^\pi(s)$ for all $s$.

**Proof by unrolling.** Start from the hypothesis and expand $Q^\pi$ by its
definition:

$$
V^\pi(s) \;\le\; Q^\pi(s,\pi'(s))
= \mathbb{E}_{\pi'}\big[r_t + \gamma V^\pi(s_{t+1}) \mid s_t = s\big]
$$

The expectation is over $\pi'$ choosing the action at time $t$. Now apply the
hypothesis again, this time at $s_{t+1}$, replacing $V^\pi(s_{t+1})$ with
$Q^\pi(s_{t+1}, \pi'(s_{t+1}))$ and expanding once more:

$$
\le\; \mathbb{E}_{\pi'}\big[r_t + \gamma r_{t+1} + \gamma^2 V^\pi(s_{t+2}) \mid s_t = s\big]
$$

Each application converts one more timestep from "behave as $\pi$" to "behave as
$\pi'$". Repeating indefinitely:

$$
\le\; \mathbb{E}_{\pi'}\big[r_t + \gamma r_{t+1} + \gamma^2 r_{t+2} + \cdots \mid s_t = s\big]
= V^{\pi'}(s)
$$

**The assumptions doing the work.** Bounded rewards and $\gamma < 1$, so the
trailing $\gamma^k V^\pi(s_{t+k})$ term vanishes and the limit is the full
return. Notice what is *not* assumed: nothing about $\pi'$ being greedy,
deterministic, or close to $\pi$.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
A **local** condition — at each state separately, the new action is no worse
than the old policy's average — is bootstrapped into a **global** guarantee
about full returns. This is the theorem that licenses "estimate the advantage,
then move probability toward positive-advantage actions", and therefore the
theoretical footing under policy iteration and every actor-critic method.
</KeyIdea>

## The identifiability problem in $Q = V + A$

Dueling architectures split the network into a value stream and an advantage
stream and add them. The loss only ever sees the sum, which creates a problem:

$$
Q(s,a) = V(s) + A(s,a) = \big(V(s) + c\big) + \big(A(s,a) - c\big)
$$

for any $c$. The decomposition is unidentifiable — nothing forces the $V$ head
to learn a value or the $A$ head to be zero-mean, and in practice they drift
apart while $Q$ stays fine.

<Warning>
The fix is to impose the missing constraint in the architecture:

$$
Q(s,a) = V(s) + \Big(A(s,a) - \tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\textstyle\sum_{a'} A(s,a')\Big)
$$

which restores $\mathbb{E}[A] = 0$, albeit under a uniform rather than
policy-weighted average. Subtracting $\max_{a'}A(s,a')$ is the alternative and
is less stable, because the reference point jumps discontinuously whenever the
argmax changes. This is a good miniature lesson: when a decomposition has a free
gauge, the loss will not pick one for you.
</Warning>

## What breaks when the critic is wrong

<Steps>
<Step title="Scale">
Values live on a scale of roughly $R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$, so a reward in the
hundreds at $\gamma = 0.99$ gives targets in the tens of thousands, and a
network initialised near zero spends a long time just reaching the right order
of magnitude. Return normalisation — or adaptive schemes like PopArt that
rescale the output layer while preserving the function — is what stops the
scale from dominating the learning problem.
</Step>

<Step title="Non-stationarity">
The critic is fitted to a target that moves as the policy improves, so it is
solving a sequence of regression problems rather than one. This is not a bug to
remove; it is what distinguishes value learning from supervised learning, and it
is why value networks empirically lose effective capacity over long training
runs.
</Step>

<Step title="Explained variance, not loss">
The value loss is uninterpretable — it scales with return magnitude, which
changes as the policy improves. $1 - \operatorname{Var}(G - V)/\operatorname{Var}(G)$
is the right diagnostic: near 1 means the critic explains the returns, near 0
means the advantages downstream of it are noise.
</Step>
</Steps>
