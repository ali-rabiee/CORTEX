## Trial, error, and a nudge

Value-based methods learn how good things are, then act greedily. Policy
gradients skip the middleman: adjust the policy's parameters so that good
behaviour becomes more likely.

The loop is almost embarrassingly simple.

<Steps>
<Step title="Run the current policy and collect trajectories">
Sample $N$ episodes. Record every state, action, and reward. Nothing clever.
</Step>

<Step title="Score each trajectory by its return">
One number per trajectory: how much reward it collected.
</Step>

<Step title="Push up the good, push down the bad">
Increase $\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$ for actions in high-return
trajectories, decrease it for low-return ones, in proportion to the return.
</Step>

<Step title="Throw the data away and repeat">
The policy has changed, so the trajectories no longer come from it. This is the
on-policy constraint, and it is what makes the method expensive.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Intuition>
You are training a dog with no vocabulary in common. You cannot explain what you
want. You can only let it try things and reward what you liked, and it adjusts
the odds of doing that again.

The awkward part is that you rewarded a whole *sequence*. The dog did nine
things and got one treat, and now every one of the nine gets a little more
likely — including the three that were actively unhelpful.
</Intuition>

## Why the dynamics disappear

Here is the fact that makes the method possible, and it is worth being able to
state without the algebra.

The probability of a trajectory is the policy's choices multiplied by the
world's responses. When you take the log, that becomes a sum. When you
differentiate with respect to the policy's parameters, every term involving the
world's responses is a constant and vanishes.

<KeyIdea>
You are left with a gradient that mentions only $\log \pi_\theta$ and the
observed returns. **You never need to know, estimate, or invert the transition
dynamics** — only to sample from them, which is what running the robot does for
free. This is the whole reason policy gradients are model-free.
</KeyIdea>

## The variance problem

One scalar return has to explain the goodness of every action in an episode.
That is a brutal credit assignment problem, and it shows up as gradient
estimates so noisy that naive REINFORCE needs thousands of episodes for tasks
that look trivial.

Two fixes are close to free.

**Causality.** An action at time $t$ cannot have influenced rewards collected
before $t$. Weight each action by the return *from that point on* rather than by
the whole episode's return, and you have removed a chunk of pure noise without
introducing any bias.

**A baseline.** Subtract a state-dependent number $b(s)$ from the weight.

<Figure src="/figures/pg-baseline-variance.svg"
        alt="Left: three trajectories with returns 95, 100, 105, all pushed up by large positive arrows. Right: the same trajectories weighted by return minus a baseline of 100, giving one downward arrow, one zero, one upward arrow."
        caption="Without a baseline every sampled action is reinforced and the signal is a small difference between large numbers. Centring the weights turns 'slightly less good' into an actual push in the other direction." />

<Warning>
Subtracting a baseline that depends only on the state leaves the gradient
**unbiased** — the proof is two lines and is in Level 2. This surprises people,
because it looks like you are changing what you optimise. You are not; you are
changing the variance of an estimator of the same quantity. Subtracting anything
that depends on the *action* would bias it, which is exactly the mistake to
avoid.
</Warning>

The natural choice of baseline is the value function $V(s)$, which makes the
weight $G_t - V(s_t)$ — an estimate of the advantage. That single substitution
turns REINFORCE into actor-critic, and every modern method from A2C to PPO to
SAC is a descendant.
