recall:
  prompt: "Give your interview answer on policy gradients. Why are they model-free, and what are the two things you always fix first?"
  keyPoints:
    - "Lead with the estimator and the cancellation: the dynamics terms differentiate to zero, which is exactly why no model is needed."
    - "Name the variance problem as the method's defining weakness, then the two free fixes — return-to-go from causality, and a state-dependent baseline that is provably unbiased."
    - "State the on-policy constraint as a cost: one batch of environment interaction buys one update, and everything from PPO to V-trace exists to relax that safely."
    - "Know the collapse mode: a high-variance step makes the policy near-deterministic, log-prob gradients vanish for the abandoned actions, and exploration ends permanently. Entropy bonus, smaller steps, better baseline."
interview:
  answer: >-
    Policy gradients optimise the policy's parameters directly against expected
    return, instead of learning a value function and acting greedily on it. The
    derivation is the log-derivative trick: you can't differentiate through an
    expectation whose distribution depends on theta, but you can rewrite grad p
    as p times grad log p, which turns the gradient back into an expectation
    you can sample. Then the key structural fact — the trajectory probability
    factorises into the policy's choices and the environment's transitions, the
    log makes that a sum, and the transition terms don't depend on theta so
    they differentiate to zero. What's left mentions only grad log pi and the
    observed return. That's why the method is model-free: you never estimate or
    invert the dynamics, you only sample from them, which is what running the
    robot does anyway. The practical character of the method is that it's
    unbiased and extremely high variance. One scalar return has to assign
    credit across every action in an episode. Two fixes are free in the sense
    of costing no bias. Causality: an action can't have affected rewards that
    came before it, so weight by return-to-go instead of the full episode
    return. And a baseline: subtracting any function of the state leaves the
    gradient unbiased, because the expected score function is the gradient of
    the constant one, which is zero. It must not depend on the action or that
    proof fails. Use the value function as the baseline and the weight becomes
    the advantage — that's actor-critic, and GAE is the lambda dial between
    high-bias and high-variance advantage estimates. The other cost is that
    it's on-policy. The expectation is taken under the current policy, so one
    gradient step invalidates the batch. Importance sampling fixes that in
    principle, but a product of per-step ratios has variance exponential in the
    horizon — which is why PPO clips the per-step ratio, TRPO bounds a KL, and
    V-trace truncates. They're all answering the same question: how far can I
    move before my data stops being usable?
  followUps:
    - q: "Your policy learns for a while, then collapses to always taking one action and never recovers. What happened?"
      a: >-
        A high-variance update pushed the policy near-deterministic, and once
        the probability of the other actions is tiny, two things kill you: you
        stop sampling them so you get no gradient information about them, and
        the log-probability gradients for them are effectively gone. It's an
        absorbing state of the optimisation. I'd attack it from three sides —
        an entropy bonus to put a floor under the policy's stochasticity, a
        smaller step size or a trust region so no single update can make that
        jump, and a better baseline so the gradient variance that produced the
        bad step is lower in the first place. If it's a Gaussian policy on
        continuous actions, I'd also check whether the learned log-std is
        collapsing and consider bounding it.
    - q: "How do you decide between a policy gradient method and something off-policy like SAC?"
      a: >-
        Mostly by how expensive a sample is. On-policy methods throw away every
        batch after one update, so they need a lot of interaction — which is
        fine in a fast parallel simulator and painful on real hardware. SAC and
        other off-policy methods reuse a replay buffer many times and are
        typically an order of magnitude more sample-efficient, at the cost of
        being more delicate: they bootstrap, they're off-policy, and they use
        function approximation, so they're in deadly-triad territory and need
        target networks and twin critics to stay stable. My default is PPO in
        simulation where I can throw parallel environments at it, and SAC when
        samples are the binding constraint.
    - q: "Is the policy gradient theorem still relevant now that most RL is on language models?"
      a: >-
        It's more central than it was, just under different names. RLHF-style
        fine-tuning is a policy gradient method where the policy is the
        language model, the action is a token or a completion, and the reward
        comes from a learned preference model. Everything from Level 2 carries
        over unchanged — you still need a baseline or the variance is
        unusable, and you still need to bound how far the policy moves, which
        is why the objectives in that literature look like PPO with a KL
        penalty back to the reference model. The variant that's become common
        drops the learned value baseline and uses the mean return over a group
        of sampled completions instead, which is exactly a baseline in the
        sense of Level 2 — state-dependent, action-independent, and unbiased
        for the same reason.
    - q: "What would you try first if training is just slow rather than unstable?"
      a: >-
        Check the advantage estimator before touching anything else. If it's
        raw returns-to-go with no baseline, adding a critic is the single
        largest win available. If there's a critic, I'd look at whether the
        advantages are normalised per batch, since unnormalised advantages
        interact badly with a fixed learning rate. Then GAE lambda, which is
        the actual bias-variance dial — 0.95 is the standard starting point.
        After that it's parallel environments, because on-policy methods scale
        almost linearly in batch throughput and that's usually cheaper than
        algorithmic cleverness. Bigger networks come last; they rarely fix a
        variance problem.
  traps:
    - "Saying policy gradients are model-free 'because they're sampling-based'. The precise reason is that the dynamics terms in $\\log p_\\theta(\\tau)$ don't depend on $\\theta$ and differentiate to zero — a structural cancellation, not a property of Monte Carlo."
    - "Claiming a baseline reduces bias. It reduces *variance* and leaves the gradient unbiased; being able to give the two-line proof separates people who derived it from people who read it."
    - "Forgetting that the baseline must not depend on the action. That constraint is exactly what makes the proof work, and interviewers probe it."
    - "Presenting the on-policy constraint as a minor inefficiency. It is the defining cost of the method and the reason PPO, TRPO, and V-trace all exist."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Learns then collapses to one action → variance-driven determinism">
A large update made the policy near-deterministic; the abandoned actions are no
longer sampled and their log-prob gradients have vanished. Entropy bonus, trust
region or smaller steps, better baseline. Check whether a Gaussian policy's
log-std is collapsing.
</Step>

<Step title="Very slow but stable → the advantage estimator">
Raw returns-to-go with no baseline is the most common cause and the largest
single win to fix. Then per-batch advantage normalisation, then GAE $\lambda$.
</Step>

<Step title="Learns in simulation, unusable sample count on hardware → on-policy cost">
Not a bug. Every batch buys one update by construction. Either parallelise the
simulator or move to an off-policy method and accept the stability trade.
</Step>

<Step title="Loss goes down, returns do not move → you are reading the wrong number">
The policy-gradient 'loss' is a surrogate whose value is meaningless; only its
gradient matters. Watch returns, entropy, and explained variance of the critic
instead.
</Step>

<Step title="Critic loss falls, policy does not improve → check the detach">
If gradients flow through the advantage into the critic, you are optimising a
different objective entirely. This is the single most common implementation bug
in the method.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The line that distinguishes this answer is the cancellation. Almost everyone can
say policy gradients are model-free; very few can say *why* — that the log of a
factorised trajectory probability separates into policy terms and transition
terms, and only the former depend on $\theta$. Say that, then give the two-line
baseline proof, and you have demonstrated you derived the method rather than
memorised its shape.
</Warning>
