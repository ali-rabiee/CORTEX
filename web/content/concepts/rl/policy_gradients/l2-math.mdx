## The objective and the trick

Maximise the expected return over trajectories drawn from the policy:

$$
J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_\theta}\big[R(\tau)\big],
\qquad
p_\theta(\tau) = \rho(s_0)\prod_{t} \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\, P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t, a_t)
$$

The difficulty is that $\theta$ appears in the *distribution*, not in the
integrand. You cannot differentiate under the expectation directly.

<Derivation title="The score-function estimator, and where the dynamics go">

**Step 1 — write the expectation as an integral and differentiate.**

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \nabla_\theta \int p_\theta(\tau) R(\tau)\, d\tau
= \int \nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)\, R(\tau)\, d\tau
$$

$R(\tau)$ does not depend on $\theta$, so it stays put as a coefficient.

**Step 2 — the log-derivative identity.** Since
$\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta = \nabla_\theta p_\theta / p_\theta$, we have
$\nabla_\theta p_\theta = p_\theta \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta$. Substituting
turns the integral back into an expectation — which is the entire point,
because expectations can be sampled:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}
\big[\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)\, R(\tau)\big]
$$

**Step 3 — expand the log of the factorised trajectory probability.**

$$
\log p_\theta(\tau) = \log\rho(s_0)
+ \sum_t \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)
+ \sum_t \log P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t, a_t)
$$

**Step 4 — differentiate, and watch the world disappear.** The initial-state
distribution and every transition term are functions of the environment, not of
$\theta$:

$$
\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau) = \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)
$$

giving

$$
\boxed{\;\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}
\Big[\Big(\textstyle\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\Big) R(\tau)\Big]\;}
$$

**No assumption of determinism, linearity, or differentiability of the
dynamics** was used — only that the trajectory distribution factorises and that
you can sample from it.

</Derivation>

## Two variance reductions that cost no bias

<Derivation title="Causality: rewards before $t$ carry no information about $a_t$">

Expand $R(\tau) = \sum_{t'} r_{t'}$ and consider a cross term with $t' < t$:

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\; r_{t'}\big]
$$

Condition on everything up to time $t$. Then $r_{t'}$ is already determined and
comes out of the inner expectation as a constant, leaving

$$
\mathbb{E}\Big[r_{t'}\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{a_t\sim\pi_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\big]}_{=\;0}\Big] = 0
$$

The inner expectation vanishes by the same identity used below. So past rewards
contribute nothing in expectation and only add variance. Drop them:

$$
\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)
\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{t'\ge t}\gamma^{t'-t} r_{t'}}_{G_t,\ \text{return-to-go}}\Big]
$$

</Derivation>

<Derivation title="Why a state-dependent baseline is free">

Claim: $\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\, b(s)\big] = 0$
for any $b$ not depending on $a$.

$$
\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\big]
= \sum_a \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,\frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}
= \nabla_\theta \sum_a \pi_\theta(a\mid s)
= \nabla_\theta 1 = 0
$$

Since $b(s)$ does not depend on $a$, it factors out of the sum and multiplies
zero. Therefore $G_t - b(s_t)$ is an unbiased weight for any such $b$.

**The assumption doing the work is that $b$ is constant with respect to the
action.** If $b$ depended on $a$ it would stay inside the sum, the telescoping
to $\nabla_\theta 1$ would fail, and a bias term would remain.

</Derivation>

The variance-minimising baseline is close to $V^\pi(s)$, which makes the weight
$G_t - V(s_t)$ — a single-sample estimate of the advantage
$A^\pi(s,a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s)$. Fitting $V$ with a critic is what turns
REINFORCE into actor-critic, and GAE is the generalisation that trades bias
against variance with a $\lambda$ knob.

<KeyIdea>
Three estimators of the same gradient, in increasing sophistication and
decreasing variance: weight by the full return $R(\tau)$, by the return-to-go
$G_t$, or by the advantage $\hat A_t$. All three are unbiased for the same
quantity. Everything that separates REINFORCE from PPO is variance reduction
plus step-size control — the underlying gradient never changed.
</KeyIdea>

## The on-policy constraint

The expectation is taken under $p_\theta$. Change $\theta$ and your samples are
from the wrong distribution. Formally you can repair this with importance
sampling:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\left[\frac{p_\theta(\tau)}{p_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\tau)}\,
\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)\, R(\tau)\right]
$$

and the dynamics cancel in the ratio too, leaving a product of per-step policy
ratios.

<Warning>
A product of $T$ ratios has variance that grows exponentially in the horizon —
one unlikely action early on can dominate the whole batch. This is why nobody
uses the full-trajectory correction. PPO uses the per-step ratio with a clip
that bounds how far the update can exploit it, TRPO bounds a KL divergence
instead, and V-trace truncates the ratios explicitly. All three are answering
the same question: how far can I move before my data stops being usable?
</Warning>
