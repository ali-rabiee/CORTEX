## The objects

$$
G_t = \sum_{k \ge 0} \gamma^k\, r_{t+k},
\qquad
V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\big[G_t \mid s_t = s\big]
$$

The reward function $r$ is the complete specification of the task; everything
else in reinforcement learning is machinery for maximising it.

## Potential-based shaping

Replace $r$ with $r' = r + F$ where

$$
F(s, a, s') = \gamma\,\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)
$$

for an arbitrary $\Phi: \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}$.

<Derivation title="The shaped optimal value is $V^* - \Phi$, so the policy is unchanged">

**Step 1 — write the shaped Bellman optimality equation.**

$$
V'^*(s) = \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)\Big[r(s,a,s') + \gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s) + \gamma V'^*(s')\Big]
$$

**Step 2 — guess $V'^* = V^* - \Phi$ and verify.** Substitute on the right:

$$
\max_a \sum_{s'} P\Big[r + \gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s) + \gamma\big(V^*(s') - \Phi(s')\big)\Big]
$$

The $\gamma\Phi(s')$ terms cancel — this is where the $\gamma$ on the first term
earns its place — and $\Phi(s)$ does not depend on $a$ or $s'$, so it comes out
of both the max and the sum:

$$
= \max_a \sum_{s'} P\big[r + \gamma V^*(s')\big] \;-\; \Phi(s)
\;=\; V^*(s) - \Phi(s)
$$

So $V^* - \Phi$ satisfies the shaped equation, and by uniqueness of the Bellman
fixed point it *is* $V'^*$.

**Step 3 — read why the policy is preserved.** The difference is a function of
**state alone**. Adding a state-dependent constant to every action's value cannot
change which is largest:

$$
\arg\max_a Q'^*(s,a) = \arg\max_a \big[Q^*(s,a) - \Phi(s)\big] = \arg\max_a Q^*(s,a)
$$

**And note the structural echo.** This is the same reason a state-dependent
baseline leaves a policy gradient unbiased — a quantity that does not depend on
the action cannot change a comparison between actions. The same fact appears in
three different places in this material.

</Derivation>

<Derivation title="Why no cycle can be made profitable">

Sum the shaping term along a trajectory $s_0, s_1, \ldots, s_T$ with the
appropriate discounting:

$$
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t\Big(\gamma\Phi(s_{t+1}) - \Phi(s_t)\Big)
= \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Big(\gamma^{t+1}\Phi(s_{t+1}) - \gamma^t\Phi(s_t)\Big)
= \gamma^T\Phi(s_T) - \Phi(s_0)
$$

Every intermediate term cancels — the telescoping — leaving only the endpoints.

**For a closed cycle**, $s_T = s_0$, so the total shaping reward is

$$
\big(\gamma^T - 1\big)\Phi(s_0) \;\le\; 0 \quad\text{when } \Phi(s_0) \ge 0
$$

and in general it is bounded rather than unbounded. Compare the naive bonus,
where each traversal of a loop adds a fixed positive amount with nothing
cancelling it, so the total grows without limit.

**This is why the form is the only safe one.** Any $F$ that does not telescope
leaves a residual per cycle, and a residual per cycle is a reward for cycling.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
The practical consequence is a strong one: the guarantee holds for *any* $\Phi$,
so you can encode a heuristic that is completely wrong and still not change the
task. A bad potential slows learning; it cannot make the optimal behaviour
different. Very few practical techniques come with an unconditional guarantee,
and this makes "is my shaping potential-based?" a question worth asking before
adding any bonus at all.
</KeyIdea>

## The scale nobody thinks about

The optimal policy is invariant under $r \mapsto cr$ for $c > 0$ — which is true,
and is why people conclude the scale does not matter. Everything downstream is
not invariant.

$$
|V^\pi(s)| \le \frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}
$$

<Warning>
At $\gamma = 0.99$ that factor is 100. A reward of order 100 gives value targets
of order $10^4$, so a critic initialised near zero spends a long time reaching the
right order of magnitude before it can start being accurate — and a learning rate
tuned at one reward scale is wrong at another.

This is a common cause of a run that presents as an optimiser problem. Normalise
the returns, or the value targets, and the symptom disappears. It is the same
reasoning that makes advantage normalisation worth having, one level down.
</Warning>

## When is a proxy safe to optimise?

Let $r^\star$ be what you want and $\tilde r$ the proxy you can compute. The
question is not whether they are equal but *where* they agree.

$$
\pi^\star = \arg\max_\pi J_{r^\star}(\pi),
\qquad
\tilde\pi = \arg\max_\pi J_{\tilde r}(\pi)
$$

<Derivation title="Why capability makes misspecification worse">

Suppose the proxy is accurate on a set of behaviours $\mathcal{B}$ — the ones you
observed while validating it:

$$
\big|J_{\tilde r}(\pi) - J_{r^\star}(\pi)\big| \le \epsilon
\qquad\text{for } \pi \in \mathcal{B}
$$

and unconstrained outside $\mathcal{B}$.

**A weak optimiser stays inside $\mathcal{B}$.** It cannot find behaviours far
from what you observed, so the proxy's error stays bounded and optimising it
approximately optimises the true objective. This is why the proxy looked fine.

**A strong optimiser leaves $\mathcal{B}$.** Maximising $\tilde r$ over *all*
policies actively seeks the region where $\tilde r$ is largest, which — since
$\tilde r$ is unconstrained outside $\mathcal{B}$ — is exactly where the two
diverge most.

$$
\tilde\pi = \arg\max_\pi J_{\tilde r}(\pi)
\quad\text{is drawn toward}\quad
\arg\max_\pi \big[J_{r^\star}(\pi) + \text{error}(\pi)\big]
$$

so the optimiser is partly maximising the *error*.

**The inversion.** $J_{r^\star}(\tilde\pi)$ can decrease as the optimiser
improves, while $J_{\tilde r}(\tilde\pi)$ rises. Reported reward goes up and
actual performance goes down.

**Why this is structurally uncomfortable.** Your validation covers a region that
the thing being validated is actively trying to leave, so the usual defence —
test it and see — does not extend to a stronger version of the same system.

</Derivation>

<Steps>
<Step title="Track the true objective separately">
If you optimise a proxy, measure what you care about on a held-out basis.
Divergence between the two curves is the only visible signal that the proxy has
been found out, and it is invisible if you plot only the training reward.
</Step>

<Step title="Prefer sparse-and-true over dense-and-approximate">
A sparse reward for genuine task completion cannot be gamed the way a dense proxy
can. It converts a specification problem into an exploration problem, which has
its own tools — demonstrations, curricula, hindsight relabelling — and those are
better understood.
</Step>

<Step title="Constrain rather than penalise, where you can">
A penalty term is another proxy and can be traded against the main reward. A hard
constraint enforced outside the objective — the safety-filter pattern — cannot
be, which is why anything that must never happen belongs there rather than in the
reward.
</Step>
</Steps>
