## The reward is the specification

There is no separate statement of what the task is. The reward function *is* the
task, and the agent optimises what you wrote rather than what you meant.

<KeyIdea>
This makes reward design a specification problem, and it fails the way
specifications fail — not by being violated, but by being satisfied in a way you
did not anticipate. An agent that games its reward is not malfunctioning. It is
working, and you wrote the wrong thing.
</KeyIdea>

<Intuition>
Pay a team by lines of code and you will get verbose code. Pay by bugs fixed and
you will get bugs introduced. Neither team is cheating — they are optimising the
thing you actually specified, and the gap between that and what you wanted is
where all the trouble lives.

The difference with a robot is that it has no social intuition to fall back on,
so the gap is exploited immediately rather than after a while.
</Intuition>

## Why shaping is dangerous

Sparse rewards are hard to learn from, so the natural instinct is to add
intermediate bonuses. That instinct changes the problem.

<Figure src="/figures/reward-shaping-invariance.svg"
        alt="Left: a chain of states with a plus-one bonus for moving toward the goal, and a note that the loop A to B to A earns reward indefinitely. Right: the same chain with potential-based shaping, where the loop sums to gamma minus one, which is negative."
        caption="A bonus attached to a transition can be generated repeatedly. Potential-based shaping telescopes, so any cycle nets at most zero and no loop can be made profitable." />

The canonical failure: reward the robot $+1$ each time it moves closer to the
goal. Moving away costs nothing. So the loop *toward, away, toward, away* earns
$+1$ every two steps forever, and an optimal policy oscillates rather than
finishing.

<Warning>
The agent is correct. Given that objective, cycling genuinely is optimal —
infinite reward beats any finite reward for completing the task. Nothing has gone
wrong with the optimiser, and no amount of tuning it will help.
</Warning>

## The form that is provably safe

Ng, Harada, and Russell showed that shaping of the form

$$
F(s, a, s') = \gamma\,\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)
$$

for any function $\Phi$ over states leaves the optimal policy **unchanged**.

<Steps>
<Step title="Why it works">
Summed along a trajectory, the terms telescope — every intermediate $\Phi$
appears once positively and once negatively — leaving only the endpoints. Around
a closed loop that gives $\gamma\Phi(s) - \Phi(s) \le 0$, so no cycle can be made
profitable.
</Step>

<Step title="It holds for *any* $\Phi$">
Which means you can encode a heuristic that is wrong and still not break the
task. A bad potential slows learning; it cannot change what the optimal
behaviour is. That is an unusually strong guarantee for a practical technique.
</Step>

<Step title="And it is the only such form">
Every other shaping term is a modification of the task, whether or not you meant
one. So "is my shaping potential-based?" is a question with a yes-or-no answer,
and it is worth asking before adding any bonus.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Capability makes misspecification worse

The uncomfortable finding is that a proxy reward validated against a weak agent
is not thereby safe for a stronger one.

<KeyIdea>
The proxy and the true objective agree over the behaviours a weak agent produces
— which is exactly where you tested it — and diverge outside that region. A more
capable optimiser explores further, finds the divergence, and exploits it. So
improving the agent can make performance on the *actual* task worse while the
reported reward rises.

That inversion is what makes reward design different from ordinary engineering:
your validation covers a region that the thing you are validating is actively
trying to leave.
</KeyIdea>

## What to do instead

<Steps>
<Step title="Prefer sparse and true over dense and approximate">
A sparse reward for actually completing the task cannot be gamed in the way a
dense proxy can. It is harder to learn from — which is an exploration problem
with its own solutions, including demonstrations, curricula, and hindsight
relabelling — rather than a specification problem.
</Step>

<Step title="If you shape, make it potential-based">
It costs nothing, the guarantee is unconditional, and it removes an entire class
of failure. There is very little reason to use any other form.
</Step>

<Step title="Read the learned behaviour as a bug report">
A policy doing something absurd that scores well has found a genuine gap in your
specification. The productive response is to look at what it is exploiting, not
to penalise that specific behaviour — which usually just moves the exploit.
</Step>

<Step title="Evaluate on the true objective, separately">
If you are optimising a proxy, measure the thing you actually care about on a
held-out basis. Divergence between the two curves is the signal that the proxy
has been found out, and it is invisible if you only track the training reward.
</Step>
</Steps>
