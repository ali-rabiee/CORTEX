interview:
  answer: >-
    The framing I'd start with is that the reward function is the complete
    specification of the task — there's no separate statement of what you wanted,
    so the agent optimises what you wrote. That means reward hacking isn't a
    malfunction. The agent is working correctly and the specification was wrong,
    and treating it as a bug in the optimiser sends you looking in the wrong
    place. The concrete version people hit immediately is shaping. Sparse rewards
    are hard to learn from, so the instinct is to add intermediate bonuses — say
    plus one each time the robot moves closer to the goal. That changes the
    problem: moving away costs nothing, so the loop toward-away-toward earns plus
    one every two steps forever, and an optimal policy oscillates rather than
    finishing. The agent is right; infinite reward beats any finite reward for
    completing the task. The fix is the Ng, Harada and Russell result: shaping of
    the form gamma times Phi of s-prime minus Phi of s leaves the optimal policy
    unchanged for any potential function Phi. The mechanism is telescoping —
    summed along a trajectory the intermediate potentials cancel and only the
    endpoints survive, so a closed cycle contributes gamma-to-the-T minus one
    times Phi, which is bounded rather than growing. No loop can be made
    profitable. If you substitute it into the Bellman equation you find the
    shaped optimal value is just V-star minus Phi — a state-dependent offset,
    and since it doesn't depend on the action the argmax is unchanged. Which is
    structurally the same reason a state-dependent baseline leaves a policy
    gradient unbiased. What makes it a rule rather than one option is the
    uniqueness half: that's the only form with the guarantee, so any other bonus
    modifies the task whether or not you meant it to. And the thing I'd raise
    that people find uncomfortable is that misspecification gets worse with
    capability. Your proxy is accurate over the behaviours you validated it on,
    which are the ones a weak agent produced, and it's unconstrained outside
    that. A stronger optimiser explores further and finds the divergence —
    maximising the proxy is partly maximising its error. So you can see the
    reported reward rising while actual task performance falls, and testing a
    smaller model gives you no assurance about a larger one.
  followUps:
    - q: "So how would you actually design a reward for a manipulation task?"
      a: >-
        Sparse and true for the thing I care about — did the object end up where
        it should — because that can't be gamed the way a dense proxy can. That
        turns it into an exploration problem rather than a specification problem,
        and exploration has better tools: demonstrations to seed the policy,
        hindsight relabelling if it's goal-conditioned, a curriculum. If I need
        shaping on top I'd make it potential-based, with a distance-to-goal
        potential, because it costs four lines and the guarantee is
        unconditional — a wrong potential slows learning and can't change the
        optimal behaviour. And anything that must never happen — exceeding a
        force limit, entering a keep-out region — goes in a constraint layer
        rather than as a penalty, because a penalty is tradeable against the
        main reward by construction and a constraint isn't.
    - q: "How would you know your agent is hacking the reward?"
      a: >-
        Not from the training curve, because by definition hacking makes the
        optimised reward go up — that's what it is. So I'd log the true
        objective separately, judged by something the agent isn't optimising: a
        scripted success check, a held-out detector, or a person watching
        rollouts. Divergence between those two curves is the entire diagnostic
        and it doesn't exist if you only plot the reward. Honestly the highest
        value thing is watching video of the rollouts, because reward hacking is
        almost always visually obvious and almost never visible in a plot. And
        I'd audit the components: log each term's share of the total magnitude,
        because a five-term composite where one term is ninety percent of the
        magnitude is a single-objective problem wearing a costume, and people
        tune coefficients without ever checking the realised contributions.
    - q: "Doesn't learning the reward from human preferences solve this?"
      a: >-
        It relocates it. A learned reward is a model fitted to a finite set of
        comparisons, so it's a proxy with its own out-of-distribution behaviour,
        and the capability argument applies unchanged — optimising it hard drives
        the policy outside the distribution the comparisons came from, where the
        model is extrapolating. The tell is in the practice: methods that
        optimise a learned reward almost always add a penalty keeping the policy
        close to the one that generated the training comparisons. That penalty is
        an explicit admission that the learned reward is only valid locally,
        and it's the same structure as a trust region for the same reason. It's
        genuinely useful where writing the reward is infeasible, and it isn't a
        dissolution of the problem.
    - q: "Why does the shaping term need the gamma on Phi of s-prime?"
      a: >-
        Because the return discounts future rewards, and the gamma is what makes
        consecutive terms cancel exactly rather than approximately. If you write
        out the discounted sum of gamma-Phi-of-s-t-plus-one minus Phi-of-s-t,
        the term at step t contributes gamma-to-the-t-plus-one times Phi of
        s-t-plus-one, and that's precisely cancelled by the minus
        gamma-to-the-t-plus-one Phi from the next step. Drop the gamma and the
        cancellation is off by a factor, residuals accumulate, and the telescoping
        — and therefore the whole guarantee — is gone. It's a small detail
        carrying the entire theorem, and it's the kind of thing that gets
        dropped when someone reimplements shaping from memory. The other one is
        forcing Phi to zero at a true terminal, since there's no following step
        to cancel the final term.
  traps:

    - "Judging progress by the reward being optimised. Hacking makes that curve rise, so the true objective has to be measured independently."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Reward rising, true objective falling → hacking">
Nearly diagnostic. Watch rollouts and audit which reward component is supplying
the increase.
</Step>

<Step title="Agent oscillates near the goal → non-telescoping shaping">
A bonus being collected repeatedly. Check whether the shaping is potential-based;
if not, make it so.
</Step>

<Step title="Odd behaviour at episode boundaries → $\Phi$ not zeroed at terminals">
The final shaping term is uncancelled and becomes a real reward for how the
episode ends. And check that a time-limit truncation is not being treated as a
terminal.
</Step>

<Step title="Tuning one coefficient changes nothing → one term dominates">
Log each component's share of the total magnitude. A carefully balanced composite
is often a single term plus noise.
</Step>

<Step title="Learns something absurd that scores well → read it as a bug report">
Penalising the specific behaviour usually moves the exploit. The fix is upstream,
in what the reward measures.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Two things make this answer land. Framing reward hacking as the optimiser working
correctly on a wrong specification — because that reframes it from a debugging
problem into a design problem and tells you where to look. And giving the shaping
theorem with its *uniqueness*, since that converts "is this bonus safe?" from a
judgement call into a yes-or-no check. Adding that misspecification gets worse
with capability, and that this means testing a smaller model provides no
assurance about a larger one, is the part that signals you have thought about
what your validation actually covers.
</Warning>
