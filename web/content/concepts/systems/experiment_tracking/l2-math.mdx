## How fast two runs separate

Let $\Delta W(t)$ be the weight-space distance between two runs that are
identical except for a perturbation of size $\epsilon$ at $t = 0$.

<Derivation title="Time to divergence, and why precision cannot buy it">

In an amplifying regime the difference grows exponentially until it saturates at
the diameter $D$ of the region the runs explore:

$$
\|\Delta W(t)\| \approx \min\!\left(\epsilon\, e^{\lambda t},\; D\right)
$$

Measured on a small MLP trained with SGD near the edge of stability,
$\lambda = 0.235$ per step — a doubling time of 2.95 steps — and $D \approx 32$,
the typical distance between two runs from different seeds. Solving for
saturation:

$$
t^{*} = \frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\!\frac{D}{\epsilon}
$$

**The assumption doing the work is that $\lambda > 0$** — that training amplifies
rather than contracts perturbations. That is a property of the regime, not of the
code, and it is what the whole result hinges on.

Now read the dependence on $\epsilon$. It is logarithmic, so precision enters
only through its number of digits:

$$
\frac{\partial t^{*}}{\partial (\text{decimal digits})} = \frac{\ln 10}{\lambda} \approx 9.8 \text{ steps}
$$

| perturbation | $\epsilon$ | $t^{*}$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| float16 ulp at $\|w\|=0.4$ | $2.4\times10^{-4}$ | 50 steps |
| float32 ulp | $3.0\times10^{-8}$ | 89 steps |
| $10^{-12}$ nudge | $10^{-12}$ | 133 steps |
| float64 ulp | $5.6\times10^{-17}$ | 174 steps |

Sixteen extra orders of magnitude of precision buys **86 steps**.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
Against a run of $10^5$ steps, that is nothing. Bit-level reproducibility is not
a resource you can buy more of; in an amplifying regime it is structurally
unavailable. What remains achievable — and is what people actually mean by
reproducible — is that the *distribution* of outcomes matches: same
configuration, same data, comparable spread over seeds.
</KeyIdea>

## What makes a comparison mean something

Write the observed difference between a treatment run and a baseline run as the
effect you care about plus the contributions of every other factor that differed:

$$
\Delta_{\text{obs}} = \underbrace{\tau}_{\text{treatment}} \;+\; \sum_{j} \beta_j\, \delta_j \;+\; \eta
$$

where $\delta_j$ is how much nuisance factor $j$ differed and $\eta$ is seed
noise.

<Derivation title="Control, randomise, or be confounded">

Averaging $n$ paired runs gives

$$
\mathbb{E}[\hat\tau] = \tau + \sum_j \beta_j\, \mathbb{E}[\delta_j],
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}[\hat\tau] = \frac{\sigma_\eta^2 + \sum_j \beta_j^2 \mathrm{Var}[\delta_j]}{n}
$$

Three cases, and only three:

1. **Held constant.** $\delta_j = 0$ always. It contributes nothing to either
   term. The cost is that your conclusion is now conditional on that value.
2. **Randomised.** $\mathbb{E}[\delta_j] = 0$ but $\mathrm{Var}[\delta_j] > 0$.
   It enters the variance, which falls as $1/n$. Your conclusion generalises over
   the factor.
3. **Confounded.** $\mathbb{E}[\delta_j] \neq 0$ — the factor moved with the
   treatment. It enters the *bias*, which has no $n$ in it.

The third is the one that ruins experiments, because it is invisible: you get a
tight confidence interval around a wrong number. Running the baseline last month
on a different GPU, or with the dependency versions of that month, puts you
squarely there.

</Derivation>

<Warning>
Note that case 1 and case 2 answer different questions, which is the point
Bouthillier et al. press. Fixing the data split shrinks your error bars and
narrows the claim to that split. If you want to say "this method is better", the
split, the initialisation and the augmentation order all have to be in case 2 —
randomised — even though that makes the interval wider. A narrower interval
around a narrower claim is not the more rigorous result, and it usually reads as
though it were.
</Warning>
