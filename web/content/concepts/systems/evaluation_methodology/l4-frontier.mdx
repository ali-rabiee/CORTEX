## The reckoning

<PaperCard refKey="henderson2018matters" />

The finding that mattered was about a ratio. Across random seeds, one algorithm's
results could span a range as wide as the gaps being claimed between algorithms —
and hyperparameters, reward scaling, network architecture, and even which
implementation you used moved results comparably.

<KeyIdea>
If the noise is as large as the effect, a comparison over a few runs is
measuring seed luck. That is not a criticism of anyone's honesty; it is a
statement about what the measurements could possibly have established. The paper
also found different implementations of the *same* algorithm differing
substantially, which is why naming an algorithm does not specify a method.
</KeyIdea>

<PaperCard refKey="colas2018seeds" />

The natural follow-up: how many runs would you actually need? Standard power
analysis, applied to the effect sizes RL papers were claiming, gives numbers far
above what was in use. This is the step that converts a methodological complaint
into something actionable, and the same calculation carries directly to
real-robot trials — where it gives the roughly 300-per-arm figure for a 10-point
improvement.

## New reporting norms

<PaperCard refKey="agarwal2021precipice" />

Rather than only diagnosing, this one supplied replacements and the tooling to
use them: interquartile mean instead of the mean over a handful of runs,
stratified bootstrap confidence intervals over both runs and tasks, and
performance profiles showing the fraction of runs above each threshold.

<Steps>
<Step title="Why IQM rather than mean or median">
Run distributions are heavy-tailed, so at five runs a mean is dominated by
whichever run got lucky and a median effectively uses one or two. Discarding the
extreme quartiles is robust without discarding most of the data.
</Step>

<Step title="Why the interval is the more important half">
A robust point estimate with no uncertainty attached is still not a measurement.
The bootstrap interval is what lets a reader tell a real improvement from a
plausible one.
</Step>

<Step title="Why performance profiles">
They show the whole distribution, and they make *dominance* visible — one
method's curve lying above another's at every threshold is a far stronger claim
than a difference of means, and it is sometimes visible when the means are not
distinguishable.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
These norms were adopted in benchmark RL and only partially in robotics, for a
structural reason rather than a cultural one: you cannot run 100 seeds of a real
robot. The transferable parts are the ones that do not require volume — report
intervals, stratify, pair the trials, and pre-register the protocol. The parts
that require many runs have to be bought with a cheaper evaluation instead.
</Warning>

## Careful work in robotics

<PaperCard refKey="mandlekar2021robomimic" />

Worth reading for its methodology as much as its findings. A large controlled
study across algorithms, observation spaces, and demonstration sources — and the
result that operator quality and observation choices swamp several of the
algorithmic differences being debated at the time.

<KeyIdea>
The pattern recurs whenever someone runs a careful ablation in this field: the
factors that dominate are usually not the ones the papers were arguing about.
Data quality, observation design, and normalisation move results more than the
architectural choice under comparison — which is a strong argument for spending
your evaluation budget on the baseline rather than on the fifth variant.
</KeyIdea>

## The pragmatic response

<PaperCard refKey="li2024simpler" />

Since real trials are the binding constraint, build a cheap evaluation and
establish that it *ranks methods the way the real one does*. The correlation is
the quantity being validated, not the simulator's appearance.

<Warning>
The failure mode is specific and worth naming: a simulated evaluation that looks
realistic and ranks differently is worse than having none, because its realism
is what makes people trust it. So the correlation has to be measured on real
policies with real results, and reported — and it should be re-measured when the
policy class changes, since a proxy validated on one family of methods need not
hold for another.
</Warning>

## Where this leaves you

<Steps>
<Step title="Established and adopted">
Report intervals. Stratify by generalisation axis. Do not compare against a
number measured under a different protocol.
</Step>

<Step title="Established and under-adopted">
Pairing trials on identical initial conditions, pre-registering the protocol,
and reserving a held-out scene set touched once. All are free, and all are rare.
</Step>

<Step title="Still open">
How to evaluate generalist policies at all. A VLA claims breadth across tasks,
objects, and instructions, and the number of trials required to characterise a
policy across that space is far beyond any lab's budget — so current claims rest
on a sampled slice chosen by the same people making the claim.
</Step>
</Steps>
