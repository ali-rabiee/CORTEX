## The interval for a single success rate

$k$ successes in $n$ trials, assumed independent and identically distributed.
The Wald interval that everyone reaches for first,

$$
\hat p \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}},
\qquad \hat p = k/n
$$

is a normal approximation centred on $\hat p$, and it misbehaves exactly where
robotics lives.

<Warning>
At $k = 0$, $n = 20$: Wald gives $0 \pm 0$. A zero-width interval from data
entirely consistent with a true rate of 10%. At $k/n$ near 0 or 1 its coverage
is far below the nominal 95%, and it routinely produces bounds outside $[0,1]$.
</Warning>

<Derivation title="The Wilson score interval">

Instead of approximating around the estimate, invert the score test: find the
values of $p$ that would *not* be rejected. Solve

$$
\left|\frac{\hat p - p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}}\right| \le z
$$

for $p$. Squaring and rearranging gives a quadratic in $p$, whose roots are

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{\hat p + \dfrac{z^2}{2n} \;\pm\; z\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \dfrac{z^2}{4n^2}}}
{1 + \dfrac{z^2}{n}}\;}
$$

**Read the structure.** The centre is $\hat p$ pulled toward $1/2$ by
$z^2/2n$ — a shrinkage that vanishes as $n$ grows. The interval is asymmetric
near the boundaries and always inside $[0,1]$. At $k=0$ it gives a genuinely
non-degenerate upper bound.

**The numbers, for an observed 70%:**

| $n$ | 95% interval | width |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | [0.40, 0.89] | 0.50 |
| 20 | [0.48, 0.86] | 0.37 |
| 50 | [0.56, 0.81] | 0.25 |
| 100 | [0.60, 0.78] | 0.18 |
| 400 | [0.65, 0.74] | 0.09 |

Width falls like $1/\sqrt{n}$, so halving it costs four times the trials.

</Derivation>

## Comparing two policies

<Derivation title="Sample size for an unpaired comparison">

To detect a difference $\delta = p_2 - p_1$ with significance $\alpha$ and power
$1-\beta$, the standard two-proportion formula gives, per arm,

$$
n \;\approx\; \frac{2\,(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2\;\bar p\,(1-\bar p)}{\delta^2},
\qquad \bar p = \tfrac{p_1+p_2}{2}
$$

At $\alpha = 0.05$ and power $0.8$, $(z_{\alpha/2}+z_\beta)^2 = (1.96+0.84)^2 \approx 7.85$:

| comparison | $n$ per arm |
| --- | --- |
| 0.50 vs 0.60 | ~389 |
| 0.70 vs 0.80 | ~294 |
| 0.80 vs 0.90 | ~200 |
| 0.50 vs 0.70 | ~94 |

**The $\delta^2$ in the denominator is the whole story.** Halving the effect you
want to detect quadruples the trials. A 10-point improvement — the size people
routinely claim — costs hundreds of real rollouts per arm.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
Carry the number 294 around. It is the cost of establishing a 10-point
improvement at a 70% base rate, unpaired, and it immediately tells you how to
read a table of a dozen methods separated by a few points on twenty trials each.
Those tables are ranking noise, and the honest response to one is to ask for
intervals rather than to argue about the ordering.
</KeyIdea>

## Why pairing is worth so much

<Derivation title="The variance that pairing removes">

Model trial $i$ under policy $j$ as depending on both the scene and the policy.
Unpaired, you estimate

$$
\hat\Delta = \hat p_2 - \hat p_1,
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(\hat\Delta) = \frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n} + \frac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n}
$$

with the two samples drawn over *different* scenes, so all the variation in
scene difficulty is inside those terms.

Paired, run both policies on the same $n$ scenes and define per-scene outcomes
$(X_i, Y_i)$. The estimator is the mean of the differences $D_i = Y_i - X_i$:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\bar D) = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(Y) + \mathrm{Var}(X) - 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)}{n}
$$

The covariance term is the gain, and it is large precisely when scenes vary in
difficulty — an easy scene tends to succeed under both policies, a hard one
under neither.

**For binary paired data this reduces to McNemar's test**: only the discordant
scenes carry information. If $b$ is the count where policy 2 succeeded and
policy 1 failed, and $c$ the reverse, the test statistic depends on $b$ and $c$
alone, and the scenes both policies handled identically are correctly ignored.

**The assumption doing the work** is that pairing is genuine — the same initial
conditions, reproduced. Approximately similar scenes do not pair, and if you
cannot reset to the same configuration, you cannot claim the gain.

</Derivation>

## Training-seed variance is a separate problem

Everything above concerns evaluation noise for a *fixed* policy. There is a
second source: retrain with a different seed and you get a different policy.

<Steps>
<Step title="RL seed distributions are heavy-tailed">
Some runs find a good solution and some do not, so a mean over three or five
runs is often reporting whether one got lucky. The distribution, not the mean, is
the object of interest.
</Step>

<Step title="Interquartile mean over stratified bootstrap intervals">
Discard the top and bottom quartiles of runs and average the rest — robust to a
single lucky or catastrophic run without discarding as much as a median. Bootstrap
over both runs and tasks to get an interval, and report the interval.
</Step>

<Step title="Performance profiles beat any single number">
Plot the fraction of runs exceeding each performance threshold. It shows the
whole distribution, and it makes dominance visible when one method's curve lies
above another's everywhere — a much stronger claim than a difference of means.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Multiple comparisons

If you evaluate ten variants and report the best, you have run ten tests and
reported the maximum. The probability that at least one exceeds a 5% threshold
by chance is $1 - 0.95^{10} \approx 40\%$.

<Warning>
This is the mechanism behind a great deal of irreproducible improvement, and it
does not require any intent to mislead — trying ten things and reporting the one
that worked is a completely natural way to do research. The fixes are to correct
the threshold, to pre-register which comparison is primary, or to reserve a
final held-out set of scenes touched exactly once. In robotics the last of these
is both the cheapest and the least commonly done.
</Warning>
