## The uncomfortable arithmetic

Robot policies are evaluated by rolling them out and counting successes. That
makes a success rate a Bernoulli estimate, and Bernoulli estimates from small
samples are much less informative than they look.

<Figure src="/figures/eval-confidence-intervals.svg"
        alt="Five horizontal confidence intervals for an observed 70% success rate at 10, 20, 50, 100 and 400 trials. The n equals 10 interval spans roughly 40 to 89 percent; the n equals 400 interval spans 65 to 74."
        caption="Every one of these is 'a 70% success rate'. At ten trials the measurement is consistent with a policy that fails a third of the time and one that almost never does." />

<Intuition>
Someone tells you a coin came up heads seven times out of ten and asks whether
it is biased. You would not commit either way — seven of ten is entirely
ordinary for a fair coin.

Now notice that this is exactly the evidence behind a great many reported robot
results, and that the claim being made is usually much stronger than "the coin
might be biased".
</Intuition>

## Why this is worse for robots than for benchmarks

In simulation you run ten thousand episodes and the problem disappears. On
hardware, a single trial takes minutes: reset the scene, place the objects, run,
score, reset. Three hundred trials is a week.

<KeyIdea>
The interval narrows like $1/\sqrt{n}$, so halving the width costs four times
the trials. That is an unpleasant exchange rate against a currency measured in
robot-hours, and it is the fundamental reason evaluation is the weakest part of
robot learning research — not carelessness, but a genuinely expensive
measurement.
</KeyIdea>

Comparisons are harder still. Measuring one policy to within ten points takes
about a hundred trials; showing that one policy beats another *by* ten points,
at 70%, takes roughly three hundred per arm. A results table with a dozen
methods a few points apart on twenty trials each is reporting ranking noise, and
the authors usually know it.

## What actually helps

The useful responses are structural rather than statistical — they change what
you measure, not just how you analyse it.

<Steps>
<Step title="Pair the trials">
Evaluate both policies from *identical* initial conditions: same object poses,
same lighting, same clutter. Most of the variance in a robot evaluation comes
from scenes being unequally hard, and pairing differences that away instead of
averaging over it. This is free and it is the single highest-return change
available.
</Step>

<Step title="Fix the protocol before you look">
Decide the number of trials, the initial conditions, the scoring rule, and what
counts as a partial success — and write it down first. Deciding to stop when the
numbers look good is not a small bias; with intervals this wide it is most of
the effect.
</Step>

<Step title="Stratify by what you are claiming">
One aggregate success rate answers no interesting question. Report seen objects
separately from unseen ones, seen instructions from rephrased ones, trained
lighting from changed lighting. A policy at 80% overall could be at 95% on
familiar scenes and 20% on novel ones, and only the split tells you what you
built.
</Step>

<Step title="Report the interval, not the point">
"70% (95% CI 40-89%, n=10)" is honest and immediately conveys how much weight to
put on it. A bare "70%" invites a conclusion the data does not support.
</Step>
</Steps>

## The failure modes to recognise

<Warning>
**Cherry-picked resets.** The experimenter places objects, and after a hundred
resets they place them in positions the policy handles. This is unconscious and
enormous. Randomise the initial conditions from a written list, or have someone
who has not seen the policy do the resets.

**Evaluating on your development scenes.** If you tuned against these
configurations, they are training data. Held-out scenes are as necessary as a
held-out test set, and they are much easier to forget when the "dataset" is a
room.

**Silent protocol drift.** Fifty trials over three days across two people
accumulates small changes in what counts as success, where objects start, and
how long a timeout is. Write the protocol down and score from video where you
can.
</Warning>

## The pragmatic escape

Since real trials are the scarce resource, the productive move is to build an
evaluation that is cheap and *demonstrated to correlate* with the real one —
usually a simulated suite matched to the real setup.

The correlation is the thing that has to be established, not the simulator's
realism. A simulated evaluation that ranks methods the same way the real one
does is useful even if its absolute numbers are wrong; one that looks
photorealistic and ranks differently is worse than nothing, because it is
persuasive.
