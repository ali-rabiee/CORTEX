interview:
  answer: >-
    Evaluating a robot policy is a Bernoulli estimation problem, and the
    uncomfortable part is how little a small sample tells you. Seven successes
    out of ten looks like a 70 percent policy; the 95 percent interval is
    roughly 40 to 89 percent, which is consistent with a policy that fails a
    third of the time and with one that almost never does. The width shrinks
    like one over root n, so halving it costs four times the trials — and on
    hardware a trial is minutes, because you have to reset the scene. Comparing
    two policies is much harder than measuring one. The standard two-proportion
    power calculation says detecting a ten-point improvement at a seventy
    percent base rate needs about three hundred trials per arm, unpaired, at
    conventional power. That number is the one I carry around, because it tells
    you how to read a table of a dozen methods a few points apart on twenty
    trials each — that's ranking noise, and the honest response is to ask for
    intervals rather than argue about the ordering. What I'd actually change,
    in order. First, pair the trials: evaluate both policies from identical
    initial conditions, same object poses, same lighting. Most of the variance
    in a robot evaluation is that some scenes are simply harder, and pairing
    differences that out instead of averaging over it. It costs nothing beyond
    writing your initial conditions down and reusing them, and for binary
    outcomes only the discordant scenes carry information, which is McNemar's
    test. Second, fix the protocol before looking at any results — number of
    trials, the scene list generated from a seed, the scoring rule, what counts
    as partial success. Stopping when the numbers look good isn't a small bias
    when intervals are this wide. Third, stratify: seen objects versus unseen,
    seen instructions versus rephrased, trained lighting versus changed. A
    policy at eighty percent overall might be ninety-five on familiar scenes
    and twenty on novel ones, and that's the entire finding, invisible in the
    aggregate. And I'd frame the whole thing as expensive rather than as people
    being careless. The measurement really does cost robot-hours, which is why
    the productive move is building a cheap evaluation — usually simulated —
    and validating that it *ranks* methods the way the real one does. The
    correlation is what you're establishing, not the simulator's realism.
  followUps:
    - q: "You have budget for 200 real trials. How do you spend them?"
      a: >-
        One well-powered paired comparison rather than several underpowered
        ones — a hundred scenes run under both policies. Five twenty-trial
        comparisons give you five numbers you can't defend, and the temptation
        to report the favourable one is exactly the multiple-comparisons problem
        that produces irreproducible improvements. If the comparison I care
        about needs more power than 200 paired trials give, I'd rather say so
        and use the budget to characterise one policy well — stratified, with
        intervals — than produce a comparison the data can't support. And I'd
        check the discordant count: if ninety of the hundred scenes came out the
        same under both policies, I effectively had ten informative trials, and
        I should report that rather than the hundred.
    - q: "Why is pairing so much better, mechanically?"
      a: >-
        Because it changes what you're estimating. Unpaired, you estimate two
        means over different scene draws, so all the variance in scene
        difficulty sits inside both estimates. Paired, you estimate the mean of
        the per-scene *differences*, and the variance of a difference includes
        minus twice the covariance — which is large precisely when scenes vary
        in difficulty, since an easy scene tends to succeed under both policies
        and a hard one under neither. For binary outcomes it reduces to
        McNemar: the concordant scenes are correctly ignored and only the ones
        where the policies disagreed carry signal. The requirement is that the
        pairing is genuine — the same initial conditions actually reproduced. If
        I can't reset to the same configuration I can't claim the gain.
    - q: "How would you evaluate a generalist policy that claims broad generalisation?"
      a: >-
        I'd start by admitting the problem is not solved. A VLA claims breadth
        across tasks, objects, phrasings, and scenes, and characterising a
        policy over that space needs far more trials than any lab has — so every
        current claim rests on a slice sampled by the people making the claim,
        which is the weakest link in that literature. What I'd do is make the
        sampling explicit and adversarial to myself: define the axes in advance,
        sample scenes within each stratum from a seed rather than by choosing
        them, and have someone who hasn't seen the policy do the resets and the
        scoring. Then report per-stratum with intervals and be honest that the
        aggregate isn't the interesting number. And I'd invest in a simulated
        suite whose ranking correlation with real trials I've measured, because
        that's the only way to get the volume the claim actually needs.
    - q: "Isn't this over-engineering for a research prototype?"
      a: >-
        For a prototype where I'm deciding whether an idea is worth pursuing,
        yes — ten trials is a fine smoke test and I'd say so rather than
        pretending otherwise. What I'd insist on even then is the language:
        "seven of ten, so it's doing something" is honest, and "seventy percent
        success" invites a conclusion the data doesn't support. The cost of the
        rigour is mostly front-loaded and small anyway — generating a scene list
        from a seed and reusing it is an afternoon, and it's what makes the
        eventual comparison possible. The expensive part isn't the methodology,
        it's the trials, and good methodology is how you get more out of the
        trials you can afford.
  traps:

    - "Choosing initial conditions by hand. After a hundred resets, experimenter placement drifts toward configurations the policy handles, and the bias is large and unconscious."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Suite says 85%, demo room fails → the eval scenes were dev scenes">
Anything tuned against is training data. Reserve a held-out scene set and touch
it once.
</Step>

<Step title="Improvements never replicate → underpowered comparisons plus selection">
Trying ten variants and reporting the best is a multiple-comparisons procedure
whether or not it was intended as one. Pre-register the primary comparison, or
reserve a final held-out set.
</Step>

<Step title="Results shift between operators or days → protocol drift">
Log the operator and the timestamp. Score blind from video where possible. Small
changes in reset placement and success judgement accumulate quickly.
</Step>

<Step title="Paired comparison shows nothing → check the discordant count">
If most scenes came out the same under both policies, the effective sample is
much smaller than the total. Report the discordant count alongside the result.
</Step>

<Step title="Simulated eval disagrees with real → the proxy was never validated">
Measure the ranking correlation on real policies before trusting it, and
re-measure when the policy class changes.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The two moves that make this answer land are a number and a tone. The number is
that detecting a 10-point improvement at 70% costs roughly 300 trials per arm —
it converts a vague worry about rigour into an engineering constraint, and it
immediately explains how to read most published tables. The tone is that this is
*expensive*, not sloppy: the measurement genuinely costs robot-hours, and the
productive response is pairing, pre-registration, and validated cheap proxies
rather than criticism. Interviewers in this field have all been on the wrong end
of an underpowered evaluation, and they are listening for whether you understand
the constraint or just the statistics.
</Warning>
