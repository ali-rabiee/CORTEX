## The organising taxonomy

<PaperCard refKey="brunke2022safelearning" />

The most useful thing this survey does is separate three families by *where the
guarantee comes from*, which determines what each one survives.

<Steps>
<Step title="Safety by construction">
Restrict the policy class so unsafe behaviour is unrepresentable. Strongest when
it applies, and it usually applies only to simple systems and simple constraints.
</Step>

<Step title="Safety by constrained learning">
Penalise or constrain during training — Lagrangian methods, constrained policy
optimisation. Bounds an *expectation* over the training distribution, which is a
statistical statement, not a per-episode guarantee.
</Step>

<Step title="Safety by runtime filtering">
Project the action at execution time. The guarantee is independent of how the
policy was trained, which is why it composes with an arbitrary learned
controller — and why it is what actually gets deployed.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
The corollary is worth stating explicitly: constrained training does **not**
strengthen a filtered system's guarantee. It reduces how often the filter has to
intervene, which matters for performance and for whether anyone leaves the filter
switched on — but the safety claim comes from the filter, and swapping the policy
does not require redoing it.
</KeyIdea>

## The filter machinery

<PaperCard refKey="ames2019cbf" />

The reference for the forward-invariance argument and the QP formulation.
Its practical significance is that it turns a Lyapunov-style analysis into a
component: one quadratic objective, one linear constraint per barrier, solved in
microseconds around an arbitrary nominal controller.

<Warning>
The guarantee is bought with three assumptions, and it is worth naming which one
bites in your setting. **Known dynamics** — the Lie derivatives need $f$ and $g$,
and model error enters the constraint directly, remedied by tightening with an
error bound you must actually have. **Relative degree one** — if the input does
not appear in $\dot h$ the constraint says nothing about $u$. **Feasibility** —
with input bounds, the QP can have no solution, which is a statement about the
state rather than about the solver.
</Warning>

<PaperCard refKey="dalal2018safeexploration" />

A pragmatic middle path from the RL side: learn a linear model of how actions
affect the constraint signal, and add a closed-form projection layer to the
policy. Weaker than a full CBF and much cheaper to obtain, and it targets the
period that matters most — keeping constraints satisfied *during exploration*
rather than only once the policy has converged.

## The formal-methods line

<PaperCard refKey="alshiekh2018shielding" />

The same runtime-filtering idea with a formal guarantee: synthesise a shield
from a temporal-logic specification, and let it correct any action that would
violate the property. In a discrete setting the shield is airtight.

<KeyIdea>
The limitation is the abstraction, not the method. The guarantee is relative to
the model you synthesised against, and constructing a faithful discrete
abstraction of continuous contact-rich manipulation is itself the hard problem —
any tractable abstraction is lossy exactly where the difficulty is. That is why
continuous control converged on barrier functions and reachability, where the
model stays continuous and the conservatism is explicit rather than buried in the
abstraction step.
</KeyIdea>

## Knowing when you are outside your competence

<PaperCard refKey="lindemann2023conformalplanning" />
</PaperGrid>

Both filters and shields answer "is this action about to violate a constraint I
wrote down?" Neither notices that the *scene* is unlike anything the policy was
trained on, because that is not expressible as a barrier.

Conformal prediction supplies the missing signal: take any anomaly score, and a
rank argument on a held-out calibration set turns it into a threshold with
finite-sample coverage — distribution-free, requiring only exchangeability.
Lindemann et al. carry it into planning, so a planner reasons with calibrated
prediction regions around learned forecasts rather than with point predictions.

<Warning>
Exchangeability is the assumption deployment erodes. Lighting drifts, the object
set changes, hardware wears — so today's observations are not exchangeable with
a calibration set from six months ago, and the threshold's nominal rate stops
being its actual rate. The operational form of the guarantee is "valid over the
period the calibration represents", which makes monitoring the empirical flag
rate against the nominal one, and recalibrating on recent data, part of running
the system.
</Warning>

## What the field has and has not settled

<Steps>
<Step title="Settled">
That safety belongs in a layer independent of the policy, that runtime filtering
composes with arbitrary learned controllers, and that expectation constraints are
the wrong object for anything that must never happen.
</Step>

<Step title="Workable but assumption-heavy">
Barrier functions for constraints expressible in a model you have. Every
deployment is an argument about whether the model error bound is real and whether
the safe set is control-invariant given the actuators.
</Step>

<Step title="Genuinely open">
Semantic safety. "Do not close the gripper on a person's hand" is not a barrier
function — it requires recognising a situation, so its reliability is bounded by
a perception model's, and no amount of control theory upgrades that. The honest
architectural response is to keep it in a separate layer with a weaker claim
attached rather than to let it contaminate the guarantees you can actually make.
</Step>
</Steps>
