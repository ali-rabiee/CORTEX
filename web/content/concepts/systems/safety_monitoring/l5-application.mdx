interview:
  answer: >-
    The starting point is that safety can't be a property of the policy. A
    learned policy returns an action for any input, including scenes unlike
    anything it trained on, and it has no reliable internal notion of being out
    of its depth because nothing in the objective asked it for one. Training
    with a penalty on unsafe actions doesn't fix that either — a penalty shapes
    the expected behaviour over the training distribution, and safety is a
    per-instance constraint that has to hold on exactly the unusual inputs the
    training distribution didn't cover. So the architecture is layered, and the
    guarantee lives below the policy. The policy proposes an action; a safety
    filter with its own sensing and its own simple model projects it onto the
    set of actions that keep the system inside a verified safe region. Concretely
    that's a control barrier function: define h of x positive inside the safe
    set, and require h-dot at least minus alpha of h. That's a rate limit that
    scales with your distance from danger, so you can move fast in the interior
    and the permitted rate of approach goes to zero at the boundary — which
    makes the set forward invariant. For control-affine dynamics the condition is
    linear in u, so finding the nearest safe action is a QP: minimise the
    distance to the policy's action subject to that inequality and the input
    bounds. It solves in microseconds and returns the policy's action unchanged
    whenever the constraint is slack, which matters for adoption — a filter that
    constantly overrides gets switched off. Three assumptions buy that guarantee
    and each fails in a way worth naming: you need the dynamics, which are wrong,
    so you tighten by a model-error bound you have to actually have; you need the
    input to appear in h-dot, so a position barrier with acceleration control
    needs a higher-order form; and the QP can be infeasible, which isn't a solver
    problem — it says the safe set was designed geometrically without accounting
    for braking distance under the actuator limits. Below the filter I'd want a
    real-time limit checker on joint, velocity and force bounds, and a hardware
    e-stop with no software in the path, because that's the layer that works when
    the computer has hung. And the piece none of that covers is noticing the
    scene is outside what the policy handles at all — for that I'd use conformal
    prediction to turn an anomaly score into a threshold with a coverage
    guarantee, while being clear that it assumes exchangeability, which drift
    breaks, so recalibration is part of operating the thing.
  followUps:
    - q: "What would you refuse to promise?"
      a: >-
        Semantic safety, at the same confidence as the geometric constraints.
        "Don't close the gripper on a person's hand" isn't a barrier function —
        it requires recognising a situation, so its reliability is bounded by a
        perception model's reliability, which is bounded by the same things that
        limit the policy. I'd still build it, with a person detector and a
        conservative interlock, but I'd keep it in a separate layer with a
        weaker claim attached and say so explicitly. What I won't do is put it
        in the same mechanism as joint limits and force thresholds, because that
        quietly downgrades the guarantee on the constraints I *can* make good on
        to the reliability of the one I can't. Being precise about which claims
        are provable and which are statistical is most of what a safety case is.
    - q: "Your safety QP is infeasible during fast motions. What do you do?"
      a: >-
        At runtime, execute a predefined verified failsafe — a braking or retract
        controller — and log it. Passing the policy's action through is the worst
        option, because infeasibility means no admissible action satisfies the
        constraint, so the policy's certainly doesn't. And relaxing the
        constraint converts a detected violation into an undetected one. Then
        afterwards I'd treat recurring infeasibility as a design bug rather than
        a runtime one: it means the safe set isn't control-invariant given the
        input bounds. A geometric set like "stay ten centimetres from the
        obstacle" ignores braking distance, so at speed you can be inside h
        greater than zero and outside the set you can actually keep yourself in.
        The fix is to design the set to respect the actuation limits, which is
        what backup-controller and reachability methods compute directly.
    - q: "Isn't a backup-controller approach simpler than barrier functions?"
      a: >-
        Often yes, and I'd reach for it when the barrier is awkward to
        construct. You verify a simple controller — a hard brake, a retract to
        home — and define the safe set as the states from which it succeeds.
        Then you allow the policy's action only if the resulting next state is
        still in that set. It trades analysis for computation: you don't need a
        barrier with the right relative degree, you need to simulate the backup
        forward and check. It also degrades more honestly, because when the
        check fails you have a specific verified thing to do rather than an
        infeasible QP and a decision to make in the exception handler. The cost
        is that the safe set is now implicit and expensive to characterise, so
        you can't reason about it as easily offline.
    - q: "How would you know the safety layer is working, before something goes wrong?"
      a: >-
        Instrument the intervention itself. Log how often the filter changes the
        action and by how much, on every step — a rising intervention rate means
        the policy is drifting toward the boundary, and it moves well before a
        success rate does. Same for infeasibility counts and OOD flags. And I'd
        test the layer directly rather than hoping the policy generates the test
        case: feed the filter deliberately unsafe proposals, full-speed commands
        straight into the barrier, and confirm it corrects them. Then feed the
        *policy* garbage while leaving the filter's sensing intact and confirm
        the system still holds — if it doesn't, my layers aren't independent,
        which is the exact failure the architecture exists to prevent. Every
        failsafe path gets executed, because one that has never run is a
        hypothesis, and this is the one place where that's physical.
  traps:
    - "Proposing to make the policy safe through reward shaping or a constrained objective. Those bound an expectation over the training distribution; safety is a per-instance constraint on inputs that distribution did not cover."
    - "Letting the safety filter read the policy's perception. Layers must fail independently, and a filter downstream of the same perception is the same opinion with a QP after it."
    - "Treating QP infeasibility as a numerical issue. It means the safe set is not control-invariant given the actuation limits — a design bug, not a runtime one."
    - "Claiming semantic safety at the same confidence as geometric constraints. It inherits the perception model's reliability and belongs in its own layer with a weaker claim."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Filter intervening constantly → the policy is running at the boundary">
Log intervention rate and magnitude. A high rate is not a filter problem; it says
the policy was never shaped to respect the constraint, and it is the precursor to
someone switching the filter off.
</Step>

<Step title="QP infeasible at speed → the safe set is not control-invariant">
Geometric sets ignore braking distance. Redesign against the input bounds, and
have a verified failsafe for the residual cases.
</Step>

<Step title="Filter fails when the policy fails → the layers are not independent">
The filter is reading the policy's perception. Give it its own sensing —
encoders, force-torque, direct range — and its own simple model.
</Step>

<Step title="OOD flag rate drifting from nominal → exchangeability broken">
The conformal threshold has stopped meaning what it claims. Recalibrate on recent
verified-nominal data, and monitor the empirical rate as a drift alarm.
</Step>

<Step title="Safe in testing, unsafe in a new cell → semantic constraints failed silently">
Anything depending on recognition inherits the detector's reliability. Check
which constraints were geometric and which were semantic, and whether the claim
you made covered both.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The register that works here is precision about what is guaranteed and what is
not. Anyone can say "we add a safety layer". What distinguishes a strong answer
is naming the mechanism (a CBF-QP projecting onto the safe set), the three
assumptions it rests on and how each fails, the independence requirement that
makes layering mean anything, and the boundary between constraints you can prove
and constraints bounded by a perception model. Volunteering the last of those —
that you would not promise semantic safety at the same confidence — is worth more
than any amount of enthusiasm about the machinery.
</Warning>
