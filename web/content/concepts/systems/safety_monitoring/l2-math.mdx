## The safe set and forward invariance

Let the state be $x$ and define a continuously differentiable $h$ whose zero
super-level set is the region you want to stay in:

$$
\mathcal{C} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : h(x) \ge 0\}
$$

$h$ might be distance to an obstacle minus a margin, or remaining joint travel,
or a force threshold minus the measured force.

<Derivation title="Why a condition on $\dot h$ keeps you inside forever">

**The naive condition.** Requiring $\dot h \ge 0$ everywhere certainly keeps
$h \ge 0$, and it also forbids ever approaching the boundary — far too strong to
be useful, since the policy could never move toward an obstacle at all.

**The barrier condition.** Let $\alpha$ be a strictly increasing function with
$\alpha(0) = 0$ (an extended class-$\mathcal{K}$ function; $\alpha(h) = \gamma h$
is the usual choice). Require

$$
\dot h(x, u) \;\ge\; -\alpha\big(h(x)\big)
$$

Read what this permits. When $h$ is large you may decrease it quickly. As
$h \to 0$, the permitted rate of decrease $\alpha(h) \to 0$.

**Why that implies invariance.** Along any trajectory, compare $h(t)$ with the
solution of $\dot y = -\alpha(y)$, $y(0) = h(x_0) \ge 0$. By the comparison
lemma, $h(t) \ge y(t)$ for all $t$. And $y = 0$ is an equilibrium of
$\dot y = -\alpha(y)$, so a solution starting at $y(0) > 0$ approaches zero
asymptotically and never reaches it in finite time. Hence $h(t) \ge y(t) > 0$:
the set is **forward invariant**.

**Why this is the useful form.** The constraint is inactive in the interior and
tightens only near the boundary. The policy keeps full authority everywhere it
is not about to cause a violation — which is what "minimal intervention" means
formally.

</Derivation>

## The filter as a quadratic program

Assume control-affine dynamics, which covers most robot models:

$$
\dot x = f(x) + g(x)\,u
$$

Then

$$
\dot h = \nabla h(x)^\top\big(f(x) + g(x)u\big)
= \underbrace{L_f h(x)}_{\text{drift}} + \underbrace{L_g h(x)}_{\text{row vector}}\,u
$$

which is **affine in $u$**. So the barrier condition is a linear inequality, and
finding the nearest safe action is a QP:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
u^* = \arg\min_{u \in \mathcal{U}} \;& \|u - u_\pi(x)\|^2 \\
\text{subject to}\quad & L_f h(x) + L_g h(x)\,u \;\ge\; -\alpha\big(h(x)\big)
\end{aligned}}
$$

<KeyIdea>
One quadratic objective and one linear constraint per barrier. It solves in
microseconds, runs at the control rate, and returns $u_\pi$ exactly whenever the
constraint is slack. That combination — provable invariance, negligible cost,
zero interference when things are fine — is why runtime filtering became the
practical answer rather than constrained training.
</KeyIdea>

## What it assumes, and how each assumption fails

<Steps>
<Step title="Known dynamics">
$L_f h$ and $L_g h$ require $f$ and $g$. Robot models are wrong, and the error
enters the constraint directly. The standard remedy is to tighten the constraint
by a bound on the model error, $\dot h \ge -\alpha(h) + \varepsilon$, which trades
conservatism for robustness — and you must have a bound to use it.
</Step>

<Step title="Relative degree one">
$L_g h \ne 0$ is needed, or the input does not appear in $\dot h$ and the
constraint says nothing about $u$. For a position-based $h$ with acceleration
control, $L_g h = 0$ and you need a higher-order barrier that differentiates
until the input appears.
</Step>

<Step title="Feasibility">
With input bounds $u \in \mathcal{U}$, the QP can be infeasible: no admissible
action satisfies the constraint. This is not a solver problem — it says the state
is one from which safety cannot be recovered with the available actuation.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Infeasibility is the failure mode worth understanding, because it exposes a
common design error. A geometric safe set — "stay 10 cm from the obstacle" —
ignores braking distance, so at speed the system can be inside $\{h \ge 0\}$ and
outside the set it can actually *keep itself* in. The fix is to design a
control-invariant set that respects the input bounds, which is what backup-controller
and reachability methods compute directly. Whatever you do, the residual
infeasible cases need a defined failsafe rather than whatever the solver returns
on failure.
</Warning>

## The alternative: a backup controller

Rather than a barrier, verify a simple controller $\pi_{\text{backup}}$ — a hard
brake, a retract to a home pose — and define the safe set as the states from
which it succeeds:

$$
\mathcal{S} = \big\{x : \text{the trajectory under } \pi_{\text{backup}} \text{ from } x \text{ never violates the constraint}\big\}
$$

Then allow the learned policy's action only if the resulting next state is still
in $\mathcal{S}$; otherwise hand over to the backup.

<KeyIdea>
This trades analysis for computation. You do not need a barrier function with the
right relative degree — you need to be able to *simulate the backup controller
forward* and check. It is often much easier to construct in practice, and it
degrades honestly: when the check fails you have a specific verified thing to do,
rather than an infeasible QP.
</KeyIdea>

## A calibrated "I do not know"

Neither mechanism above notices that the *scene* is unlike anything in training.
For that you need an uncertainty score with a meaning.

<Derivation title="Conformal coverage, from a rank argument">

Take any score $s(x)$ where larger means more anomalous — ensemble variance, a
density estimate, a reconstruction error. Its scale is arbitrary and its
calibration unknown.

**Step 1 — score a held-out calibration set.** Compute $s_1, \ldots, s_n$ on $n$
in-distribution examples not used for training.

**Step 2 — take an order statistic as the threshold.** Let

$$
\hat q = \text{the } \big\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \big\rceil \text{-th smallest of } s_1,\ldots,s_n
$$

**Step 3 — the guarantee.** If the test score $s_{n+1}$ is exchangeable with the
calibration scores, then all $(n+1)!$ orderings are equally likely, so the rank
of $s_{n+1}$ among the $n+1$ scores is uniform. Therefore

$$
\Pr\big(s_{n+1} \le \hat q\big) \;\ge\; 1 - \alpha
$$

**Note what is not assumed.** Nothing about the distribution's form, nothing
about the model being calibrated, nothing about $s$ being a probability. The
argument is combinatorial — it is about ranks — which is why it holds for any
score you care to invent.

**The assumption that does the work is exchangeability**, and it is exactly what
deployment breaks. Lighting drifts, the object set changes, the hardware wears —
so today's observations are not exchangeable with March's calibration set. The
honest statement is that the guarantee holds over the period the calibration
represents, which makes recalibration on recent data part of operating the
system rather than an optional refinement.

</Derivation>

## Expected safety is not safety

A constrained-RL objective of the form

$$
\max_\pi\; \mathbb{E}[R] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbb{E}[C] \le d
$$

bounds the *expected* cost. That permits rare catastrophic violations offset by
many safe episodes, which is precisely the trade you do not want when the cost is
an injury.

<Warning>
If the constraint must hold per-episode rather than on average, an expectation
constraint is the wrong object. CVaR — the mean over the worst $\beta$-fraction
of episodes — is the usual improvement and it is still a statistical statement,
not a guarantee. Anything that must never happen belongs in the filter layer,
where the claim is invariance rather than an expectation, and not in the training
objective.
</Warning>
