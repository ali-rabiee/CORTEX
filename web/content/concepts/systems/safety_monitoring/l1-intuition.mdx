## Why safety cannot live in the policy

A learned policy is a function that returns an action for any input. Show it a
scene unlike anything in its training data and it does not decline — it produces
something confident and wrong. It also has no reliable internal notion of being
out of its depth, because nothing in the training objective asked it to have one.

<KeyIdea>
So safety has to be a **separate layer that does not depend on the policy being
right**. The policy proposes; something simpler, independently sensed, and
actually verifiable disposes. Any architecture where the guarantee flows through
the network has no guarantee.
</KeyIdea>

<Intuition>
A skilled driver does not make a car safe. Seatbelts, crumple zones, and ABS make
a car safe, and they work precisely because they do not assume the driver is
skilled — they are simple, testable mechanisms that hold regardless.

The learned policy is the driver. You want it to be excellent. You do not want
your safety case to rest on it.
</Intuition>

## Defence in depth

<Figure src="/figures/safety-filter-layers.svg"
        alt="A learned policy proposes an action to a safety filter with its own sensing, which solves a small optimisation to find the nearest safe action before commanding the actuators. Below, nested layers: policy, safety filter, real-time limit checker, hardware e-stop."
        caption="Each layer is simpler and more verifiable than the one above it. The capable component is unverifiable, the verifiable components are not very capable, and the architecture is what lets you have both." />

<Steps>
<Step title="The learned policy — capable, unverifiable">
Does the task. Makes no promises. Should be *penalised* for unsafe behaviour
during training, not because that makes it safe but because it makes the layer
below intervene less often.
</Step>

<Step title="The safety filter — simple model, provable invariance">
Takes the proposed action and returns the nearest action that keeps the system
inside a safe set. Uses its own sensing and its own low-fidelity dynamics model,
so it does not inherit the policy's failure modes. This is where the guarantee
actually lives.
</Step>

<Step title="The real-time limit checker — bounds, not models">
Joint limits, velocity limits, force thresholds, workspace boundaries. No
optimisation, no model, no learning. Runs at the control rate on the real-time
side and is simple enough to read in full.
</Step>

<Step title="The hardware stop — no software in the path">
A physical circuit. It works when the computer has hung, the network is down, and
the software is in a state nobody anticipated — which is the state you most need
it in.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Minimal intervention is what makes it practical

The filter is not a replacement policy. It solves a small optimisation: find the
action closest to what the policy asked for, subject to remaining safe. When the
policy is behaving, the filter changes nothing and costs nothing.

<KeyIdea>
That matters for adoption as much as for performance. A safety layer that
constantly overrides the policy will be turned off, and a layer that only acts
at the boundary will not. It also gives you a free diagnostic: how often the
filter intervenes, and by how much, measures how close your policy is running to
the edge — a leading indicator that a success rate will not give you.
</KeyIdea>

## Two kinds of safety, and they should not share a layer

<Warning>
**Geometric and dynamic constraints** — joint limits, velocity, force, workspace
bounds, self-collision — are checkable against quantities you measure directly.
They can live in a simple layer you can verify line by line, and in some settings
certify.

**Semantic safety** — "do not close the gripper on a person's hand", "do not
knock the cup off the table" — requires *recognising a situation*. Its
reliability is bounded by the perception model's reliability, which is bounded
by the same things that limit the policy.

Putting them in the same mechanism quietly downgrades the guarantee on the first
set to the reliability of the second. Keep them in separate layers with
different claims attached, and be honest about which claim is which.
</Warning>

## Knowing when you do not know

The remaining gap is detecting that the current situation is outside what the
policy can handle at all, which needs an uncertainty signal the policy does not
natively provide. Two workable approaches.

**Ensemble disagreement.** Train several policies and measure how much they
differ. Where the data was dense they agree; where it was absent they diverge.
Cheap in principle, expensive in inference, and it gives a number without a
calibrated meaning.

**Conformal prediction.** Take any score you like — ensemble variance, a density
estimate, a reconstruction error — and calibrate a threshold on held-out data so
that the flag fires at a chosen rate. The coverage guarantee is distribution-free
and requires only that calibration and deployment data be exchangeable, which is
about as weak an assumption as is available.

<Warning>
Exchangeability is a real assumption and it is exactly what deployment violates
over time: the lighting changes, the object set changes, the robot wears. A
conformal threshold calibrated in March is not calibrated in September. Recalibrate
on recent data, and treat the guarantee as a statement about a period rather than
about the system.
</Warning>
