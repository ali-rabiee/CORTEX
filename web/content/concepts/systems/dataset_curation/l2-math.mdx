## What a mixture of operators does

Let the dataset be generated by operators $j = 1 \ldots J$ with policies
$\pi_j(a \mid o)$ and mixing weights $w_j$. The dataset's conditional
distribution is a mixture:

$$
p_{\mathcal{D}}(a \mid o) = \sum_j \tilde w_j(o)\, \pi_j(a\mid o),
\qquad
\tilde w_j(o) = \frac{w_j\, p_j(o)}{\sum_{j'} w_{j'}\,p_{j'}(o)}
$$

where $p_j(o)$ is operator $j$'s state distribution — so the effective mixing
weight is state-dependent, since operators visit different states.

<Derivation title="MSE cloning fits the mixture mean">

The MSE-optimal deterministic policy is the conditional mean:

$$
\mu^*(o) = \mathbb{E}_{p_\mathcal{D}}[a \mid o]
= \sum_j \tilde w_j(o)\, \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_j}[a\mid o]}_{\mu_j(o)}
$$

a weighted average of the operators' mean actions.

**When this is benign.** If the $\mu_j$ are close, the average is a reasonable
compromise and the mixture acts like noise around a common strategy — the usual
case for operators trained on the same protocol.

**When it is harmful.** If two operators approach an object from opposite sides,
the midpoint passes through the object. The averaged action was demonstrated by
nobody and may be invalid, and the loss is *lower* there than at either
strategy. This is mode averaging with the modes supplied by people rather than
by task ambiguity.

**Why quality mixing is worse than it sounds.** A novice is not simply a noisier
expert — a novice often has a systematically different strategy, so
$\mu_{\text{novice}} \ne \mu_{\text{expert}}$ in a structured way, and the
average inherits neither's competence.

</Derivation>

## Two fixes, with different costs

<Steps>
<Step title="Filter">
Keep only operators above a quality bar. Removes the averaging entirely, and
costs you their coverage — which may have been the only data you had in some
region of state space.
</Step>

<Step title="Condition">
Add an operator or quality token to the observation and train on everything.
Then $\mathbb{E}[a \mid o, j] = \mu_j(o)$: the conditional mean is taken *within*
an operator rather than across them, so requesting the expert at inference
recovers the expert's strategy while the novice data still trains the shared
encoder.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
Conditioning is the same mechanism as return conditioning in offline RL: keep
low-quality data for representation learning and use a conditioning variable to
ask for the behaviour you want at test time. Its precondition is that quality is
*labelled*, which is why logging the operator per episode is worth far more than
it looks at collection time — you cannot recover it later.
</KeyIdea>

## Coverage, stated properly

Let $d^{\pi_{\text{deploy}}}(s,a)$ be the distribution the policy will actually
meet and $d^{\mathcal{D}}$ the data's. The condition you want is a support
condition:

$$
\operatorname{supp}\big(d^{\pi_{\text{deploy}}}\big) \subseteq \operatorname{supp}\big(d^{\mathcal{D}}\big)
$$

<Warning>
Two things make this harder than it looks. It is **circular**: the deployment
distribution depends on the policy, which depends on the data — the same
covariate-shift loop, seen from the collection side. And it is **unmeasurable
from the loss**: held-out likelihood is computed on the data distribution, so it
says nothing about regions with no data. A policy can have excellent validation
loss and be extrapolating everywhere it will actually operate.

The practical proxy is a density estimate in a learned embedding space — encode
your demonstrations, encode a sample of deployment observations, and look at the
nearest-neighbour distance from deployment points into the training set. Large
distances mark the extrapolation regions, and they are where to collect next.
</Warning>

## The marginal value of a demonstration

<Derivation title="Why gaps beat density">

Consider the policy's error in a local region containing $n$ demonstrations.
Where the region is supported, the estimator behaves like a local average and
its error falls roughly as

$$
\varepsilon(n) \sim \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\Big|\frac{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}{\mathrm{d}n}\Big| \sim \frac{\sigma}{2 n^{3/2}}
$$

so the marginal value of one more demonstration decays as $n^{-3/2}$ — steeply.

In an *unsupported* region, the network is extrapolating and its error is not
bounded by data at all, only by inductive bias. The first demonstration there
converts an unbounded error into a bounded one.

**The consequence.** The comparison is not between two small improvements; it is
between shaving a fraction off an already-small error and removing an
unconstrained one. This is what justifies gap-directed collection — and it runs
directly against what is cheap, since another session in the same setup is
always the easiest thing to run.

</Derivation>

## Breadth versus depth, mechanically

Why does task count dominate demonstrations per task for generalisation? Because
of what the loss makes cheapest.

With $T$ tasks and one instruction each, the policy can minimise the training
loss by learning a lookup:

$$
\pi(a \mid o, \ell) \approx \pi_{\text{task}(\ell)}(a\mid o),
\qquad \text{task}(\cdot) \text{ a table with } T \text{ entries}
$$

This is *optimal* on the training set and has zero compositional structure. For
decomposition to be the cheaper solution, the same verb must appear with many
objects and the same object with many verbs — so that representing "pick" and
"mug" separately costs $O(V + N)$ parameters instead of $O(VN)$ table entries.

<KeyIdea>
Compositionality is not something the architecture provides; it is what the data
makes economical. That is why the actionable version of "collect more data" is
"vary verbs and objects independently", and why a hundred tasks at fifty
demonstrations beats five tasks at a thousand for anything you want to
generalise.
</KeyIdea>

## Splitting, and the near-duplicate trap

Consecutive frames within an episode are nearly identical, so a per-timestep
random split leaks:

$$
\text{corr}\big(o_t, o_{t+1}\big) \approx 1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{a validation frame's neighbour is in the training set}
$$

Held-out loss then measures interpolation between adjacent frames rather than
generalisation, and it will look excellent while the policy fails.

<Warning>
Split by **episode**, and preferably by **session** — episodes from one sitting
share lighting, object instances, operator state, and hardware calibration, so
an episode-level split still leaks session-level nuisance factors. If you want
validation loss to predict anything about deployment, hold out whole sessions,
and ideally whole operators.
</Warning>
