## The dataset is the specification

In imitation learning there is no reward function saying what good behaviour is.
There is only the data. The policy's ceiling, its failure modes, its
generalisation, and its idiosyncrasies are all properties of what it was shown.

<KeyIdea>
That makes curation the opposite of preprocessing. It is where you decide what
the robot will do — and in most projects it is higher-leverage than the choice of
architecture, because a careful ablation almost always finds that data quality
and observation design move results more than the algorithmic difference being
argued about.
</KeyIdea>

<Intuition>
You are teaching someone a craft by letting them watch. They will absorb your
technique, your shortcuts, your bad habits, and the fact that you only ever work
in good light on a clean bench.

Now imagine they watch three people with different techniques and no way to tell
who is the expert. They will not pick the best one; they will average.
</Intuition>

## Three axes, and they are not interchangeable

<Steps>
<Step title="Coverage">
Which states, objects, poses, lightings, and starting configurations appear.
This is the axis that determines where the policy works at all, and it is
directly the covariate-shift problem seen from the data side.
</Step>

<Step title="Quality">
Operator skill and consistency. Mixed quality is not neutral — it is actively
harmful to cloning, because the loss fits the *mean* of the demonstrated
behaviours, and the mean of two different strategies need not be a strategy.
</Step>

<Step title="Breadth versus depth">
The split between number of distinct tasks and demonstrations per task. This is
where the counterintuitive result lives.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Breadth beats depth for generalisation, and it is not close. BC-Z found zero-shot
success on held-out tasks tracked the *number of distinct training tasks* far
more than the demonstrations per task. The mechanism is economic: with a handful
of tasks, memorising a lookup from instruction to behaviour minimises the loss
perfectly and generalises to nothing. Only when verbs and objects recur in varied
combinations does decomposing the instruction become the cheaper representation.

The caveat is a floor. Below roughly fifty demonstrations a task usually will not
work at all, so top up the thin tasks first and spend the remainder on breadth.
</Warning>

## Curation is a loop, not a filter

<Figure src="/figures/dataset-curation-funnel.svg"
        alt="Raw episodes pass through success, quality, and deduplication filters, shrinking from 2400 to 950. A coverage analysis of the survivors feeds back into the next collection session."
        caption="The output of curation is a collection plan as much as a dataset. Adding more of what you already cover is the cheapest thing to collect and close to the least useful." />

The filters matter, but the feedback arrow is what separates a curated dataset
from a filtered one. After each session, ask what the surviving data is thin on
— which objects appear five times, which lighting conditions never, which
starting poses are absent — and target the next session at those gaps.

<KeyIdea>
Marginal value falls sharply with coverage. The five hundredth demonstration of
a mug on a white table teaches almost nothing; the first demonstration on a
reflective surface may fix a whole failure class. Collection is cheapest where
you already are, which is exactly where it is least useful.
</KeyIdea>

## Failures are not garbage

<Warning>
Remove failed episodes from the *cloning* training set — maximum likelihood on a
failure is teaching the failure. But archive them rather than deleting, because
different consumers want different things: a value function, an offline RL
objective, and a learned success detector all need negative examples and have no
other source for them.

Deleting is an irreversible decision made on behalf of every future use of the
data, taken at the moment you know least about what those uses will be.
</Warning>

## The two quiet mistakes

**Splitting by timestep instead of by episode.** Consecutive frames are near
duplicates, so a random per-timestep split puts near-identical examples in both
train and validation. Validation loss then looks excellent and means nothing —
this is the single most common reason a policy with a beautiful loss curve fails
on the robot.

**Recording only the outcome.** The metadata you did not log — operator,
session, lighting, object instance, hardware revision — is exactly what you will
want when results shift inexplicably three weeks later, and it cannot be
recovered.
