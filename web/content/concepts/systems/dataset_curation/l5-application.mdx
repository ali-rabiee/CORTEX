interview:
  answer: >-
    In imitation learning there's no reward function saying what good behaviour
    is — there's only the data. So the dataset is the specification: the
    policy's ceiling, its failure modes, and its idiosyncrasies are all
    properties of what it was shown. That makes curation the opposite of
    preprocessing; it's where you decide what the robot will do. And that's not
    just a framing — robomimic ran a careful controlled study and found
    demonstration quality and observation choices moved results more than
    several of the algorithmic differences people were arguing about. Which has
    a direct consequence for how you compare methods: if data quality dominates
    the architecture, an uncontrolled comparison is largely measuring which
    method happened to get better demonstrations. There are three axes and
    they're not interchangeable. Coverage — which states, objects, and
    conditions appear — determines where the policy works at all. Quality.
    And the breadth-versus-depth split, where the counterintuitive result lives:
    BC-Z found zero-shot generalisation tracks the number of distinct training
    tasks far more than demonstrations per task. A hundred tasks at fifty demos
    beats five tasks at a thousand. The mechanism is about what the loss makes
    cheapest — with few tasks, memorising a lookup from instruction to behaviour
    is optimal on the training set and generalises to nothing, and only varied
    verb-object combinations make decomposition the economical representation.
    On quality specifically, the thing worth being precise about is that mixed
    quality isn't just noisier. If two operators have different strategies, MSE
    cloning fits the conditional mean over the mixture, so an expert and a
    novice give you the average — which can be worse than either, because the
    midpoint of "left around the object" and "right around it" goes through the
    object. Two fixes with different costs. Filter, which removes the averaging
    and costs you the weak operator's coverage. Or condition: add an operator or
    quality token, train on everything, request the expert at inference. Then
    the conditional mean is taken within an operator rather than across them,
    and the novice data still trains the shared encoder. That has a
    collection-time precondition though — you have to have logged the operator,
    and you can't recover it later.
  followUps:
    - q: "What's the first thing you'd change on a team with a working collection pipeline?"
      a: >-
        The split, and then the metadata. Splitting by timestep leaks
        near-duplicate frames, and it's the single most common reason a
        beautiful validation curve accompanies a policy that doesn't work — but
        even per-episode splits leak, because episodes from one sitting share
        lighting, object instances, calibration, and operator state. I'd hold
        out whole sessions and ideally whole operators, because a policy that
        generalises across sessions but not across operators has learned one
        person's technique and nothing else exposes that. Then metadata:
        operator, session, object instance, lighting, hardware revision, outcome
        and failure category, logged per episode. It costs one struct at
        collection time and it's the only thing that answers "what changed?"
        three weeks later — which is a question you will definitely ask.
    - q: "How do you decide what to collect next?"
      a: >-
        By finding where the policy is extrapolating, which the loss cannot tell
        you. Held-out likelihood is computed on the data distribution, so it's
        structurally silent about regions with no data. What I'd do instead is
        embed the training demonstrations and a sample of deployment
        observations and look at nearest-neighbour distance from deployment into
        training — large distances mark the unsupported regions, and those are
        the next session. The cheap version is just counting episodes per task,
        object, and lighting cell and listing the thin ones. The reason this
        matters is asymmetric marginal value: in a well-covered region the
        error is already small and another demo buys the usual root-n
        improvement, while the first demo in an uncovered region converts an
        unbounded extrapolation error into a bounded one. And that runs directly
        against what's cheap to collect, since another session in the same setup
        is always the easiest thing to run.
    - q: "Should you throw away failed episodes?"
      a: >-
        Remove them from the cloning training set, because maximum likelihood on
        a failure is teaching the failure. But archive rather than delete, and
        I'd push back on anyone doing otherwise. A value function, an offline RL
        objective, and a learned success detector all need negative examples and
        have no other source for them — a success detector in particular is
        usually the weakest component in a closed-loop system, and it's trained
        on exactly this data. Deleting is an irreversible decision taken on
        behalf of every future use of the dataset, at the moment you know least
        about what those uses will be. The same argument applies to aborted
        episodes and to the operator's notes.
    - q: "Does any of this change with a pretrained VLA?"
      a: >-
        The breadth argument gets stronger and the quality argument stays the
        same. Pretraining supplies the semantics — the model already knows what
        a mug is — so your demonstrations only need to teach motor skill, which
        shifts the marginal value further toward covering new *situations*
        rather than repeating the same one. But the fine-tuning data still sets
        the behaviour, so mixed operator quality averages exactly as before, and
        at a few hundred demonstrations the forgetting risk means you're
        constrained to LoRA or co-fine-tuning anyway. The thing that genuinely
        changes is that pooled cross-embodiment data becomes worth contributing
        to — Open X-Embodiment showed heterogeneous pooling gives positive
        transfer across robot bodies, which wasn't obvious, and it means your own
        setup isn't necessarily the right unit of work.
  traps:
    - "Treating curation as preprocessing. In imitation learning the dataset is the specification, and it outweighs the architecture in every careful ablation."
    - "Describing mixed-quality data as merely noisier. MSE fits the mean over the operator mixture, and the mean of two strategies need not be a strategy."
    - "Splitting by timestep, or even by episode. Consecutive frames are near-duplicates and sessions share nuisance factors; hold out sessions and operators."
    - "Deleting failures. They are useless for cloning and are the only negative examples a critic or success detector will ever get."

Note that a correctly-split low validation loss still would not prove much, since it measures accuracy on the expert's state distribution rather than the policy's."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Low validation loss, poor robot performance → check the split">
Per-timestep splits leak near-duplicates. Per-episode splits still leak session
factors. Hold out whole sessions, and operators if you can.
</Step>

<Step title="Policy averages two approaches → mixed operator strategies">
Histogram the actions per dimension conditioned on operator. A bimodal
distribution is the averaging problem visible before it reaches the robot.
Filter or condition.
</Step>

<Step title="Works on trained objects, fails on new ones → breadth, not volume">
More demonstrations of the covered objects will not help. New objects and new
scenes will.
</Step>

<Step title="Results shift with no code change → unlogged metadata">
Hardware revision, operator, lighting, object instance. If it was not logged it
cannot be diagnosed, only guessed at.
</Step>

<Step title="Success detector performs poorly → you deleted its training data">
Failures are the only negative examples available. Archive them.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The framing that carries this answer is that the dataset is the specification —
it makes curation a design activity rather than a chore, and it explains why
every careful ablation finds data dominating the architecture. Follow it with
the two results that actually change decisions (breadth over depth, and quality
outweighing algorithms) and the mechanical account of what mixed quality does to
an MSE loss. That combination is what distinguishes someone who has collected a
dataset from someone who has downloaded one.
</Warning>
