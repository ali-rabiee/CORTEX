## Making collection scalable, and exposing operator variance

<PaperCard refKey="mandlekar2018roboturk" />

RoboTurk's practical contribution was a phone-based teleoperation interface that
let many people contribute demonstrations. Its lasting contribution was making
**operator identity** a variable in the dataset rather than an unmodelled
nuisance — which is the precondition for everything in Level 2 about
conditioning on quality.

## Measuring what actually matters

<PaperCard refKey="mandlekar2021robomimic" />

A controlled study across algorithms, observation spaces, and demonstration
sources, with the finding that demonstration quality and observation design move
results more than several of the algorithmic differences being argued about at
the time.

<KeyIdea>
This is an allocation argument, and it cuts two ways. Spend marginal effort on
data rather than on the fifth architectural variant. And hold data fixed when
comparing methods — an uncontrolled comparison is substantially measuring which
method happened to be trained on better demonstrations, which is the same
pairing discipline that evaluation requires, applied one level up.
</KeyIdea>

## Breadth over depth

<PaperCard refKey="jang2022bcz" />

The result that changes what you go and collect. Zero-shot success on held-out
tasks tracked the number of distinct training tasks far more than the
demonstrations per task — so a hundred tasks at fifty demonstrations beats five
tasks at a thousand, for anything you want to generalise.

The mechanism is about what the loss makes cheapest. With few tasks, a lookup
from instruction to behaviour is optimal on the training set and generalises to
nothing; only varied verb-object combinations make decomposition the economical
representation. It is also, inconveniently, the more annoying thing to collect —
which is part of why it took a while to become standard practice.

## Pooling across domains and bodies

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="ebert2021bridge" />
<PaperCard refKey="oneill2023openx" />
</PaperGrid>

Bridge Data made the case that data from *other* domains and tasks measurably
improves a target task. Open X-Embodiment pushed it much further: 22 embodiments
pooled across many labs, with a single policy beating per-embodiment training.

<Warning>
The non-obvious part is the heterogeneity. Different robots have different
kinematics, action spaces, and camera setups, so interference rather than
transfer was entirely plausible — this is the negative-transfer regime familiar
from multi-task learning. That it helps says the shared structure — visual
scenes, object semantics, manipulation strategy — outweighs the embodiment
differences at this scale. It is a scale-dependent claim, and it is the argument
for contributing to a pooled corpus rather than treating your own setup as the
unit of work.
</Warning>

## Collection as a design problem

<PaperCard refKey="khazatsky2024droid" />

DROID's contribution is the protocol. A standardised rig so that many labs
produce data that actually combines, and deliberate collection in real kitchens,
offices and homes rather than one instrumented lab.

<KeyIdea>
Volume is the easy axis; diversity is the hard one, because diversity requires
physically going somewhere else. A single lab can collect a hundred thousand
episodes and still have one lighting distribution, one floor, one set of object
instances. That constraint is structural, and it is why the standardised-protocol
approach matters more than any individual dataset's size.
</KeyIdea>

## Separating the scaling axes

<PaperCard refKey="lin2024scalinglaws" />

The most useful recent framing: stop treating "more data" as one dial. Separate
the number of *environments*, the number of *objects*, and the number of
demonstrations per environment-object pair — and find that diversity along the
first two matters more than volume along the third.

<Steps>
<Step title="What this tells you to do">
Once a cell is above its floor, additional demonstrations in that cell are the
cheapest and least valuable thing to collect. New environments and new objects
are more expensive and worth more.
</Step>

<Step title="What it does not settle">
Where the floor is for a given task, how the axes trade against each other
quantitatively, and whether the exponents hold outside the tasks studied.
Robotics does not yet have scaling laws in the sense language modelling does —
it has a first careful attempt at asking the question properly.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The honest summary of this whole line is that data curation is the least
glamorous and highest-leverage part of robot learning, and that the field's
published incentives point the other way. A paper reporting "we collected more
carefully" is harder to publish than one reporting a new architecture, which is
exactly why the careful ablations keep finding that data dominates the
architecture.
</Warning>
