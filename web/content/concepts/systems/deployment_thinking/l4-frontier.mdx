## Scale is an infrastructure result

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="levine2016armfarm" />
<PaperCard refKey="kalashnikov2018qtopt" />
</PaperGrid>

Both are cited for their policies and both are, in substance, systems papers.
Fourteen arms running for months, or seven robots for the QT-Opt collection,
means automatic scene reset, detecting a jammed gripper or an object that has
left the workspace, restarting failed jobs, and streaming data reliably enough
that a month of collection is not lost to a corrupt shard.

<KeyIdea>
None of that appears in a method section, and all of it is what made the data
volume possible. Data scale in robotics is bought with systems engineering — the
useful reading of these papers is that the bottleneck was never the learning
algorithm, and that anyone proposing to collect a million real trajectories is
proposing an infrastructure project.
</KeyIdea>

QT-Opt adds a second lesson: training asynchronously against a buffer that is
still growing, with policies deployed to robots while training continues.
Decoupling the collection rate from the training rate is what makes continuous
operation possible, and it introduces its own problem — the data is off-policy
by a lag that varies with how long deployment takes.

## Deployment engineering feeding back into training

<PaperCard refKey="hwangbo2019anymal" />

The actuator network is the clearest example of a deployment measurement
changing the training setup. System identification left a residual well above
sensor noise, which said the simulator's model class was too small — so they
measured the real actuator response and inserted a learned model of it into the
simulator.

<Warning>
The generalisable move is to treat the sim-to-real gap as a measurement rather
than a nuisance. Replay a real trajectory through the simulator from the same
initial state and compare; where the divergence starts tells you which component
is missing. That is a deployment activity producing a training artefact, and it
is much more targeted than widening randomisation ranges until something works.
</Warning>

## When the loop timing is the result

<PaperCard refKey="kaufmann2023swift" />

A physical drone beating human champions on a racing course. The learned policy
is one component of a stack that includes visual-inertial state estimation with
its own latency, a control loop whose margin is measured in tens of
milliseconds, and residual dynamics identified from real flight data rather than
assumed from the simulator.

<KeyIdea>
At those speeds the phase lag from a few tens of milliseconds is a large
fraction of the available margin, so the timing budget is not a detail to be
optimised afterwards — it is the constraint the design is built around. Reading
the result as "RL beat the humans" misses that several non-learned components
each had to be right, which is the general shape of any deployed learned
controller.
</KeyIdea>

## Throughput as a first-class objective

<PaperCard refKey="kim2025oft" />

For large policies, the current form of this concern. The study measures
throughput and success *together* across parallel decoding, chunk length, and
action-head type — treating the frontier between them as the object of interest
rather than reporting speed as an afterthought.

<Steps>
<Step title="Why the joint measurement is the right artefact">
The two trade against each other, so either number alone lets you pick a
flattering operating point. Any policy can be made fast by degrading it. A
deployment has a hard latency constraint and wants the best success rate
available *at* that constraint, which is a point on a frontier, not a headline.
</Step>

<Step title="What it says about where the effort goes">
The gains come from decoding strategy and chunk length — choices entirely
orthogonal to the backbone. That is the same pattern as the careful ablations in
evaluation and data: the factors that dominate outcomes are usually not the ones
the papers are arguing about.
</Step>
</Steps>

## What is still not written down

<Warning>
Deployment is under-published for a structural reason: it is specific to a
system, it does not generalise into a citable claim, and it is where most of the
engineering time actually goes. The consequence for you is that this knowledge
mostly transfers through practice rather than through papers, and that a
candidate who can talk concretely about timestamps, staleness bounds, fallback
behaviours, and configuration fingerprints is signalling something that no
reading list demonstrates.
</Warning>
