## A different objective

A research result maximises the mean success rate on a chosen task suite. A
deployed system is judged on something else entirely: what it does on its worst
day, whether it fails predictably, and whether anyone can tell why.

<Intuition>
A racing car is optimised for lap time on a known circuit with a mechanic
between runs. A road car is optimised for the tail: starting in the cold,
behaving predictably when a tyre deflates, and telling you what is wrong when
something is.

They are both cars, and almost none of the engineering transfers.
</Intuition>

<KeyIdea>
The shift is from *peak* to *tail*. A policy with 85% mean success and a
graceful, detectable failure mode is deployable. One with 92% mean success that
occasionally drives the arm into the table is not, and no amount of improving
the mean changes that.
</KeyIdea>

## Latency is a budget

<Figure src="/figures/deployment-latency-budget.svg"
        alt="Two timing bars across one control period. The typical loop fits sensing, network, inference and actuation inside the period with slack. The p99 loop's inference overruns the deadline, so the previous action is held and the delay for that cycle doubles."
        caption="The loop has a period and the work has to fit inside it. What breaks deployments is not the average — it is the occasional excursion that misses the deadline, at a moment nothing predicted." />

Sense, transport, infer, act. Each has a duration, and their sum has to fit
inside the control period with margin. Two things follow that research code
never has to confront.

<Steps>
<Step title="The p99 is the number, not the mean">
A loop that is usually fast and occasionally slow is worse than one uniformly
slower. The uniform one can be designed around — you tune for its delay and it
is stable. The intermittent one violates that tuning at unpredictable moments,
and the resulting misbehaviour looks like a policy bug rather than a systems
one. Production systems often *cap* inference time deliberately, giving up
speed they could sometimes have in exchange for a bound.
</Step>

<Step title="Delay costs phase margin, and the cost scales with frequency">
A pure delay $\tau$ adds a phase lag of $360 f \tau$ degrees at frequency $f$.
At 2 Hz, 30 ms is 22 degrees and nobody notices. At 5 Hz, 50 ms is 90 degrees —
most of your margin. At 5 Hz, 100 ms is 180 degrees, and a corrective action
arrives exactly in antiphase, which is to say it becomes positive feedback.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
This is why the same latency is irrelevant for a slow reaching motion and fatal
for a contact-rich or dynamic one. "Is 50 ms acceptable?" has no answer without
knowing what bandwidth the task requires — and that is the question to ask
before optimising anything.
</Warning>

## Everything at the seams needs a defined behaviour

Research code assumes each step returns. Deployed code lives somewhere a USB
camera drops frames, a network hiccups, a garbage collector pauses, and a GPU
thermal-throttles at hour six.

<Steps>
<Step title="Stale observation">
The frame did not arrive. Does the policy act on the last one? For how long
before that is unsafe? Acting on a 300 ms old image as though it were current is
the default behaviour of code that never considered it.
</Step>

<Step title="Missed deadline">
Inference overran. Hold the previous action, interpolate, or stop? All three are
defensible; having no answer is not.
</Step>

<Step title="Out-of-distribution input">
The policy will produce an action for anything, confidently. Something has to
notice that the scene is unlike anything in training, and the policy itself is
not that something.
</Step>

<Step title="Degraded mode">
When the loop cannot run — a component died, latency blew out — there must be a
behaviour that is safe. Usually a slow stop and a hold, and it needs to be
tested rather than assumed.
</Step>
</Steps>

## What deployment adds that research skips

<Steps>
<Step title="Determinism and versioning">
Which weights, which preprocessing, which normalisation statistics, which
calibration. A policy is a *tuple* of all of those, and shipping the weights
alone reproduces nothing.
</Step>

<Step title="Monitoring that watches inputs, not just outputs">
Success rate tells you it broke. Input distribution statistics tell you *before*
it broke — the lighting changed, a camera drifted, the object set is different
from last month.
</Step>

<Step title="Recovery, not just success">
The interesting question is never "does it succeed?" but "what happens when it
does not?" A policy that fails by stopping is worth more than one with a higher
success rate that fails by continuing.
</Step>

<Step title="A rollback path">
The new policy is worse in some situation you did not test. Being able to revert
in a minute is worth more than being confident it will not happen.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
Almost none of this is machine learning, and in a deployed robot system almost
all of it is where the effort goes. That is worth saying out loud in an
interview: the question is usually testing whether you have shipped something or
only trained something.
</KeyIdea>
