## Delay, in the frequency domain

A pure transport delay of $\tau$ has the transfer function

$$
G_\tau(j\omega) = e^{-j\omega\tau},
\qquad
|G_\tau| = 1,
\qquad
\angle G_\tau = -\omega\tau
$$

<Derivation title="Why delay is uniquely dangerous, and how to budget for it">

**Step 1 — magnitude one, phase everything.** $|e^{-j\omega\tau}| = 1$ at every
frequency. A delay does not attenuate anything; it shifts a sinusoid in time. So
any analysis looking only at gain sees nothing, and the margin disappears
silently.

**Step 2 — the phase cost grows with frequency.** In degrees,

$$
\phi(f) = \omega\tau \cdot \frac{180}{\pi} = 360\, f\, \tau
$$

The same delay in seconds is a larger fraction of a cycle at higher frequency.
This is why 50 ms is irrelevant for a slow reach and destabilising for a
contact-rich task — the bandwidths differ.

**Step 3 — the stability condition.** A negative-feedback loop is unstable if
the total phase lag reaches 180° while the loop gain is still above unity. If
the rest of the loop already consumes some phase, leaving a margin $\phi_m$ at
the crossover frequency $f_c$, the delay may consume at most $\phi_m$:

$$
\boxed{\;\tau_{\max} = \frac{\phi_m}{360\, f_c}\ \text{seconds}\;}
$$

**Numbers.** With a 45° margin: at $f_c = 1$ Hz, 125 ms. At 3 Hz, 42 ms. At
10 Hz, 12.5 ms.

**The assumption doing the work** is that $\tau$ is a *pure* delay and constant.
A varying delay is worse than its mean — the loop is being analysed at an
operating point it keeps leaving — which is the formal reason jitter deserves
more attention than average latency.

</Derivation>

<Warning>
$\tau$ is the **whole loop**: sensor exposure and readout, transport,
preprocessing, inference, command transmission, and actuator response.
Inference is usually the largest single term and rarely more than half the
total. A team that halves its model's inference time and finds the loop barely
improved has usually never measured the other terms.
</Warning>

## Deadline misses come from the tail

Let $L$ be the loop latency, a random variable, and $T$ the control period. Then

$$
P(\text{miss}) = P(L > T)
$$

and at control rate $f = 1/T$ the expected misses per hour are
$3600 f\, P(L > T)$.

<KeyIdea>
The mean of $L$ appears nowhere in that expression. A distribution with a 40 ms
median and a 250 ms p99, against a 100 ms period, misses at least 1% of cycles —
360 per hour at 10 Hz. Reporting mean inference time tells you nothing about
whether the loop will hold, which is why deployment reports percentiles and
often *caps* inference deliberately, giving up speed it could sometimes have in
exchange for a bound.
</KeyIdea>

The cost of a miss compounds. Holding the previous action for one extra period
means that cycle's effective delay is $2T$ rather than $T$, so the phase lag for
that cycle doubles — at exactly the moment the controller was tuned for the
nominal value.

## What action chunking actually trades

Predict $H$ actions and execute $T_a$ of them before re-planning. The amortised
inference cost per executed action is

$$
\bar c = \frac{c_{\text{infer}}}{T_a}
$$

which is how a policy that cannot run at control rate is made to run at control
rate.

<Derivation title="The reactivity cost, quantified">

Between re-plans, the policy is open loop for $T_a$ steps, so the age of the
observation the current action was computed from ranges over
$[\,\tau_0,\ \tau_0 + (T_a-1)T\,]$, with mean

$$
\bar\tau_{\text{eff}} = \tau_0 + \frac{(T_a - 1)\,T}{2}
$$

**Read it as a trade.** Chunking divides the *compute* per action by $T_a$ and
adds $(T_a-1)T/2$ to the *average staleness*. At 10 Hz with $T_a = 8$, that is
350 ms of added mean delay — which by the phase formula is 105° of lag at 1 Hz.

**So chunking does not remove the latency problem; it moves it.** You buy
throughput and pay in reactivity, and the exchange rate is set by $T_a$. That is
why the right response to "the policy lags a moving target" is to reduce $T_a$,
not to make the model faster: the model was never the binding term.

</Derivation>

## Jitter as a queueing problem

If inference is served asynchronously and the control loop reads the most recent
available action, the system is a queue. Two regimes matter.

<Steps>
<Step title="Utilisation below one, bounded queue">
Mean inference time under the period. Waiting time still grows sharply as
utilisation $\rho = \bar c / T$ approaches 1 — the standard queueing result — so
running at 90% utilisation is not "10% of headroom", it is a regime where small
perturbations produce large waits.
</Step>

<Step title="Utilisation above one, unbounded growth">
Mean inference time above the period. The queue grows without bound and the
action being executed becomes arbitrarily stale. The fix is to *drop* stale
requests rather than queue them: a control system wants the newest answer, not
the oldest pending one.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The default behaviour of a naive async wrapper is to queue, which is exactly
backwards for control. Under load, a queue delivers increasingly old actions
while reporting healthy throughput. Dropping instead — keep only the most recent
request, discard the rest — degrades gracefully and makes the staleness bounded
by construction.
</Warning>

## The tuple that is actually your policy

$$
\text{policy} = \big(\theta,\ \text{preprocessing},\ \text{normalisation stats},\ \text{calibration},\ \text{action decode},\ \text{control params}\big)
$$

Every one of these can change independently, and a mismatch in any of them
produces behaviour that looks like a model regression. Normalisation statistics
computed on a different dataset revision, a camera recalibrated last week, a
gripper firmware update that changed the command scaling — all present as "the
new policy is worse".

<KeyIdea>
Version the tuple, not the weights, and record its hash with every rollout. This
is the deployment analogue of freezing an evaluation protocol: the point is that
when two results disagree, you can establish in one second whether they were
produced by the same system.
</KeyIdea>
