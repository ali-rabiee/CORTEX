  prompt: "Give your interview answer on deploying a robot policy. What is the shift in objective, and what would you build first?"
  keyPoints:
    - "The shift is from peak to tail: research maximises mean success, deployment optimises the worst case, the recovery, and the predictability."
    - "Quote latency as a budget with a percentile: $\\tau_{\\max} = \\phi_m/(360 f_c)$, and the p99 rather than the mean, since misses come from the tail."
    - "Name what has an undefined behaviour: stale observation, missed deadline, OOD input, dead component. Each needs a decided response, and blocking is never one."
    - "Version the whole tuple — weights, preprocessing, normalisation stats, calibration, decode and control parameters — because 'the new policy is worse' is usually not the weights."
interview:
  answer: >-
    The shift I'd lead with is that research optimises the peak and deployment
    optimises the tail. A research result is a mean success rate on a chosen
    task suite. A deployed system is judged on what it does on its worst day,
    whether it fails predictably, and whether anyone can tell why — so a policy
    at eighty-five percent with a graceful detectable failure mode is
    deployable, and one at ninety-two that occasionally drives the arm into the
    table is not, however much you improve the mean. Concretely, three things.
    First, latency is a budget rather than a number, and the percentile matters
    more than the mean. A pure delay has unit magnitude and pure phase, so it
    adds 360 times f times tau degrees of lag at frequency f — which means at
    5 Hz, 50 milliseconds costs 90 degrees and 100 milliseconds costs 180, where
    a corrective action arrives in antiphase and becomes positive feedback. If I
    know the crossover frequency and the phase margin I need, the delay budget
    is margin over 360 f, and that's the *whole* loop: exposure, transport,
    preprocessing, inference, command, actuator. Inference is usually the
    largest single term and rarely more than half, so teams that only optimise
    the model often find the loop barely improved. And the tail is what breaks
    you — a forty millisecond median with a 250 millisecond p99 against a 100
    millisecond period misses at least one percent of cycles, which is hundreds
    per hour, and each miss holds a stale action and doubles that cycle's delay.
    Production systems often cap inference deliberately, giving up speed they
    could sometimes have in exchange for a bound. Second, everything at the
    seams needs a defined behaviour. Stale observation, missed deadline,
    out-of-distribution input, dead component. Research code assumes each step
    returns; deployed code lives where a camera drops frames and a GC pauses.
    The one answer that's never acceptable is blocking, because that turns a
    latency problem into unbounded loss of control. Third, version the whole
    tuple, not the checkpoint — weights, preprocessing, normalisation
    statistics, calibration, decode parameters, control parameters. The most
    expensive bug I've seen described is "the new policy is worse" where the
    weights never changed and the normalisation stats were recomputed on a newer
    dataset revision.
  followUps:
    - q: "Your policy can't run at the control rate. What are the options, in order?"
      a: >-
        First I'd measure where the time actually goes, because inference is
        usually the largest term and rarely the majority — exposure, transport,
        and preprocessing are often half the loop and nobody has looked. Then
        action chunking, which amortises one inference over several executed
        steps and is the standard answer. But it's worth being precise that
        chunking doesn't remove the latency problem, it moves it: executing
        T_a actions open-loop adds roughly T_a minus one over two, times the
        period, to the average observation staleness, so at 10 Hz with a chunk
        of eight that's 350 milliseconds of added mean delay. You've bought
        throughput and paid in reactivity. That's also why the right response to
        "the policy lags a moving target" is to reduce T_a rather than speed up
        the model — the model was never the binding term. After that it's the
        ordinary levers: quantisation, a smaller vision encoder, fewer camera
        views, and for a token-decoding policy, parallel decoding or chunk
        compression.
    - q: "How would you run inference asynchronously without making things worse?"
      a: >-
        Drop, don't queue, and that's the part people get wrong. A queue is
        right for a job system and wrong for control: under load it delivers
        increasingly stale actions while throughput still looks healthy. Keeping
        only the newest pending request bounds staleness by construction, so the
        degradation shows up as a lower effective control rate rather than as a
        growing lie. The control thread then never blocks — it reads the
        freshest available action, and if there isn't one recent enough it takes
        a defined fallback: hold within a bounded staleness window, controlled
        stop beyond it. And I'd stamp results with the *observation's* timestamp
        rather than the completion time, because staleness has to be measured
        from when the world was seen — stamping at completion makes a slow
        inference look fresh, which is exactly backwards.
    - q: "What would you monitor in production?"
      a: >-
        Inputs before outputs. A success rate tells you it broke; input
        statistics tell you before it broke. So I'd track an
        out-of-distribution score — nearest-neighbour distance from the current
        observation embedding into the training set — plus cheap image
        statistics like brightness and saturated fraction, and alert on drift.
        Those catch the lighting changing, a camera drifting, or the object set
        being different from last month, which are the realistic causes of a
        slow degradation. On the systems side, deadline miss rate and the
        latency percentiles, because both are leading indicators that don't show
        up in success rate until after they've cost something. And I'd make sure
        rollback takes under a minute, because the new policy will eventually be
        worse in a situation nobody tested and reverting quickly is worth more
        than confidence that it won't happen.
    - q: "How do you test the failure paths?"
      a: >-
        By executing them, not by writing them. A fallback that has never been
        triggered is a hypothesis. So: unplug the camera while it's running,
        kill the inference process mid-episode, inject 500 milliseconds of
        artificial latency, feed it a frame from a completely different scene.
        Each one should produce the behaviour I decided on, and in my experience
        at least one won't the first time — usually because something upstream
        blocks rather than timing out, or a staleness check is comparing against
        the wrong clock. I'd also want the staleness bound itself derived rather
        than picked: it should come from the task's bandwidth, since that's what
        determines how quickly an old observation stops being a defensible basis
        for action.
  traps:
    - "Reporting mean inference time. Deadline misses come from the tail, and the mean appears nowhere in $P(L > T)$."
    - "Treating latency as an inference problem. It is the whole loop, and inference is rarely more than half of it."
    - "Presenting action chunking as removing the latency problem. It trades throughput for observation staleness, and the trade should be quantified."
    - "Versioning the checkpoint rather than the configuration tuple. Normalisation statistics, calibration, and decode parameters all change behaviour and all present as a model regression."

The mean does not determine $P(L > T)$, so 45 ms average is consistent with regular deadline misses if the distribution has a tail — and it usually does, from GC pauses, cache misses, and thermal throttling. And the 50 ms period has to cover the whole loop; if exposure and transport are another 30 ms, the budget is already gone. The evaluation protocol is a reasonable second check for the same reason, and it is also something you should have frozen."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Intermittently unstable, fine most of the time → the latency tail">
Check p99 and the deadline-miss rate, not the mean. A uniformly slower loop can
be tuned for; an intermittently slow one violates the tuning at random moments.
</Step>

<Step title="Sluggish, lags a moving target → chunk length, not model speed">
Reactivity is set by how often you look at the world, which is $T_a$. Making the
model faster does not change it.
</Step>

<Step title="Oscillates in contact → phase margin consumed by delay">
Compute $360 f\tau$ at the task's bandwidth. If it is approaching the margin,
the loop is the problem and no policy change will fix it.
</Step>

<Step title="Worse after an update with no model change → the configuration tuple">
Normalisation statistics, calibration, decode parameters, firmware. Fingerprint
the tuple and compare.
</Step>

<Step title="Degrades slowly over weeks → input drift">
Lighting, camera pose, object set. Monitor input statistics and OOD distance;
the success rate notices last.
</Step>

<Step title="Freezes rather than failing → something blocks">
A blocking call somewhere in the loop. Every wait needs a timeout and every
timeout needs a defined next action.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
This concept is asked to find out whether you have shipped something or only
trained something, so the useful register is concrete rather than architectural.
Timestamps on every input. A staleness bound derived from the task bandwidth. A
p99 rather than a mean. Drop rather than queue. A configuration fingerprint on
every log line. Failure paths that have actually been executed. None of it is
machine learning, and in a deployed robot system almost all of the effort goes
there — saying so plainly is itself the signal.
</Warning>
