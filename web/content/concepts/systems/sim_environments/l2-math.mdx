## The budget

Wall-clock for a run is

$$
T_{\text{wall}} = \frac{N_{\text{steps}}}{n_{\text{env}} \cdot f}, \qquad
f \;\propto\; \frac{1}{n_{\text{substep}} \cdot n_{\text{iter}} \cdot c_{\text{model}}}
$$

Every fidelity knob divides $f$, and $N_{\text{steps}}$ is what your algorithm
needs to learn. For a fixed budget the two are exchangeable: doubling substeps
halves the samples. There is no setting at which fidelity is free, and the useful
question is which side of the trade buys more on the task at hand.

| configuration | throughput | $10^9$ steps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 env, contact-rich | $2\times10^3$/s | 5.8 days |
| 1 env, simple | $2\times10^4$/s | 13.9 h |
| 32 CPU envs | $3\times10^5$/s | 55 min |
| 4096 GPU envs | $5\times10^5$/s | 33 min |

## What a batch is actually worth

Transitions within an episode are not independent. Model the per-sample gradient
contributions along a trajectory as AR(1) with correlation $\rho$.

<Derivation title="Effective sample size, and why short rollouts help">

For the mean of $T$ correlated samples,

$$
\mathrm{Var}\!\left[\bar{X}_T\right] = \frac{\sigma^2}{T}\underbrace{\left(1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{T-1}\left(1 - \tfrac{k}{T}\right)\rho^{\,k}\right)}_{\text{inflation factor } \kappa(T)}
$$

so a rollout of length $T$ is worth $T/\kappa(T)$ independent samples. As
$T \to \infty$ the sum converges:

$$
\kappa(\infty) = 1 + 2\sum_{k \ge 1} \rho^{\,k} = \frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}
$$

**The assumption doing the work is stationarity of the correlation structure** —
real trajectories have phases with different $\rho$, so this is a model rather
than a measurement. What it captures correctly is the shape: $\kappa$ grows
towards a ceiling set by $\rho$, and a short rollout never reaches it.

| $\rho$ | $T=4$ | $T=16$ | $T=256$ | $T \to \infty$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.9 | 3.52 | 9.83 | 18.30 | 19.0 |
| 0.99 | 3.95 | 15.18 | 127.6 | 199.0 |

With $n_{\text{env}}$ independent environments the total effective count is
$n_{\text{env}} T / \kappa(T)$. At a fixed batch of $65{,}536$ and $\rho = 0.99$:

$$
1 \times 65536 \to 330, \qquad 256 \times 256 \to 514, \qquad 16384 \times 4 \to 16590
$$

A fiftyfold gain in independent information for the same collected transitions.

</Derivation>

<KeyIdea>
Note what did *not* happen: the correlation is unchanged. Short rollouts simply
stop paying for its tail, and the environments supply independence for free. This
is the real reason massively parallel RL changed hyperparameters rather than just
schedules — a four-step rollout would be an obviously bad idea if transitions
were independent, and is a good one precisely because they are not.
</KeyIdea>

## What short rollouts cost

Cutting a rollout at $T$ means bootstrapping from the critic rather than
observing the return, so the critic's error enters the advantage.

<Derivation title="Critic dependence grows as the horizon shrinks">

A $T$-step truncated GAE advantage terminates in $(\gamma\lambda)^T V(s_{t+T})$,
so a value error $\varepsilon$ contributes

$$
\text{bias} \;\approx\; (\gamma\lambda)^{T}\,\varepsilon
$$

With $\gamma = 0.99$, $\lambda = 0.95$:

| $T$ | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 | 64 | 128 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $(\gamma\lambda)^T$ | 0.78 | 0.61 | 0.37 | 0.14 | 0.020 | 0.0004 |

At $T = 4$, 78% of the critic's error passes straight into the advantage. At
$T = 64$, 2%.

</Derivation>

<Warning>
So the two effects pull in opposite directions, and the horizon is a real choice
rather than a knob to maximise. Massively parallel setups get their statistical
advantage precisely in the regime where they depend most on the value function —
which is why they are sensitive to value-loss weighting and critic capacity in a
way that long-rollout setups are not, and why "it worked at $T=64$ and broke at
$T=8$" usually points at the critic rather than at the horizon itself.
</Warning>

## The timestep is set by the stiffest thing in the scene

From the explicit-integrator bound $\Delta t < 2\sqrt{m/k}$, and cost linear in
$1/\Delta t$:

| material | $k$ | max $\Delta t$ | physics steps per simulated second |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| soft rubber | $10^4$ | 20 ms | 50 |
| hard plastic | $10^6$ | 2 ms | 500 |
| aluminium | $10^8$ | 200 µs | 5000 |
| steel | $10^9$ | 63 µs | 15811 |

Honest steel costs **32×** hard plastic for the same simulated second — one stiff
object in the scene sets the price for everything. That is the throughput reason
simulated contact is softened, which sits alongside the numerical one.
