interview:
  answer: >-
    The framing I'd start with is that a simulator isn't an approximate copy of
    the world you evaluate by closeness — it's a program with a cost model. Every
    fidelity knob, smaller timestep, more solver iterations, better contact, is
    priced in wall-clock, and that comes out of the same budget as the samples
    your algorithm needs. So fidelity and sample count are exchangeable, and the
    question is always which side buys more on this task. Then the thing I find
    people usually have backwards: why simulation moved onto the GPU. The usual
    answer is that GPUs do physics faster, which is partly true and isn't the
    binding constraint. If you keep physics on the CPU and the policy on the GPU,
    every step ships observations one way and actions back. At four thousand
    environments and ten to the four steps per second that's about twenty-four
    gigabytes a second, against roughly twenty-five achievable over PCIe. At
    sixteen thousand it's ninety-four. So the link was the ceiling, and no
    kernel-level optimisation raises it — what Isaac Gym did was put state,
    physics and policy all in device memory so the transfer stops existing. Brax
    went further and compiled the whole rollout and update as one XLA program.
    And then the part I think is genuinely interesting, which is that parallelism
    isn't only faster, it changes the statistics. Transitions within an episode
    are strongly correlated, so a long rollout has far fewer independent samples
    than transitions. If you model that as AR-one, the variance inflation factor
    tends to one plus rho over one minus rho — a hundred and ninety-nine at rho
    equals point nine nine. But that's the limit for a long rollout. A four-step
    fragment only pays the first few terms of the sum, so the factor is under
    four. Same sixty-five thousand transitions: one environment gives you an
    effective sample size around three hundred and thirty, sixteen thousand
    environments gives you sixteen and a half thousand. Roughly fifty times the
    independent information for the same collection. That's why Rudin and
    colleagues found the hyperparameters had to change — very short rollouts are
    correct in that regime rather than a compromise, and they'd be an obviously
    bad idea if transitions were independent. There's a real cost though, and I'd
    volunteer it: bootstrapping over a short horizon means gamma-lambda to the T
    of the critic's error lands in the advantage. At T equals four that's
    seventy-eight percent; at sixty-four it's two. So massive parallelism buys
    you sample independence and makes you much more dependent on the value
    function, which is why those setups are sensitive to critic quality in a way
    long-rollout ones aren't.
  followUps:
    - q: "How would you decide between more fidelity and more samples?"
      a: >-
        I'd sweep it rather than argue about it, because the exchange rate is
        measurable — run the same budget at a few settings of substeps and solver
        iterations and print steps per second next to samples affordable, so the
        trade is visible instead of implicit. On the fidelity side I'd only pay
        for things the policy can actually exploit. Penetration the policy leans
        on is worth fixing; penetration you can see in the viewer that never
        changes behaviour is not. And I'd check whether the knob addresses the
        right problem: solver iterations improve constraint accuracy and don't
        relax the timestep stability bound, so if the issue is instability rather
        than penetration, iterations are the wrong knob and you'll pay linearly
        for nothing.
    - q: "What sets the timestep?"
      a: >-
        The stiffest contact in the scene, through the explicit-integrator bound
        delta-t under two root m over k. Hard plastic allows about two
        milliseconds; steel demands sixty-three microseconds. Since cost is
        linear in one over delta-t, that's a factor of thirty-two in compute for
        the same simulated second — one stiff object sets the price for
        everything else. Which is the throughput reason simulated contact gets
        softened, sitting alongside the numerical one, and it's worth knowing
        because that softening is a specific sim2real failure: the policy learns
        against something that yields where the real part doesn't.
    - q: "Same seed, different results at 2048 versus 4096 environments. Bug?"
      a: >-
        No, and it's worth knowing why. Batch shape changes kernel launch
        geometry and therefore reduction order, and float32 addition isn't
        associative, so the arithmetic differs in the last bits and training
        amplifies that. The practical consequence is concrete: the environment
        count is part of the experiment's configuration, not a throughput setting
        you can adjust freely. If you compare a baseline at 2048 against a
        treatment at 4096 you've confounded the method with the count, and that's
        a bias no number of seeds removes. So it goes in the run record next to
        the seed.
    - q: "How would you benchmark a simulator you're evaluating?"
      a: >-
        End-to-end steps per second with the policy in the loop, because that's
        the number that divides into the sample budget — physics-only benchmarks
        are how people conclude their simulator is fast while their training is
        slow, and they hide exactly the round-trip cost that matters. And I'd
        synchronise CUDA around the timing region, since launches are
        asynchronous and timing without it measures how fast you can enqueue
        work. That's a large, stable number unrelated to anything, and it's
        behind most too-good-to-be-true GPU benchmarks. Then I'd scale the
        environment count and look for where throughput stops rising, which tells
        you which wall you've hit.

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Throughput stops scaling with env count → a bandwidth wall">
Compute limits degrade gradually; a bus limit flattens. Check whether anything
crosses to host memory per step.
</Step>

<Step title="Suspiciously high benchmark numbers → missing synchronisation">
CUDA launches are asynchronous. Without `synchronize()` you are timing the
enqueue.
</Step>

<Step title="Unstable after increasing parallelism → the critic">
Short rollouts pass most of the value error into the advantage. Raise the value
loss weight or lengthen the horizon before reverting.
</Step>

<Step title="Odd behaviour near the horizon → truncation treated as termination">
The critic has learned that time running out is worth nothing. Bootstrap at the
time limit, from the pre-reset state.
</Step>

<Step title="Policy exploits penetration → the timestep, not the solver">
Iterations improve constraint accuracy and do not relax $\Delta t < 2\sqrt{m/k}$.
Diagnose which one you actually have.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
What makes this answer strong is that all three claims are quantitative and none
of them is the expected one. GPU simulation is about the transfer, not the
physics. Parallelism changes the statistics, not just the clock. And the benefit
comes with a specific, computable cost in critic dependence. Volunteering that
last one — the price of the thing you just advocated — is usually what separates
having used a tool from having reasoned about it.
</Warning>
