## A program with a price list

<Intuition>
It is tempting to think of a simulator as an approximate copy of the world, and
to evaluate it by how close the copy is. The more useful view for someone
training policies is that it is a program with a cost model. Every knob that
improves fidelity — a smaller timestep, more solver iterations, better contact —
is priced in wall-clock time, and that time comes out of the same budget as the
samples your algorithm needs. What makes a simulator good for learning is not
maximum fidelity but the best fidelity per unit of throughput, on the axes your
task actually depends on.
</Intuition>

Start with the budget. A locomotion policy might need $10^9$ environment steps.
At 2000 steps/s in a single contact-rich environment that is **5.8 days**. At
$5\times10^5$ steps/s across 4096 parallel environments it is **35 minutes**.
Nothing about the algorithm changed; the experiment went from a weekly cadence to
a coffee break.

## The two walls

<Figure src="/figures/sim-parallelism-walls.svg" alt="Left: bandwidth needed for a CPU-physics, GPU-policy round trip rises past the PCIe ceiling between 4096 and 16384 environments. Right: for a fixed batch of 65,536 transitions at correlation 0.99, the effective sample size rises from 330 with one environment to 16,590 with 16,384." caption="The transfer is what forced simulation onto the GPU — and once there, the same batch turned out to be statistically worth much more." />

The first is the one people miss. Keeping physics on the CPU and the policy on
the GPU means every step ships observations one way and actions back. At 4096
environments and $10^4$ steps/s that is 23.6 GB/s, against roughly 25 GB/s
achievable over PCIe 4.0 ×16. At 16384 it is 94 GB/s — not a tuning problem.
Putting state, physics and policy all in device memory removes the transfer
rather than optimising it.

<KeyIdea>
The second wall is a benefit in disguise. Consecutive steps within an episode are
strongly correlated, so a long rollout contains far fewer independent samples
than transitions. Collecting the same 65,536 transitions across 16,384
environments instead of one raises the effective sample size from about 330 to
about 16,590. Parallelism does not merely produce batches faster — it produces
*better* batches, which is why it changed hyperparameters and not just schedules.
</KeyIdea>

## What sets the timestep

<Steps>
<Step title="The stiffest contact in the scene">
Explicit stability needs $\Delta t < 2\sqrt{m/k}$. Hard plastic allows 2 ms;
steel demands 63 µs.
</Step>

<Step title="And cost is linear in $1/\Delta t$">
So honest steel costs **32×** hard plastic for the same simulated second. This is
why simulated contact is softened, and it is a throughput decision before it is a
physics one.
</Step>

<Step title="Control runs slower than physics">
A 50 Hz policy against a 1 kHz simulation means 20 substeps per action, with the
action held across them. That ratio is a design choice with consequences for what
the policy can react to.
</Step>

<Step title="Solver iterations buy constraint accuracy, not stability">
More iterations reduce constraint violation and penetration. They cost linearly
and do not relax the timestep bound, so they are the cheaper knob to reach for
when contact looks wrong.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
A GPU simulator's results generally depend on the number of environments, because
kernel configurations and reduction orders change with batch shape. A run at 4096
environments is not bitwise reproducible at 2048, so the environment count is
part of the experiment's configuration and belongs in the run record — not a
throughput setting you can adjust between a baseline and a treatment.
</Warning>
