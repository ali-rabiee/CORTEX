  prompt: "Give your interview answer on point cloud processing. What is the constraint, and what does the standard solution cost?"
  keyPoints:
    - "A cloud is a set, so the function must be permutation invariant — that constraint determines the architecture."
    - "Max-pooling delivers it and bounds the influential subset by the bottleneck width — measured 9.1% of points, 512/512 unchanged."
    - "Robustness and blindness are the same fact, which is why attention-based aggregators help."
    - "Sensor density falls as $1/Z^2$, so neither fixed radius nor fixed k is scale-consistent."
interview:
  answer: >-
    The thing that makes point clouds different from images is that a cloud is a
    set. There's no grid, no canonical ordering, and the number of points varies
    — so any function of it has to give the same answer when you shuffle the
    rows. That rules out convolution, whose weight sharing assumes every element
    has the same relative neighbours on a lattice. Permutation invariance isn't a
    nice property here, it's a hard constraint, and it determines the
    architecture. The standard answer, from PointNet, is to embed each point
    independently and then combine them with a symmetric function — an
    elementwise max. That's genuinely invariant. What I find more interesting is
    what it costs, because it's provable rather than empirical. If the global
    descriptor is K independent maxima, each one is attained at exactly one
    point, so at most K points can influence the output no matter how many you
    feed in. I measured this on a trained four-class model with a
    two-hundred-fifty-six-dimensional bottleneck and a thousand-and-twenty-four
    points: the mean critical set was ninety-three point six points, about nine
    percent. Keeping only those and discarding the rest left the prediction
    unchanged in five hundred and twelve out of five hundred and twelve test
    clouds, and deleting ninety percent of the non-critical points also changed
    nothing in all of them. And I'd read that twice, because it's the same fact
    in two moods. It's a robustness result — the network is immune to losing most
    of its input, which is exactly what you want from a sparse noisy sensor. And
    it's a blindness result — ninety-one percent of the cloud provably cannot
    affect the answer, so anything that only shows up there is unreachable, not
    just hard. That's the principled reason attention-based aggregators like
    Point Transformer help: a weighted sum lets every neighbour contribute, so
    the bound doesn't apply. It's a property of the aggregator, not of point
    clouds. The other thing I'd raise is that the data is radically non-uniform.
    A depth camera has fixed angular resolution, so point spacing grows linearly
    with range and areal density falls as one over Z squared. For a
    six-forty-by-four-eighty camera with a sixty-degree field of view, a
    two-centimetre neighbourhood holds about five thousand two hundred points at
    thirty centimetres and nineteen points at five metres. So a fixed radius
    means completely different things in different parts of one scene — and a
    fixed k doesn't fix it either, it just moves the problem, because holding k
    at thirty-two spans one point six millimetres near and twenty-six
    millimetres far.
  followUps:
    - q: "So which do you pick, fixed radius or fixed k?"
      a: >-
        It follows from what the feature means, and I'd resist a global default.
        If the feature describes a physical scale — a normal, a curvature, an
        FPFH descriptor — then the radius is the thing that has to stay
        meaningful, and you accept that the sample count varies. If it's a
        learned descriptor that expects a fixed input size, you hold k and accept
        that it's describing a different physical extent in the far field. The
        cleaner move is usually to voxel downsample first so density is uniform,
        and then the parameter means one thing everywhere. That costs you the
        near-field resolution you paid for, which is a real trade rather than a
        free fix.
    - q: "How would you check what your point cloud model is actually using?"
      a: >-
        Extract the critical set — it's two lines, an argmax over the per-point
        features, and unlike most attribution methods it's exact rather than
        approximate, because those points are literally the only ones reaching
        the classifier. Then plot them over the cloud. If they're sitting on the
        table rather than the object, the model has found a shortcut and the
        accuracy number is answering a different question. It's the same
        diagnostic role as a channel ablation for a policy: high accuracy plus a
        model that isn't looking at the object is the failure that survives every
        aggregate metric.
    - q: "When would you use point clouds over images for a policy?"
      a: >-
        When demonstration count is the binding constraint, which it usually is
        in robot learning. The argument is about what the model doesn't have to
        learn: geometry is measured directly by a depth sensor and expensive to
        infer from pixels, so supplying it explicitly removes a whole thing from
        the learning problem. It's the same category of move as delta actions or
        a canonical object frame — decide which variation you supply and which
        the model acquires from data. Against that, you inherit the depth
        sensor's failure modes, and those are worse than people expect: holes on
        transparent and specular surfaces, multi-path in corners, and quadratic
        error growth with range on stereo.
    - q: "Does the canonical frame matter?"
      a: >-
        Quite a lot, and for the same reason. If you hand the encoder raw sensor
        coordinates it has to learn that the same object at a different location
        is the same object, which is a large invariance to acquire from data for
        no benefit. Centring on the centroid and scaling by the extent supplies
        it directly. The wrinkle is that scaling throws away metric size, which
        is sometimes exactly what you want — a toy cup and a real cup have the
        same normalised shape — and sometimes the thing you needed. When size is
        task-relevant I'd pass the scale factor back in as an explicit feature
        rather than leaving it entangled with shape in the coordinates.
  traps:
    - "Treating permutation invariance as a design preference. It is a constraint that rules out convolution outright."
    - "Presenting the critical-set result only as robustness. The same argument bounds what the architecture can see."
    - "Assuming a fixed neighbourhood parameter is scale-consistent. Density varies by a factor of a few hundred across one scene."
    - "Reaching for attention because transformers work well. The reason here is specific — a weighted sum removes the single-winner bound."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Accurate but fooled by a distractor → check the critical set">
Two lines of argmax, and it is exact. Points on context rather than the object
means a shortcut.
</Step>

<Step title="Features clean near, noisy far → density, not sensor noise">
A fixed radius holds a few hundred times fewer points at range. Downsample to
uniform density first.
</Step>

<Step title="Model ignores fine detail entirely → the aggregator">
A single global max bounds what can reach the output. This is architectural, not
a capacity problem.
</Step>

<Step title="Same object at a new location fails → no canonical frame">
The encoder is spending capacity on position. Centre and scale, and keep the
transform.
</Step>

<Step title="Holes on glass or dark surfaces → the sensor, not the pipeline">
Depth failure modes propagate into every downstream stage. Handle missing data
explicitly rather than treating absence as empty space.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The strongest move in this answer is deriving the critical-set bound rather than
citing it — one sentence about $K$ maxima each attained at one point, and it
follows. It converts "PointNet is robust to missing points" into a statement with
a consequence, and the consequence is the interesting half: the architecture
cannot see most of its input. Being able to say why attention fixes that, in
terms of the aggregator's sensitivity rather than in terms of transformers being
good, is what separates having used these models from having read about them.
</Warning>
