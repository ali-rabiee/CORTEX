## A set, not a grid

<Intuition>
An image is a grid: every pixel has the same relative neighbours, which is what
lets a convolution share weights. A point cloud has none of that. It is an
unordered collection of coordinates, of varying size, with no canonical ordering
— so any function of it must give the same answer when you shuffle the rows.
Permutation invariance is not a nice property to have here; it is a hard
constraint that determines the architecture.
</Intuition>

The standard solution is to compute a feature per point independently, then
combine them with a symmetric function — a max. That is genuinely invariant, and
it has a consequence people underuse.

## The output sees a fraction of the input

<Figure src="/figures/pointcloud-critical-set-density.svg" alt="Left: a point cloud with a small subset highlighted, annotated that 93.6 of 1024 points determine the output on average and that keeping only those leaves the prediction unchanged in all 512 test clouds. Right: points inside a 2 cm radius against range, falling from 5215 at 0.3 m to 19 at 5 m." caption="The max-pool bottleneck makes most of the cloud provably irrelevant, and the sensor makes density vary by two orders of magnitude across a scene." />

If the global feature is an elementwise max over $K$ dimensions, then each
dimension is decided by exactly one point. So **at most $K$ points can influence
the output**, regardless of how many you feed in.

Trained on a four-class shape task with $K = 256$ and 1024 input points:

| measurement | result |
| --- | --- |
| mean critical-set size | **93.6 of 1024 (9.1%)** |
| prediction unchanged keeping only the critical set | **512 / 512** |
| prediction unchanged after deleting 90% of the rest | **512 / 512** |

<KeyIdea>
Read this twice, because it is the same fact in two moods. It is a *robustness*
result — the network is immune to losing most of its input, which matters for
sparse and noisy sensors. And it is a *blindness* result — 91% of the cloud
cannot affect the answer, so anything that shows up only there is invisible.
Fine detail, small objects in a large scene, and subtle geometry are structurally
out of reach for this architecture, not merely hard.
</KeyIdea>

## The sensor makes the data non-uniform

<Steps>
<Step title="Angular resolution is fixed, so spacing grows with range">
A 640×480 depth camera with a 60° field of view resolves 1.64 mrad per pixel:
0.49 mm between points at 0.3 m, 8.2 mm at 5 m.
</Step>

<Step title="Density therefore falls as $1/Z^2$">
415 points/cm² at 0.3 m against 1.5 at 5 m — a factor of 278 across one scene.
</Step>

<Step title="So a fixed radius means different things in different places">
A 2 cm neighbourhood holds 5215 points at 0.3 m and 19 points at 5 m. Any feature
computed over it is built from wildly different amounts of evidence.
</Step>

<Step title="And a fixed k does too">
Holding $k = 32$ neighbours spans 1.6 mm at 0.3 m and 26 mm at 5 m — a 16-fold
difference in the physical scale being described.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Neither choice is scale-consistent, which is worth stating plainly because the
two options exhaust what most libraries offer. Fixed radius keeps the geometric
meaning and varies the statistics; fixed $k$ keeps the statistics and varies the
geometry. Which is less wrong depends on whether your feature is describing a
physical scale — surface curvature, say — or needs a stable sample count. Voxel
downsampling before feature extraction is the usual compromise, and it works by
making density uniform at the cost of discarding resolution where you had it.
</Warning>
