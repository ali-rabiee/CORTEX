## Stereo, from similar triangles

Two rectified cameras separated by baseline $B$, focal length $f$ in pixels. A
world point at depth $z$ projects to $u_L$ and $u_R$, and the **disparity** is
$d = u_L - u_R$.

<Derivation title="$z = fB/d$, and why the error is quadratic">

Place the point at $(X, Z)$ relative to the left camera. Perspective projection
gives

$$
u_L = f\frac{X}{Z} + c_x,
\qquad
u_R = f\frac{X - B}{Z} + c_x
$$

Subtracting:

$$
d = u_L - u_R = f\frac{B}{Z}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\boxed{\,z = \frac{fB}{d}\,}
$$

**Now the error.** Differentiating with respect to disparity:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}d} = -\frac{fB}{d^2} = -\frac{z^2}{fB}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
|\delta z| = \frac{z^2}{fB}\,\delta d
$$

**Read the three levers.** Error grows with $z^2$ — a five-fold distance is a
twenty-five-fold error. It falls with $f$ and with $B$, both proportionally. And
it falls with better sub-pixel matching $\delta d$, which is the only one that is
a software problem.

**Numbers.** $f = 600$ px, $B = 6$ cm, $\delta d = 0.25$ px:

| $z$ | disparity | $\delta z$ | relative |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 m | 72 px | 0.2 cm | 0.3% |
| 1 m | 36 px | 0.7 cm | 0.7% |
| 3 m | 12 px | 6.2 cm | 2.1% |
| 5 m | 7.2 px | 17 cm | 3.5% |
| 10 m | 3.6 px | 69 cm | 6.9% |

**The design consequence.** At 10 m the disparity is under 4 pixels, so a
quarter-pixel matching error is 7% of the signal. Long-range stereo needs a
physically wide baseline, and no amount of processing substitutes for geometry.

</Derivation>

## Monocular ambiguity, stated properly

<Derivation title="Scale is a gauge freedom, not a measurement error">

Perspective projection of a point $(X, Y, Z)$:

$$
u = f_x\frac{X}{Z} + c_x,
\qquad
v = f_y\frac{Y}{Z} + c_y
$$

Apply a global scaling $(X, Y, Z) \mapsto (\alpha X, \alpha Y, \alpha Z)$:

$$
u' = f_x\frac{\alpha X}{\alpha Z} + c_x = u
$$

**The image is bit-identical for every $\alpha > 0$.** So the scale is not
poorly estimated — it is *unobservable*. No amount of resolution, model capacity,
or training data changes this, because the information is not in the data.

**What breaks the tie.** A second view with a known baseline; a known object
size; a metric range sensor; or a learned prior over object sizes, which is what
a metric monocular network uses. The last one is a *guess*, and it fails exactly
when the scene contains something atypically sized.

**Why the scale-invariant loss exists.** Relative-depth methods train with a loss
invariant to scale and shift, which is an explicit acknowledgement of this: they
fit the part of the problem that is determined and decline the part that is not.
Using their output for a metric measurement is using it outside what it claims.

</Derivation>

## From a depth image to a point cloud

$$
X = \frac{(u - c_x)\,z}{f_x},
\qquad
Y = \frac{(v - c_y)\,z}{f_y},
\qquad
Z = z
$$

<Warning>
Every intrinsic error has a distinct geometric signature, and knowing them turns
a vague "the cloud looks wrong" into a diagnosis.

**Wrong focal length** scales $X$ and $Y$ but not $Z$, so the cloud is stretched
or squashed laterally — a sphere becomes an ellipsoid.

**Wrong principal point** adds a lateral offset proportional to $z$, which shears
the cloud. A flat wall bends away with distance, and this reads as a tilt.

**Wrong extrinsics** between the colour and depth cameras rigidly transform one
relative to the other, so points get the wrong colour — most visible at object
boundaries, where colour bleeds onto the background.

Unproject a known flat surface and check it is planar. It is a two-minute test
that catches all three.
</Warning>

## Where self-supervised depth gets its signal

With no depth labels, train by predicting depth $\hat z$ and relative pose $T$,
warping a source frame into the target, and minimising photometric error:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{p} \Big\| I_t(p) - I_{s}\big(\pi(T \cdot \pi^{-1}(p, \hat z_p))\big) \Big\|
$$

<Derivation title="The two failures this objective structurally inherits">

The supervision assumes a **static scene** observed from two camera poses. Both
failures are violations of that assumption, not noise.

**Occlusion.** A pixel visible in the target and hidden in the source has no
valid correspondence. Its photometric error is large for *every* depth, so the
gradient it produces is meaningless. The standard remedies are a per-pixel
minimum over several source frames — a pixel occluded in one is often visible in
another — rather than an average, which would let the occluded frame dominate.

**Moving objects.** Apparent motion can be explained by camera motion with
correct depth, *or* by wrong depth. The objective cannot distinguish them. The
characteristic artefact is an object moving with the camera — a car ahead at the
same speed — being assigned near-infinite depth, because that makes it stationary
in the warp. Auto-masking, which discards pixels whose photometric error does not
improve over the no-motion baseline, addresses exactly this.

**The general point.** A self-supervised objective encodes assumptions, and the
model's failures are those assumptions failing rather than the model being bad.
Reading the objective tells you where it will break before you test it.

</Derivation>

## Combining sources

Depth from different sensors has different error structure, so fusing them means
weighting by that structure rather than averaging.

$$
\hat z = \frac{\sum_i z_i/\sigma_i^2}{\sum_i 1/\sigma_i^2},
\qquad
\sigma_{\text{stereo}}^2 \propto z^4
$$

<KeyIdea>
The $z^4$ is the variance corresponding to a $z^2$ standard deviation, and it has
a strong practical consequence: stereo's weight in a fusion falls very fast with
distance. A naive average of a stereo and a monocular estimate at 8 m is
dominated by a stereo measurement that is nearly worthless there. Inverse-variance
weighting with a depth-dependent $\sigma$ is the minimum honest treatment.
</KeyIdea>
