recall:
  prompt: "Give your interview answer on depth estimation for robotics. What is the fundamental limitation, and how do you choose a sensor?"
  keyPoints:
    - "Monocular metric depth is unobservable in principle — scaling the scene and the translation together leaves the image identical — so metric monocular output is a learned size prior, not a measurement."
    - "Stereo error grows as $z^2$: $\\delta z = z^2\\delta d/(fB)$. Quote accuracy at your working range, because a 1 m specification is 25 times worse at 5 m."
    - "Each sensor fails on a specific material, and what matters is whether the failure is loud (a hole) or quiet (a spurious match, multi-path in a corner)."
    - "Carry validity and uncertainty through the pipeline. An inpainted hole is a guess presented as a measurement, on exactly the objects where being wrong is expensive."
interview:
  answer: >-
    The thing I'd lead with is that monocular metric depth is unobservable in
    principle rather than just hard. If you scale the whole scene and the camera
    translation by the same factor, every image is bit-identical — perspective
    projection maps a point to a ray and every point on that ray gives the same
    pixel. So a doll's house and a real house are indistinguishable. That means
    no amount of resolution, model capacity, or training data recovers metric
    scale from one image; what a metric monocular network actually does is
    recognise objects and apply learned size priors. It works well because most
    scenes contain familiar things, and it fails specifically and confidently
    when they don't — a scale model, a macro shot, an unusually proportioned
    room. Which is why MiDaS trains with a scale-and-shift-invariant loss: that's
    an explicit decision to fit the observable part of the problem and decline
    the rest, and it's also why it generalises. For metric depth you need
    geometry. Stereo gives it from a known baseline, and the number I'd carry is
    that the error grows with the square of distance — differentiating z equals
    fB over d gives delta-z equal to z-squared times the disparity error over fB.
    For a typical rig, six-centimetre baseline and quarter-pixel matching, that's
    about seven millimetres at one metre and about seventy centimetres at ten. A
    hundredfold over a tenfold distance. So "accurate to a centimetre" isn't a
    specification, it's a specification at one distance, and that's the most
    common way a depth sensor gets picked for the wrong task. The three levers
    are baseline, focal length, and sub-pixel matching accuracy, and only the
    last is a software problem. The other thing I'd raise is that every sensor
    has a material it can't see, and what matters operationally is whether the
    failure is loud or quiet. Structured light on glass gives you a hole, which
    is honest — the sensor is telling you it couldn't measure. Stereo on a
    repetitive texture gives you a confident wrong disparity from a spurious
    match, and time-of-flight in a concave corner gives a systematically too-far
    reading from multi-path. Those are the dangerous ones, because downstream
    code can't distinguish them from good data.
  followUps:
    - q: "How would you use a monocular depth network in a real robot stack?"
      a: >-
        For structure rather than for measurements. Relative depth — what's in
        front of what — transfers extremely well and is genuinely useful as a
        collision-avoidance prior, as a segmentation cue, or for ordering
        objects. What I wouldn't do is take metres out of it and command a
        gripper, because its failure mode is a confident wrong number rather
        than a wide error bar. If I did need metric output from it, I'd align
        it: fit a scale and shift by least squares against a handful of metric
        anchors from stereo, a range sensor, or a known object dimension. That
        turns it into an interpolator between measurements you trust, which is
        a defensible use, rather than a measurement in its own right.
    - q: "You need centimetre accuracy at three metres. What do you specify?"
      a: >-
        I'd work backwards from delta-z equals z-squared delta-d over fB. At
        three metres wanting one centimetre, with a quarter-pixel disparity
        accuracy, fB needs to be about 225 pixel-metres — so with a 600-pixel
        focal length that's a baseline of roughly 37 centimetres, six times
        wider than a typical compact RGB-D camera. That's the useful thing about
        the formula: it tells you immediately that this is a mechanical
        requirement rather than a processing one, and that no consumer depth
        camera will do it however good its software. The alternatives are a
        longer focal length, which trades field of view, or a different sensing
        modality entirely — lidar doesn't have the quadratic term because it
        measures time of flight rather than triangulating.
    - q: "Your depth-based grasping fails on a specific set of objects. How do you diagnose it?"
      a: >-
        I'd look at the depth image on those objects first, before anything about
        the policy. If there are holes, it's a material problem — glass,
        polished metal, or dark matte surfaces defeat active sensors physically,
        and that's a sensor choice rather than a bug. If there are values but the
        grasp point is behind the object, I'd suspect boundary outliers: pixels
        straddling an edge interpolate between foreground and background, and a
        patch *mean* including a few of those lands centimetres off. Median over
        the patch fixes it. And I'd check whether anything upstream is
        inpainting, because a filled hole is indistinguishable from a
        measurement downstream, and holes cluster on exactly the objects where a
        wrong depth drives the gripper into something.
    - q: "How would you check a depth camera is calibrated?"
      a: >-
        Point it at a flat wall or table and unproject to a point cloud, then fit
        a plane and look at the residual pattern. A tape measure checks the range
        calibration and is blind to the intrinsics, which govern the lateral
        unprojection — and each intrinsic error has a distinct signature. A wrong
        focal length scales x and y but not z, so the cloud is stretched
        laterally. A wrong principal point adds an offset proportional to z,
        which shears the cloud, so a flat wall bends away with distance and
        reads as a tilt. Wrong extrinsics between the colour and depth cameras
        show up as colour bleeding across object boundaries. It's one image and
        it catches the class of error that otherwise gets chased for days as
        "the point cloud looks slightly off".
  traps:
    - "Describing monocular scale ambiguity as an accuracy problem. It is unobservable in principle, so more data and larger models do not address it."
    - "Quoting a depth accuracy without a range. Stereo error is quadratic in distance, so the two are inseparable."
    - "Treating holes as noise to be filled. They are the sensor reporting honestly, and they cluster on the objects where a wrong value is most expensive."
    - "Using a patch mean for a grasp point. Boundary pixels interpolate between foreground and background; the median is robust and costs nothing."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Flat surface bends with distance → principal point">
The unprojection offset scales with $z$. Fit a plane and check whether residuals
grow from the image centre.
</Step>

<Step title="Cloud stretched laterally but depths correct → focal length">
$f$ scales $X$ and $Y$ and not $Z$, so a sphere becomes an ellipsoid.
</Step>

<Step title="Colour bleeds across object edges → extrinsics">
The colour-to-depth transform is wrong. Most visible at boundaries.
</Step>

<Step title="Grasp point lands behind the object → boundary outliers">
Flying pixels interpolate foreground and background. Use the median over the
patch, not the mean.
</Step>

<Step title="Holes on specific objects → material, not a bug">
Glass, polished metal, dark matte. The sensor is reporting honestly, and this is
a sensor-choice question.
</Step>

<Step title="Accuracy fine in the lab, poor at range → the $z^2$ term">
Quote and test at your actual working distance, not at the datasheet's.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Two things distinguish this answer. Stating monocular scale ambiguity as
*unobservable in principle* — with the one-line argument that scaling the scene
and the translation together leaves the image identical — because that determines
what could ever fix it, and rules out "wait for a better model". And carrying the
$z^2$ error relation with real numbers, since it converts sensor selection from a
datasheet comparison into a calculation you can do in the interview. Adding that
the operationally important question is whether a sensor's failures are loud or
quiet is what signals you have debugged a perception stack rather than read about
one.
</Warning>
