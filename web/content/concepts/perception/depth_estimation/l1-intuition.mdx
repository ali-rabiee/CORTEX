## Three ways to get depth, and one impossibility

<Steps>
<Step title="Stereo — triangulate from two views">
Two cameras a known distance apart. Find the same point in both images, measure
how far it shifted, and geometry gives the distance. Metric, because the baseline
is known.
</Step>

<Step title="Active — send light out and measure what returns">
Structured light projects a known pattern and measures its deformation;
time-of-flight measures how long a pulse takes to come back. Metric, and
dependent on the light actually returning.
</Step>

<Step title="Monocular — infer it from one image">
A network predicts depth from appearance. It works remarkably well, and it
cannot recover metric scale.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
The last point is geometric rather than a limitation of current models.
Perspective projection maps a 3-D point to a *ray*, and every point along that
ray produces the same pixel — so a doll's house and a real house are
indistinguishable. Metric monocular depth requires an assumption about how large
things are, which the image cannot supply and the network has to have learned.
</KeyIdea>

<Intuition>
Someone holds up a photograph of a room and asks how wide it is. You answer by
recognising a door and knowing doors are about 80 cm — not by looking harder at
the photograph.

That is exactly what a monocular depth network does, and it explains its failure
mode: show it a scale model, or an unusually sized object, and it will confidently
give you the wrong number, because the recognition succeeded and the assumption
was wrong.
</Intuition>

## The error grows with the square of distance

For a stereo pair with focal length $f$, baseline $B$, and measured disparity
$d$, the depth is $z = fB/d$. Differentiating:

$$
|\delta z| = \frac{z^2}{fB}\,\delta d
$$

| depth | error | relative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 m | 0.2 cm | 0.3% |
| 1 m | 0.7 cm | 0.7% |
| 3 m | 6.2 cm | 2.1% |
| 5 m | 17 cm | 3.5% |
| 10 m | 69 cm | 6.9% |

<Warning>
"Accurate to a centimetre" is not a specification — it is a specification *at one
distance*. A sensor quoted at 1 m has twenty-five times that error at 5 m, and
this is the most common way a depth sensor gets chosen for the wrong task.

Two levers change the curve: a longer baseline $B$ and a longer focal length $f$
both reduce the error proportionally. That is why long-range stereo rigs are
physically wide, and why a compact RGB-D camera cannot be made accurate at range
by improving its software.
</Warning>

## Every sensor has a material it cannot see

<Steps>
<Step title="Stereo fails on textureless surfaces">
It works by matching, and a blank white wall has nothing to match. The failure is
a *wrong* disparity from a spurious match rather than a missing one, which is
worse — it returns a confident number.
</Step>

<Step title="Structured light fails on shiny and transparent things">
The projected pattern must return diffusely. Glass transmits it; polished metal
reflects it somewhere else. Usually returns a hole, which is honest.
</Step>

<Step title="Time-of-flight fails on absorbers and at corners">
Dark matte surfaces return too little light. Concave corners produce multi-path,
where the pulse bounces twice and reports a distance that is too far — a
systematic error rather than noise.
</Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>
Notice which failures are *loud* and which are *quiet*. A hole in a depth image
announces itself and can be handled. A spurious stereo match on a repetitive
texture, or a multi-path reading in a corner, returns a plausible number that is
wrong — and downstream code cannot tell. Knowing which of your sensor's failures
are silent is more useful than knowing its quoted accuracy.
</KeyIdea>

## What this means for a robot

<Steps>
<Step title="Use monocular depth for structure, not for measurements">
Relative depth — what is in front of what — transfers well. Metric numbers from a
single image should not be used to command a gripper without an independent
scale reference.
</Step>

<Step title="Match the sensor to the range">
Tabletop manipulation at 0.5-1 m is where consumer RGB-D is genuinely
sub-centimetre. Navigation at 5-10 m needs a longer baseline, and no amount of
processing substitutes for it.
</Step>

<Step title="Treat holes as information, not as noise to fill">
An inpainted hole is a guess presented as a measurement. Where the guess matters
— a grasp point — propagating the fact that the value is unknown is better than
interpolating over it.
</Step>
</Steps>
