## Establishing that it is possible at all

<PaperCard refKey="eigen2014depth" />

That a network could produce dense depth from a single image was not obvious in
2014, and the paper's architecture — a coarse global prediction refined locally —
reflects the structure of the problem: overall scene layout is a global judgement
and local detail is not.

<KeyIdea>
Its choice of a scale-invariant loss in log space is worth noticing, because it
prefigures everything that followed. Even here, fitting the metric scale directly
was the wrong target — the observable part of the problem is relative, and the
loss was built around that a decade before it became the standard framing.
</KeyIdea>

## Removing the need for labels

<PaperCard refKey="godard2019monodepth2" />

Depth labels require a range sensor and are expensive. The self-supervised
alternative predicts depth *and* relative pose, warps one frame into another, and
minimises photometric error — supervision from nothing but video.

The two contributions are targeted fixes for two distinct violations of the
static-scene assumption the objective rests on.

<Steps>
<Step title="Per-pixel minimum, for occlusion">
A pixel hidden in one source frame produces a large error for *any* depth, so its
gradient is meaningless. Averaging over frames guarantees that term is always
present; taking the minimum uses whichever frame the pixel is visible in.
</Step>

<Step title="Auto-masking, for moving objects">
Apparent motion can be explained by camera motion with correct depth *or* by
wrong depth, and the objective cannot tell them apart. An object moving with the
camera gets assigned near-infinite depth, since that makes it stationary in the
warp. Masking pixels whose photometric error does not improve over the
no-motion baseline removes exactly those.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Both failures are the objective's *assumptions* failing rather than the model
being weak, which is the general lesson worth carrying. Reading a self-supervised
loss tells you where the method will break before you test it — and the fixes
here are principled precisely because they were derived from the violation rather
than tuned against a benchmark.
</Warning>

## Fitting the observable part

<PaperGrid>
<PaperCard refKey="ranftl2020midas" />
<PaperCard refKey="ranftl2021dpt" />
</PaperGrid>

MiDaS's problem was that depth datasets are mutually incompatible: laser scans in
metres, stereo disparities in arbitrary units, ordinal labels from human
annotators. Training on a **scale-and-shift-invariant** loss makes them poolable.

<KeyIdea>
It is also the conceptually right target. Scale is a gauge freedom under
single-view projection, so a loss that ignores scale is aligned with what the data
can determine — and declining to fit an unobservable quantity is what makes the
estimate transfer across domains. The generalisation and the honesty are the same
decision.
</KeyIdea>

DPT then replaces the convolutional encoder with a transformer, which matters for
depth specifically: the cue that fixes a pixel's depth is often far away — a
recognisable object elsewhere in the scene — and a convolutional stack's receptive
field grows linearly with depth, so global context is exactly what it is short of.

## Recovering metric scale, and what that costs

<PaperCard refKey="bhat2023zoedepth" />

Pretrain for relative depth where generalisation is achievable, then attach
metric heads for specific domains.

<Warning>
The ambiguity has not been dissolved. Scaling a scene and the camera translation
together leaves every image bit-identical, so no single-view method can recover
scale *from the data*. What a metric monocular model does is recognise objects
and apply learned size priors.

That works well, because most scenes contain familiar things — and it fails
specifically and confidently on scenes that do not: a scale model, a macro shot,
an unusually proportioned room. For a robot the consequence is concrete:
monocular metric depth should not command a gripper without an independent scale
reference, because its failure mode is a confident wrong number rather than an
error bar.
</Warning>

## Where this leaves a robot

<Steps>
<Step title="Monocular for structure, geometry for measurement">
Relative depth generalises impressively and is genuinely useful — for ordering,
for segmentation cues, for collision-avoidance priors. Metric commands should
come from stereo, a range sensor, or a known reference.
</Step>

<Step title="The sensor's range is the specification">
Stereo error grows as $z^2$, so the choice is determined by the working distance
far more than by the sensor's headline accuracy.
</Step>

<Step title="Learned depth does not remove the material failures">
Glass and polished metal defeat active sensors physically. A monocular network
will happily produce a surface there — which is sometimes better than a hole and
sometimes a confident hallucination, and the difference matters when a gripper
acts on it.
</Step>
</Steps>
