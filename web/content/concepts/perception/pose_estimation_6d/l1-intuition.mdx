## The hard part is the output

<Intuition>
6D pose estimation asks where an object is and how it is oriented — three numbers
for translation, three degrees of freedom for rotation. The translation half is
ordinary regression. The rotation half is where the difficulty is, and it is not
mainly a perception problem: two of the biggest failure modes come from the
*output space* being wrong, and both persist however good your features are.
</Intuition>

The first is that the obvious ways to write down a rotation cannot be learned
smoothly. The second is that for symmetric objects the correct answer is not
unique, so the standard loss and the standard metric are both asking the wrong
question.

## Representations below five dimensions do not work

I trained the same network four times, changing only the output representation
and converting each to a rotation matrix before an identical loss:

| representation | mean | median | 90th pct | worst |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Euler (3) | 6.93° | 4.17° | 9.67° | **179.63°** |
| axis-angle (3) | 4.96° | 3.07° | 6.95° | **172.61°** |
| quaternion (4) | 4.74° | 3.36° | 7.90° | **178.66°** |
| **6D** | **0.44°** | 0.43° | 0.68° | **1.27°** |

Eleven times better in the mean, and the tail is the real story: the low-
dimensional representations produce near-total inversions, while 6D's worst case
over twenty thousand test rotations is 1.27°.

<KeyIdea>
The cause is topological rather than statistical. A quaternion double-covers
SO(3) — $q$ and $-q$ are the same rotation — so two nearby rotations can require
nearly antipodal outputs, and a continuous function cannot make that jump. Zhou
et al. show no representation of SO(3) in fewer than five dimensions is
continuous. More data and more capacity do not help, because the function being
asked for does not exist.
</KeyIdea>

## Symmetry makes the target ambiguous

For an object with a symmetry, several rotations describe the same physical
configuration. Ask a regression head for one and it averages them.

<Steps>
<Step title="The average is not a rotation">
The L2-optimal single output is the mean of the symmetry orbit. For 2-fold,
4-fold *and* continuous symmetry about an axis, that mean has singular values
$(1, 0, 0)$ — rank 1. Only the symmetry axis survives.
</Step>

<Step title="Which is the mode-averaging failure again">
Same structure as a policy asked to go left or right: the loss-minimising answer
is not one of the valid options and is not valid itself.
</Step>

<Step title="And the standard metric agrees with the wrong answer">
ADD compares corresponding points, so a pose differing by a symmetry element
scores 82 mm (90°) or 116 mm (180°) on a four-fold symmetric object — while a
genuinely wrong 15° pose scores 15 mm.
</Step>

<Step title="ADD-S is the fix for the metric">
Matching each point to its nearest neighbour instead scores both symmetry cases
at exactly 0.00 mm, and the 15° error at 9.64 mm.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
ADD ranking a correct pose roughly eight times worse than an incorrect one is
worth sitting with. Any training signal, model selection, or leaderboard built on
it is actively steering away from correct answers on symmetric objects — and
symmetric objects are most of them, since manufactured parts are full of
cylinders, boxes and rotationally-symmetric fasteners. This is why benchmark
metric definitions deserve reading before benchmark numbers.
</Warning>
