## Why the representation matters

A network computes a function $\phi: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ and a fixed
map $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to SO(3)$ turns its output into a rotation. For the network
to be able to fit the task, there must exist a *continuous*
$g: SO(3) \to \mathbb{R}^n$ with $f(g(R)) = R$.

<Derivation title="Where the quaternion representation breaks">

Unit quaternions double-cover $SO(3)$: the map $\pi: S^3 \to SO(3)$ sends both
$q$ and $-q$ to the same rotation. To use quaternions as an output space, we need
a continuous section — a continuous choice of one of the two preimages for every
rotation.

Suppose $g: SO(3) \to S^3$ were such a continuous section. Take a loop in
$SO(3)$ that is not contractible — a full $2\pi$ rotation about a fixed axis.
Lifting it to $S^3$ traces a path from $q$ to $-q$: the lift of a closed loop is
*not closed*. But $g$ being continuous and single-valued would force the lift to
return to $g$ of the starting rotation, which is $q$, not $-q$.

Contradiction. **The assumption doing the work is that $SO(3)$ is not simply
connected** — its fundamental group is $\mathbb{Z}_2$, which is exactly what the
double cover expresses. Any single-valued choice of quaternion must therefore
have a discontinuity somewhere.

Zhou et al. generalise this: $SO(3)$ admits no continuous representation in
$\mathbb{R}^n$ for $n < 5$. Six dimensions with Gram–Schmidt is the smallest
practical continuous choice.

</Derivation>

The 6D representation outputs two vectors and orthonormalises them:

$$
b_1 = \frac{a_1}{\|a_1\|}, \qquad
b_2 = \frac{a_2 - (b_1 \!\cdot\! a_2)b_1}{\|a_2 - (b_1 \!\cdot\! a_2)b_1\|}, \qquad
b_3 = b_1 \times b_2
$$

This is continuous everywhere except the measure-zero set where $a_1 = 0$ or the
two are parallel, and — crucially — it admits a continuous section: just take the
first two columns of $R$.

Measured, four identical networks differing only in the output map:

| representation | mean | 90th pct | worst |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Euler (3) | 6.93° | 9.67° | 179.63° |
| axis-angle (3) | 4.96° | 6.95° | 172.61° |
| quaternion (4) | 4.74° | 7.90° | 178.66° |
| 6D | 0.44° | 0.68° | 1.27° |

<KeyIdea>
The signature to recognise is a distribution with a reasonable centre and a
catastrophic tail. Mean error of a few degrees looks like an ordinary model that
needs more training; a worst case of 178° is not a bad fit but a structural
impossibility, and no amount of data removes it. If your rotation errors are
mostly fine with occasional total inversions, check the representation before
anything else.
</KeyIdea>

## Symmetry

Let $G \subset SO(3)$ be the object's symmetry group, so $Rg$ describes the same
physical configuration as $R$ for every $g \in G$.

<Derivation title="The L2-optimal prediction is not a rotation">

Under a squared loss on the rotation matrix, the optimal single prediction given
that all orbit elements are equally valid is the conditional mean:

$$
\hat{R} = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G} R g
$$

Take $G$ = rotations about $\hat{z}$. For 2-fold symmetry,
$\tfrac{1}{2}(I + R_z(\pi)) = \mathrm{diag}(0,0,1)$. For continuous symmetry,

$$
\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} R_z(\theta)\, d\theta = \mathrm{diag}(0,0,1)
$$

since $\int \cos = \int \sin = 0$ over the full circle.

Measured singular values of the orbit mean:

| symmetry | singular values | rank |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2-fold | $(1, 0, 0)$ | 1 |
| 4-fold | $(1, 0, 0)$ | 1 |
| continuous | $(1, 0, 0)$ | 1 |

</Derivation>

Rank 1. Everything except the symmetry axis cancels — which is informative rather
than merely broken: the axis is exactly the part of the pose that *is*
determined, and the rest is exactly what is not.

## Metrics

$$
\mathrm{ADD} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|}\sum_{x \in \mathcal{M}} \left\| (Rx + t) - (\hat{R}x + \hat{t}) \right\|
$$

$$
\mathrm{ADD\text{-}S} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|}\sum_{x_1 \in \mathcal{M}} \min_{x_2 \in \mathcal{M}} \left\| (Rx_1 + t) - (\hat{R}x_2 + \hat{t}) \right\|
$$

ADD compares *corresponding* points; ADD-S compares the point *sets*. A symmetry
element maps the model onto itself while permuting which point is where, so it is
invisible to the second and maximal to the first.

Measured on an exactly 4-fold symmetric object:

| predicted pose | ADD | ADD-S |
| --- | --- | --- |
| exact | 0.00 mm | 0.00 mm |
| 90° about the symmetry axis | **82.21 mm** | **0.00 mm** |
| 180° about the symmetry axis | **116.26 mm** | **0.00 mm** |
| 5° genuine error | 5.07 mm | 3.75 mm |
| 15° genuine error | 15.17 mm | 9.64 mm |

<Warning>
ADD ranks a physically perfect pose about eight times worse than a genuinely
wrong one. But ADD-S is not simply better: it is invariant to any transform that
maps the point set onto itself *approximately*, so it forgives real errors that
slide points along a smooth surface — a sphere scores near zero under ADD-S for
every rotation. The correct practice is to declare each object's symmetry group
and evaluate modulo exactly that, rather than choosing one metric for everything.
</Warning>

## What the error costs downstream

A rotation error $\delta\theta$ displaces a point at radius $r$ from the object
centre by $2r\sin(\delta\theta/2) \approx r\,\delta\theta$:

| object size | 1° | 5° | 10° |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 cm | 0.44 mm | 2.18 mm | 4.34 mm |
| 10 cm | 0.87 mm | 4.36 mm | 8.68 mm |
| 20 cm | 1.75 mm | 8.72 mm | 17.36 mm |

So a 5° pose error on a 10 cm object is about 4 mm at the rim — comfortably
inside a parallel gripper's tolerance for a large object, and fatal for an
insertion with 50 µm clearance.
