interview:
  answer: >-
    Six-D pose is where an object is and how it's oriented — three for
    translation and three degrees of freedom for rotation. The translation half
    is ordinary regression. What I find worth talking about is that two of the
    biggest failure modes aren't perception problems at all, they're output-space
    problems, and they persist however good your features are. The first is the
    rotation representation. The natural things to output — Euler angles, a
    quaternion — can't be learned smoothly, and the reason is topological rather
    than statistical. SO(3) isn't simply connected: a full two-pi rotation is a
    loop you can't contract, and if you lift it to unit quaternions it ends at
    minus q rather than back at q. So any single-valued choice of quaternion has
    to jump somewhere, and a continuous network can't reproduce a jump. Zhou and
    colleagues generalised that — there's no continuous representation of SO(3)
    in fewer than five dimensions. I ran the comparison: same network, same loss
    on the rotation matrix, only the output map changed. Quaternions gave four
    point seven degrees mean error with a worst case of a hundred and
    seventy-nine. The six-D representation — output two vectors, Gram-Schmidt
    them — gave point four four mean and a worst case of one point two seven over
    twenty thousand test rotations. The tail is the real signal. A worst case
    near a hundred and eighty degrees isn't a bad fit, it's a structural
    impossibility, and more data won't touch it. The second failure is symmetry.
    If an object has a symmetry group, several rotations describe the same
    physical configuration, so asking a regression head for one makes it average
    them — and the L2-optimal answer is the mean of the symmetry orbit. I
    computed that for two-fold, four-fold and continuous symmetry about an axis,
    and in all three cases the mean has singular values one, zero, zero. It's
    rank one. Not a rotation at all — only the symmetry axis survives, which is
    actually informative, because the axis is exactly the part that is determined
    and the rest is exactly what isn't. And the same thing breaks the standard
    metric. ADD compares corresponding points, so a pose that differs by a
    symmetry element — physically identical — scored eighty-two millimetres for
    ninety degrees and a hundred and sixteen for a hundred and eighty on a
    four-fold symmetric object, while a genuinely wrong fifteen-degree pose
    scored fifteen. ADD ranks a correct pose about eight times worse than an
    incorrect one. ADD-S, which matches nearest neighbours instead of
    correspondences, gives exactly zero for both symmetry cases.
  followUps:
    - q: "So should everyone just use ADD-S?"
      a: >-
        No, and I'd push back on that specifically because it's the obvious
        conclusion and it's wrong. ADD-S is invariant to any transform that
        approximately maps the point set onto itself, so it forgives real errors
        that slide points along a smooth surface — a sphere scores near zero
        under ADD-S for every rotation, including one that's completely wrong for
        a task that cares about a marking on it. The right practice is to declare
        each object's symmetry group as metadata, which you have from the CAD
        model anyway, and evaluate modulo exactly that group. Picking one metric
        globally trades one systematic error for another.
    - q: "How do you handle a continuously symmetric object like a cylinder?"
      a: >-
        Predict what's observable and be explicit that the rest isn't. The
        rank-one result is precisely the statement that only the axis is
        determined, so I'd output the axis and the translation, and mark the roll
        about the axis as undefined rather than emitting a number. That matters
        downstream: a grasp planner given a full pose will trust the roll and
        plan around it, whereas one told the roll is free can choose it for its
        own reasons — approach angle, collision avoidance, cable routing. Making
        the ambiguity explicit turns it from a source of error into a degree of
        freedom someone else can spend.
    - q: "Your rotation errors are mostly fine but occasionally near 180 degrees. Diagnosis?"
      a: >-
        That's close to diagnostic for a discontinuous representation, so I'd
        check the head before anything else. The shape is the tell: a reasonable
        median with catastrophic outliers, because the discontinuity is confined
        to a cut in rotation space rather than spread over it — my measured
        quaternion run had a three point four degree median and a hundred and
        seventy-nine degree maximum. The other candidate is symmetry, if the
        object has a two-fold symmetry and you're not evaluating modulo it, since
        a correct pose then reads as a hundred and eighty degrees wrong. Both are
        output-space problems and neither is fixed by training longer. I'd also
        add the fraction of errors above ninety degrees to the standard report,
        because an inversion rate of a fraction of a percent is invisible in a
        mean and survives every threshold metric.
    - q: "How accurate does pose need to be for manipulation?"
      a: >-
        It depends entirely on the task tolerance, and the conversion is simple —
        a rotation error displaces a point at radius r by roughly r times the
        angle. Five degrees on a ten centimetre object is about four millimetres
        at the rim. That's comfortably inside a parallel gripper's tolerance for
        picking something up, and hopeless for an insertion with fifty micron
        clearance. Which is the useful framing: for grasping, a few degrees is
        fine and the effort should go elsewhere; for tight assembly, no realistic
        pose accuracy is enough, and you switch to a compliant search where
        contact forces do the final alignment rather than trying to see your way
        in.

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Good median, occasional ~180° errors → discontinuous representation">
Check the head before the training schedule. Add the fraction of errors above 90°
to your report.
</Step>

<Step title="Bad ADD on symmetric objects, good visual results → the metric">
Evaluate modulo the declared symmetry group rather than switching metrics
globally.
</Step>

<Step title="Predictions collapse toward a degenerate matrix → orbit averaging">
An ambiguous target with a plain L2 loss. Take the minimum over the symmetry
group inside the loss.
</Step>

<Step title="Systematic offset that scales with range → calibration, not pose">
Focal length or hand-eye rotation error. The pose estimator is reporting
faithfully in a wrong frame.
</Step>

<Step title="Accurate pose, failed insertion → the wrong tool">
Convert the angle to a displacement at the part radius. Below the task tolerance,
switch to compliant search.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
What makes this answer land is refusing to treat either problem as a perception
issue. Saying the rotation failure is topological — SO(3) is not simply connected,
so the function being asked for does not exist — is checkable in one line and
explains why more data cannot help. Pairing it with the symmetry result, and
especially with ADD ranking a correct pose worse than an incorrect one, shows you
have looked at what the metrics actually compute rather than reading the
leaderboard.
</Warning>
