## Two transforms, both required

<Intuition>
Calibration is what turns a detection into an instruction. Intrinsics say which
ray in space a pixel corresponds to; hand-eye says where the camera sits in the
robot's frame. Without both, "the mug is at pixel (312, 208)" cannot become "move
the gripper here". It is unglamorous plumbing, and it is also the layer where a
surprising share of manipulation failures originate — usually diagnosed as a
perception or control problem for weeks first.
</Intuition>

The thing worth knowing is not how the method works. It is that **the number your
calibration tool reports cannot tell you whether it worked.**

## The metric is blind

<Figure src="/figures/calibration-reprojection-blind.svg" alt="Left: a board at twice the distance viewed with twice the focal length projects to an identical image, so the two cannot be separated from frontal views. Right: across four capture protocols, RMS reprojection error stays between 0.184 and 0.192 pixels while the recovered focal length is wrong by 650%, 446%, 1.1% and 0.1%." caption="Four calibrations of the same camera. The reported error is flat across all of them; the recovered focal length spans a factor of seven." />

I simulated a camera with a true focal length of 800 px and calibrated it from
ten board views under four capture protocols:

| protocol | RMS reprojection | recovered $f_x$ | error |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| fronto-parallel, fixed depth | 0.184 px | 6000 | **+650%** |
| fronto-parallel, varied depth | 0.184 px | 4365 | **+446%** |
| slight tilt (10°) | 0.192 px | 809 | +1.1% |
| good tilt (35°) | 0.190 px | 799 | −0.1% |

The two badly wrong calibrations have the *best* reprojection error. Starting the
optimiser from 700 and from 600 both converged to 6000.1 and 6000.0 — the same
wrong answer, which confirms a genuine flat direction rather than an optimiser
that got stuck.

<KeyIdea>
The reason is the same unobservability that makes monocular metric depth
impossible: a frontal plane projects with a single scale factor $f/Z$, so
doubling both leaves every pixel unchanged. Varying the *distance* does not help,
because each new view adds an unknown distance along with its equation. Only tilt
helps, and it helps fast — 10° already brings the error to about 1%.
</KeyIdea>

## What the error becomes downstream

<Steps>
<Step title="Focal error passes straight into depth">
For a known-size object, $Z = fW/w$, so $\delta Z / Z = \delta f / f$ exactly. A
1% focal error is 6 mm at 60 cm; the 446% error above is 27 cm.
</Step>

<Step title="Pixel noise scales linearly with distance">
At $f = 800$ px, one pixel of detection error is 0.75 mm at 60 cm and 6.25 mm at
5 m — linear in $Z$, unlike stereo depth error, which is quadratic.
</Step>

<Step title="Hand-eye error is a rigid offset">
A rotation error in the hand-eye transform becomes a position error proportional
to the distance from the camera to the target, so it shows up as a systematic
bias that changes across the workspace.
</Step>

<Step title="And all of it is silent">
None of these produce an error message. They produce a robot that misses by a
consistent amount, which reads like a control or perception problem.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The same structure appears in `states_observations`: a metric computed on the
training data cannot detect a problem that only exists at deployment. There, a
policy's loss was flat across the range from working to useless; here,
reprojection error is flat across a factor of seven in focal length. The remedy
is the same in both cases — the check has to be made on something other than the
data that was fitted, which for calibration means measuring a known distance in
the workspace.
</Warning>
