interview:
  answer: >-
    Calibration is what turns a detection into an instruction — intrinsics say
    which ray a pixel corresponds to, hand-eye says where the camera sits in the
    robot's frame, and without both you can't convert "the mug is at pixel three
    twelve, two oh eight" into a place to move. The part I'd actually want to
    talk about is that the number your calibration tool reports can't tell you
    whether it worked. Reprojection error is computed on the same board images
    the parameters were fitted to, so it's a training residual. If your capture
    geometry leaves a flat direction in the parameter space, the model reproduces
    those images exactly along that whole direction, and the residual stays small
    while the recovered numbers are arbitrary. The concrete case is
    fronto-parallel capture. For a board facing the camera, every point is at the
    same depth, so the projection depends on focal length and distance only
    through their ratio — double both and every pixel is identical. It's the same
    unobservability that makes monocular metric depth impossible. I simulated it:
    true focal length eight hundred pixels, ten views, and fronto-parallel
    capture gave point one eight four pixels RMS with a recovered focal length of
    six thousand. Six hundred and fifty percent wrong, with a better reprojection
    error than the correctly calibrated case. And starting the optimiser from
    seven hundred and from six hundred both converged to six thousand point one
    and six thousand point zero — identical to four figures from different
    initialisations, which is the signature of a genuine flat direction rather
    than a solver that got stuck. What I'd add is that varying the distance
    doesn't fix it, which surprises people: each new frontal view gives you one
    scale measurement and introduces one new unknown distance, so N views give N
    equations and N plus one unknowns and you're always one short. Only tilt
    works, because foreshortening depends on focal length and depth differently.
    The good news is it's cheap — ten degrees of tilt already brought the error
    to about one percent. And the propagation is one-for-one: for a known-size
    object Z equals f W over w, so a one percent focal error is a one percent
    depth error, six millimetres at sixty centimetres. So the practical
    conclusion is that you have to validate on something the calibration wasn't
    fitted to — touch a known point with the robot through the full pipeline and
    measure the miss with a ruler, at the working distance and at several places
    in the workspace.
  followUps:
    - q: "Wouldn't holding out some calibration views catch this?"
      a: >-
        It catches overfitting to particular images and not a degeneracy, because
        the degeneracy is a property of the capture distribution rather than of
        individual frames. If every view is fronto-parallel then the held-out
        ones are too, and the wrong parameters reproject them perfectly as well —
        the flat direction fits all frontal views exactly by construction. What
        does work without leaving the software is looking at conditioning: take
        the SVD of the Jacobian at the solution and a near-zero singular value
        names the unconstrained parameter combination directly. That's two lines,
        it's free at the solution, and almost nobody looks because the toolbox
        already printed a reassuring scalar.
    - q: "What's the equivalent trap in hand-eye calibration?"
      a: >-
        Near-parallel rotation axes. The constraint is R_A equals R_X R_B R_X
        transpose, and conjugation preserves the rotation angle and maps axis to
        axis — so one motion says only that R_X carries B's axis onto A's, which
        is two constraints on three degrees of freedom. Rotation about that axis
        is free, so you need a second motion with a different axis. The trap is
        that it isn't binary: at five degrees of separation the system is
        formally determined, the solver returns an answer without complaining,
        and it's wrong. I measured about three degrees of error from point one
        five degrees of input noise at five degrees separation, against point
        four at ninety — roughly sevenfold amplification, and about fortyfold at
        one degree. Three degrees at half a metre is around two and a half
        centimetres of consistent miss.
    - q: "How would you diagnose a robot that consistently misses by a couple of centimetres?"
      a: >-
        I'd measure the miss at several places in the workspace rather than one,
        because the spatial pattern separates the hypotheses. A miss that's the
        same everywhere is a translation offset in hand-eye. A miss that grows
        with distance from the camera points at focal length or hand-eye
        rotation, since both scale with range. A miss that grows towards the
        image edges is distortion. And a miss that only appears while the robot
        is moving is a time offset between streams rather than a spatial problem
        at all — that one gets misdiagnosed as spatial constantly, because a
        fixed latency looks exactly like a pose error that depends on velocity.
        Recording where each measurement was taken costs nothing and turns one
        number into three distinguishable causes.
    - q: "How often should you recalibrate?"
      a: >-
        I'd rather monitor than schedule, because both fixed answers are wrong in
        the usual way — recalibrating monthly is either wasteful or too late, and
        you can't tell which without a signal. So I'd keep the touch test as a
        periodic health check, a couple of known points, a minute to run, logged
        over time so drift is visible as a trend rather than discovered as a
        failure. Things that should trigger an immediate recalibration are any
        mechanical event: the camera being bumped, a lens changed or refocused,
        the mount being reworked. Thermal drift over a shift is real for some
        setups and the log will show it if it matters for yours.
  traps:
    - "Quoting reprojection error as evidence of accuracy. It is a training residual and is flat across a factor of seven in focal length."
    - "Believing more views, or a wider depth range, fixes a fronto-parallel capture. The count argument says you stay one equation short."
    - "Treating hand-eye observability as binary. It degrades continuously, and a near-degenerate set returns a confident wrong answer."
    - "Diagnosing a constant miss without mapping it across the workspace. The spatial pattern is what separates translation, focal, distortion and timing."

## Reading the symptoms

<Steps>
<Step title="Constant miss everywhere → hand-eye translation">
A rigid offset. Cheapest to fix and the easiest to confirm.
</Step>

<Step title="Miss grows with range → focal length or hand-eye rotation">
Both scale linearly with distance. Separate them by checking the calibration's
capture geometry first.
</Step>

<Step title="Miss grows toward image edges → distortion">
A function of image position, not depth. Check the distortion model order and
whether the capture covered the periphery.
</Step>

<Step title="Miss only while moving → time offset, not geometry">
Latency between streams looks like a velocity-dependent pose error. No amount of
spatial recalibration fixes it.
</Step>

<Step title="Everything looks fine and the numbers are wrong → check conditioning">
SVD of the Jacobian at the solution. A near-zero singular value names the
parameter combination the data never determined.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The move that makes this answer strong is treating reprojection error as a
training residual and saying why that matters — it is the same structure as a
policy's validation loss being flat across the range from working to useless.
Backing it with the measured 0.184 px at 650% focal error, and the detail that
two initialisations converged to the same wrong number, turns a general caution
about metrics into a specific claim about this one. The counting argument for why
more views cannot help is the part most people have not thought through.
</Warning>
